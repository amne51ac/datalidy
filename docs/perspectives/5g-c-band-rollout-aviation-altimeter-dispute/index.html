The 5G C-Band Rollout, Aviation Safety and a Coordination Breakdown

As AT&T and Verizon prepared to switch on new 5G service in January 2022, airlines warned of serious disruption over possible interference with radio altimeters. A late compromise near airports averted the worst, but international flights were still canceled.

Event: January 2022 · Organizations: AT&T, Verizon, FAA, FCC, Emirates, Japan Airlines, All Nippon Airways, Boeing
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Shared risks between sectors need shared timelines, data and decision rights well before launch dates.
  • Late escalation turns technical disagreements into public standoffs that damage trust on all sides.
  • Safety concerns grounded in specific equipment and conditions are easier to resolve than broad warnings.
  • Customers absorb the cost of coordination failures, through canceled flights and delayed service.

What happened

Verizon and AT&T planned to activate new 5G service using C-band spectrum on January 19, 2022, NPR reported. The carriers had bought much of that spectrum in a 2020 FCC auction for about $80 billion, and the band sits next to frequencies used by aircraft radio altimeters, which measure height above the ground and feed other systems used in low-visibility landings. The carriers had already delayed the rollout twice to negotiate with the aviation industry and the FAA.

Leaders of 10 passenger and cargo airlines asked federal officials to create 5G-free buffer zones around runways, warning in a letter that a single recent Sunday could have seen more than 1,100 flight cancellations, diversions or delays affecting about 100,000 passengers, according to NPR. On January 18, the carriers agreed to temporarily postpone turning on some towers near airports. AT&T said its service would not interfere with aviation electronics and criticized the FAA for not using the two years it had to plan, NPR reported.

International carriers still changed schedules. NPR reported that Emirates suspended service to several U.S. cities and that two Japanese airlines adjusted flights, citing concerns about Boeing 777 altimeters. Light Reading reported that Japan Airlines resumed service on January 20 after the FAA confirmed that 777 operations were no longer an issue, and that Boeing said it was working on a long-term, data-driven solution.

The human dimension

At its core, this was a coordination problem among capable organizations with different mandates. Wireless carriers had paid for spectrum and promised customers faster service. Aviation regulators and airlines carried responsibility for safety margins on equipment designed decades earlier. Each side could point to evidence and to deadlines it had not set, and each had reason to fear being blamed if something went wrong.

Under those conditions, people tend to anchor on their own risk and escalate publicly when private channels stall. Light Reading reported sharp public criticism from several participants and analysts, with blame directed at the FAA, the administration, the carriers and the aviation industry. Such public exchanges can force action, but they also harden positions and leave customers uncertain about whether travel is safe.

The signals that mattered

The signals of a pending collision were visible well before January. The auction results, two earlier delays, letters from industry groups and FAA notices restricting certain landings all pointed to an unresolved issue with a fixed launch date.

Behavioral signals were present too. When organizations move from technical working groups to public letters and media statements, it usually means trust in the private process has broken down. Repeated short extensions can also signal that each side expects the other to concede rather than that a solution is near. Leaders who track these patterns can recognize when a dispute needs a new forum, senior sponsorship or independent technical review.

  • Repeated deadline extensions without an agreed technical resolution path
  • Escalation from working-level channels to open letters and media statements
  • Uncertainty about which aircraft and altimeter models were affected
  • Customer-facing partners, such as foreign airlines, receiving guidance late
  • Divergent public statements from regulators sharing responsibility for the same risk

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders, the lesson is that cross-sector risks need a shared operating model: common data, joint testing, clear decision rights and a timeline that both sides own. For investors in infrastructure, telecom and aviation, regulatory coordination is a material execution risk. Light Reading reported that Verizon lowered its expected C-band coverage partly because towers near airports would remain off, a reminder that coordination delays can reach revenue plans.

  • Map dependencies on other sectors' equipment and regulators early in major launches
  • Agree on test data and success criteria before positions become public
  • Plan customer communication for partners who will feel the effects first
  • Track escalation tone as an early warning of a breakdown in trust

The public-good dimension

Air travel safety is a public good built on conservative margins and shared trust. So is reliable, modern communications infrastructure. The C-band dispute showed that both can be served when technical facts are surfaced early and examined jointly, and that delays in doing so place the burden on travelers and crews. Clear, evidence-based public communication from regulators helps people understand that caution and progress are not opposed.
