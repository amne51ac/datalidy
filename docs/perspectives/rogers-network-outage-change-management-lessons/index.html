The Rogers Outage: How One Configuration Change Took Down a Network

On July 8, 2022, a core network update at Rogers Communications triggered a nationwide outage that lasted about a day. A regulator-commissioned review later traced it to a configuration error and to recovery tools that depended on the network that had failed.

Event: July 2022 · Organizations: Rogers Communications, Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A risk rating that drifted from high to low after earlier phases succeeded removed the extra scrutiny that might have caught the error.
  • Recovery was slowed because engineers relied on the same network for communication and diagnostics that had just failed.
  • Routine success can quietly change how teams perceive risk, which makes risk perception itself something to measure.
  • Boards overseeing critical infrastructure should ask how change risk is scored and who can override the score.

What happened

Early on Friday, July 8, 2022, Rogers Communications suffered an outage that cut wireless and wireline services for millions of customers across Canada. In a July 12 letter, the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission noted that Rogers had publicly attributed the outage to a maintenance upgrade in its core network and required the company to answer detailed questions. Futuriom reported that Rogers' July 22 filing described a planned core network upgrade during which a router filter was deleted, triggering a flood of routing messages.

According to MobileSyrup's account of an independent review commissioned by the CRTC and published in July 2024, the change occurred during the sixth of seven planned upgrade phases. Staff removed a policy filter on distribution routers while cleaning up configuration files, which flooded the core network with routing information and caused it to crash. The review said change management audits "failed to flag the erroneous configuration change."

The review also found that the outage was not caused by a design flaw in the core architecture. Futuriom reported that Rogers failed to deliver several emergency alerts in Saskatchewan during the outage, and that the company's chief executive pledged $10 billion over three years for reliability work. The review recommended, among other steps, separating the wireless and wireline cores, which it said had made the outage so extensive because one shared network carried both.

The human dimension

One of the most instructive findings concerns risk perception. MobileSyrup reported that Rogers initially rated the upgrade as high risk, but its assessment algorithm downgraded it to low after the first five phases went smoothly. A low rating meant no lab testing or higher-level approval. This is a familiar pattern in high-reliability settings: repeated success makes a risky task feel routine, and the safeguards built for risky tasks quietly fall away. The algorithm formalized a very human tendency.

Recovery revealed a second human factor. According to the review, the management network depended on the same core network, critical sites lacked backup connectivity, and staff relied on Rogers' own mobile and internet services to communicate. Engineers could not access error logs from the failed routers and needed about 14 hours to find the root cause. People were trying to solve a problem with tools the problem had disabled.

The signals that mattered

The review's findings point to signals that sit at the boundary of technology and behavior. None requires hindsight; each can be monitored as a leading indicator of resilience.

The common thread is that the safeguards depended on assumptions about normal conditions: that a well-performing upgrade stays low risk, and that the people restoring service will have working tools. Testing those assumptions before a crisis is far less expensive than discovering them during one.

  • Risk scores that fall automatically as phases succeed, without human review
  • Change audits that check parameters but not the intent of each change
  • Single dependencies shared by production, management and staff communication
  • Recovery drills that assume engineers can reach logs and each other
  • Absence of independent backup connectivity at operations centers

Lessons for leaders and investors

The review's recommendations, including separate management networks, third-party backup connectivity, overload safeguards, a new risk algorithm and better collaboration between operations and engineering, are technical, but their core is organizational. Leaders should treat the way teams perceive and score risk as a system to design and test, not a fixed property of the work.

Investors in infrastructure and technology businesses can ask management to walk through their most recent major incident: how long it took to find the cause, what slowed the team down and what changed afterward. The answers reveal more about operational culture than any uptime statistic.

  • Require human sign-off whenever an automated process lowers the risk rating of a critical change.
  • Run failure drills that remove the tools teams normally rely on to communicate.
  • Ask in diligence how incident response depends on the systems it is meant to restore.
  • Measure near misses and risk-rating overrides as leading indicators of reliability culture.

The public-good dimension

Telecommunications networks carry emergency calls, alerts, payments and the daily coordination of public life, so their reliability is a public good as much as a commercial asset. The CRTC's follow-up, including its later letter stating that Rogers' measures had addressed the cause, reflects a reasonable expectation that critical infrastructure operators learn openly from failure. At Datalidy, we see the outage as a clear example that resilience depends on how people perceive routine work, and that those perceptions can be measured and improved.
