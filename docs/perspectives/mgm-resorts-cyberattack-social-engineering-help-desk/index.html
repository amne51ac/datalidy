MGM Resorts Cyberattack Shows the Human Side of Security

In September 2023, a cyberattack disrupted slot machines, reservations and other systems at MGM Resorts. The company later estimated a roughly $100 million hit. Reports said attackers gained access by impersonating an employee to the IT help desk.

Event: September 2023 · Organizations: MGM Resorts International, CISA, FBI
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Help desks are designed to help people quickly, which makes them a target when verification steps depend on human judgment.
  • Federal agencies urged phishing-resistant authentication because social engineering exploits normal helpful behavior.
  • Security metrics should include how often verification procedures are followed under time pressure, not just technical controls.
  • Investors should treat identity verification and help desk procedures as material cyber risk factors in diligence.

What happened

In September 2023, MGM Resorts International said it had identified a cybersecurity issue affecting certain systems and was shutting some down as part of its response, according to AGBrief. Slot machine operations, hotel reservation systems, parking and parts of the company's websites were affected. AGBrief reported that a ransomware group claimed responsibility and said MGM "was defeated by a 10-minute conversation," referring to a call to the help desk in which the caller used an employee's name found on LinkedIn. MGM did not publicly confirm how the attackers gained access.

In an October 5 filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, MGM estimated the attack would cost about $100 million, with less than $10 million in one-time expenses such as legal and consultancy fees, The Register reported. Customer data was stolen, including names, contact details, dates of birth and, for some customers, Social Security and passport numbers. MGM said it had no evidence of bank card data or passwords being compromised and expected its cyber insurance to cover the financial impact. September occupancy was 88 percent, down from 93 percent a year earlier, according to The Register.

On November 16, 2023, the FBI and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency issued an advisory on the group known as Scattered Spider, which The Register reported had claimed responsibility for the MGM attack. The advisory said the group's actors pose as employees or IT staff and call help desks to request password resets and transfers of multifactor authentication tokens. The agencies recommended phishing-resistant multifactor authentication and continued employee training against voice phishing.

The human dimension

Help desks exist to restore access quickly so people can get back to work. That is a reasonable design goal, and agents are typically measured on speed and resolution. Social engineering exploits exactly that orientation. A caller who sounds plausible, references real details and conveys urgency is asking the agent to do what the job normally rewards. Verification steps that rely on information an attacker can find online offer little protection.

This is not a story of individual carelessness. It is a story of how incentives, procedures and ordinary helpfulness interact. When an organization's metrics reward fast resolution and its verification depends on easily researched facts, the system makes certain errors more likely. Changing outcomes requires redesigning the process so that the secure path is also the easy path, rather than relying on individuals to resist pressure that the role itself creates.

The signals that mattered

Organizations can measure the behaviors that social engineering targets. The relevant signals sit in help desk operations and identity processes, not only in network monitoring.

  • Share of password and authentication resets completed with strong identity verification
  • Help desk resolution-time targets that may conflict with verification steps
  • Results of realistic voice phishing tests across support teams
  • Proportion of privileged accounts protected by phishing-resistant authentication

Lessons for leaders and investors

Security investments often focus on technology while the most consequential decisions happen in routine human interactions. Leaders who align help desk incentives with verification, and who test procedures under realistic pressure, reduce the chance that a single conversation becomes an enterprise incident.

For investors, the MGM case shows that cyber risk is partly an operations and culture question. The company's own filing estimated an impact of about $100 million, and the disruption reached guest-facing systems. Diligence that asks how identity is verified, how support teams are measured and how procedures are tested under pressure offers a more complete picture of exposure than a review of technical tools alone.

  • Review help desk metrics to ensure speed targets do not discourage proper verification
  • Adopt phishing-resistant authentication for privileged and high-risk accounts, as CISA recommends
  • Run realistic social engineering exercises and treat results as process feedback, not blame
  • In diligence, examine identity verification procedures alongside technical security controls

The public-good dimension

Hospitality companies hold sensitive personal information for millions of guests, and breaches create lasting risks for people whose identity documents are exposed. Protecting that information depends on everyday procedures as much as on technology. Security programs that respect employees, design for real-world pressures and measure behavior honestly protect both customers and staff. Datalidy views this kind of behavior-centered security design as a public-good responsibility for any organization trusted with personal data.
