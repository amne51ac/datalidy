Better.com's Zoom Layoffs and the Cost of How Cuts Are Communicated

In December 2021, Better.com told more than 900 employees on one Zoom webinar that their jobs had ended. The decision was the company's to make; the delivery became the story, and a test of leadership behavior under pressure.

Event: December 2021 · Organizations: Better.com, SoftBank
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • How a layoff is communicated is a leadership behavior that remaining employees, customers and investors read closely.
  • Public blame aimed at departing staff can turn a cost decision into a lasting culture and reputation problem.
  • Executive departures and a delayed public listing followed, showing how conduct risk can reach the capital markets.
  • Boards can monitor leadership conduct with the same discipline they apply to financial controls and liquidity.

What happened

In early December 2021, the chief executive of Better.com, a SoftBank-backed online mortgage lender, told more than 900 employees on a Zoom webinar that they were being laid off, CNN reported. "If you're on this call, you are part of the unlucky group that is being laid off," he said, adding that their employment was "terminated effective immediately." According to CNN, the cut amounted to about 9% of the workforce, and the reasons given were market efficiency, performance and productivity.

The timing drew attention. CNN reported that the company had announced plans in May to go public through a special purpose acquisition company and had received $750 million in cash from that deal the week before the call. CNN also cited Fortune's reporting that the chief executive accused former employees of "stealing" from colleagues and customers by being unproductive. In an apology email reported by Fox Business, he wrote that he owned the decision to do the layoffs, "but in communicating it I blundered the execution."

On December 10, Fox Business reported that the chief executive was taking time off effective immediately, that the chief financial officer would run day-to-day operations and report to the board, and that the board had engaged an independent firm to conduct a leadership and cultural assessment. Several senior communications and marketing leaders reportedly resigned, and HousingWire reported that the planned public listing was likely to be pushed into the following year.

The human dimension

Layoffs are sometimes necessary, and mortgage lenders in late 2021 faced a shifting rate environment. The behavioral question is not whether to reduce costs but how people experience the process. A mass video call removes the private conversation, the chance to ask questions and the sense of being treated as an individual. For those who remain, it signals how they might be treated next, which shapes trust, discretionary effort and the decision to stay.

The follow-up comments mattered as much as the call. When leaders attribute a cut to the character of departing employees, survivors often hear a threat rather than an explanation. Research on organizational justice consistently finds that people judge fairness by the process and the respect shown, not only by the outcome. The apology acknowledged execution, but the episode illustrates how quickly a communication choice can become a referendum on leadership.

The signals that mattered

Some conditions were visible before the call. The company was moving through a high-pressure transition to the public markets, in a mortgage market that was changing quickly. Under that kind of pressure, existing leadership habits tend to be amplified rather than moderated, which is why the signals that matter are usually found in everyday conduct, not in the crisis itself.

Inside most companies, the signals of conduct risk live in different places. Human resources holds exit interview notes, communications teams track internal reactions, and boards see headline attrition numbers. Rarely are these combined into a single view that shows whether trust in senior leadership is rising or falling. When they are, leaders and directors can see strain building and intervene with coaching, governance changes or support before a decision becomes a public event.

  • Internal complaints about how senior leaders communicate with staff
  • Concentration of high-stakes decisions in one executive without visible counterweights
  • Rapid growth followed by sudden shifts in market conditions and hiring plans
  • Pending capital events that raise the pressure to show efficiency quickly
  • Exit interview and engagement survey themes about trust in leadership

Lessons for leaders and investors

For boards and investors, the lesson is that leadership conduct is a measurable risk, not a matter of style. It can be observed through engagement data, attrition among high performers, internal escalations and the quality of communication during stress. A firm such as Datalidy would frame this as a behavioral signal problem: the indicators that predict conduct risk usually exist well before a crisis, scattered across people data that no one has integrated.

  • Treat restructuring communication as a designed process, with rehearsal, sequencing and individual follow-up
  • Include leadership behavior and culture in diligence ahead of capital events
  • Give boards regular, structured views of trust, attrition and escalation data
  • Pair executive coaching with clear accountability when conduct concerns surface

The public-good dimension

Job loss affects health, family stability and financial security, and the holiday timing heightened that strain for many employees. Respectful process does not soften every consequence, but it preserves dignity and makes it more likely that departing workers receive clear information on severance and benefits. When high-profile companies model careless delivery, it can normalize poor practice across an industry; when they model care, it raises the floor for everyone.
