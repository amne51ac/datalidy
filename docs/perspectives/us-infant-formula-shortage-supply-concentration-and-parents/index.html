The U.S. Infant Formula Shortage and the Risks of Concentrated Supply

By May 2022, a recall and the shutdown of Abbott's Sturgis, Michigan plant had turned a strained infant formula market into a national shortage. Parents searched store to store as regulators and the company worked toward a reopening plan.

Event: May 2022 · Organizations: Abbott, FDA, Datasembly, WIC, CVS, Target
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Concentrated supply turns a single facility problem into a population-level public health risk.
  • Shelf availability data showed the shortage building for months before it became a national story.
  • Parents under stress may adopt unsafe workarounds, so clear health communication is part of the response.
  • Quality culture and inspection timing at critical facilities are material risks for investors and regulators.

What happened

In February 2022, Abbott halted production at its Sturgis, Michigan plant and recalled certain powdered formulas after the FDA warned consumers, according to reporting compiled by FOX 13 from FOX Business and the AP. Four infant infections had been reported, including two deaths. The FDA had not concluded whether bacteria from the plant caused them, and Abbott said genetic sequencing did not match strains found in the plant. FOX 13 reported that lawmakers questioned the FDA about why it took months to inspect the plant after learning of potential problems as early as September 2021.

Retail data firm Datasembly reported that the nationwide out-of-stock rate for infant formula reached 43% in the week ending May 8. FOX 13 reported that the rate rose to 70% in the week ending May 22. Fortune reported that CVS and Target had placed limits on formula purchases, and that Abbott was the exclusive supplier for more than half of the agencies in the WIC program, which provides formula for more than 1.2 million infants, according to the National WIC Association.

On May 16, Abbott announced a consent decree with the FDA setting out steps to reopen the plant. Abbott said it could restart within about two weeks of FDA confirmation and that product would reach shelves roughly six to eight weeks after that. The company's chief executive said Abbott was "deeply sorry that our voluntary recall worsened the nationwide formula shortage."

The human dimension

Parents of infants cannot postpone feeding, and many babies tolerate only specific formulas. When shelves empty, families search multiple stores, rely on informal networks and buy more than they need when they find supply. Fortune reported that experts warned desperate parents might dilute formula or make it at home, practices that can harm infants. These are understandable responses to scarcity, and they amplify the shortage for others.

Inside organizations, the shortage reflected decisions made across many years and actors: market concentration, contract structures, inspection timing and quality practices at a single critical plant. FOX 13 reported that the FDA inspection was delayed after the company cited COVID-19 cases among employees. No single choice created the crisis; the system lacked slack where it mattered most.

The signals that mattered

The shortage was visible in data long before May. Fortune reported Datasembly figures showing out-of-stock rates between 2% and 8% for much of 2021, rising from late 2021 to 40% by late April 2022.

Those figures show that a leading indicator existed, but the signal was not connected to decisions until the shortage became severe. Retail availability data, inspection findings, complaint reports and market share information sat with different organizations. A system designed to combine them could have shown sooner that a single plant problem was likely to become a national supply crisis.

  • Rising out-of-stock rates for essential products across regions
  • Share of national supply dependent on a single plant or supplier
  • Delays between early warnings and inspection or corrective action
  • Retailer purchase limits signaling stress before shortages peak
  • Parent search behavior and questions to pediatric providers

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders and regulators, the lesson is that critical supply chains need redundancy and early warning systems that connect quality data, inventory data and public health signals. For investors, quality culture at critical facilities is a material risk, not only a compliance matter. Datalidy would frame the challenge as integrating signals that sat in separate systems and owners until families felt the consequences.

  • Map single points of failure in products where substitution is difficult
  • Treat inspection and quality escalation delays as leading risk indicators
  • Prepare evidence-based guidance for consumers before shortages peak
  • Include supply resilience and quality culture in diligence for essential goods

The public-good dimension

Infant nutrition is a basic public health need, and the shortage weighed most heavily on families with fewer resources and less flexibility to search or travel. Clear, empathetic health communication helped steer parents away from unsafe substitutes. Abbott said it would cover rebates for competing products in states where it held the WIC contract while its products were unavailable. Building resilience into essential supply chains protects the youngest and most vulnerable.
