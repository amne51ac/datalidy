Starbucks Brings Back Its Former CEO Amid a Growing Union Drive

In March 2022, Starbucks said its chief executive would retire and that its longtime former leader would return on an interim basis. The change came as workers at a growing number of U.S. stores petitioned to unionize.

Event: March 2022 · Organizations: Starbucks, Starbucks Workers United, National Labor Relations Board
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Organizing activity is a lagging signal of unmet needs that often appear earlier in employee feedback and turnover.
  • Leadership transitions during labor tension are read by employees as statements about priorities.
  • Frontline experience, including staffing, scheduling and safety, shapes the customer experience directly and visibly.
  • Investors increasingly treat labor relations and employee voice as part of governance and long-term value.

What happened

On March 16, 2022, Starbucks announced that its chief executive would retire after five years in the role and that its former longtime leader would return as interim chief executive, NBC News reported. It was his third period leading the company, and his interim salary was $1, according to NBC News. Starbucks said it expected to name a permanent chief executive by the fall.

The announcement came amid a growing union campaign. NBC News reported that workers at about 140 stores in 26 states had petitioned the National Labor Relations Board to unionize, according to Starbucks Workers United. The same report noted that a group of 75 investors had urged the company's chair and chief executive to adopt a neutral stance toward organizing, and that the NLRB had filed a complaint alleging retaliation against two Phoenix employees seeking to unionize.

Al Jazeera reported that shares rose about 6% at the opening bell following the news. NBC News noted that the outgoing chief executive had led a digital modernization, including a revamped loyalty program, and had expanded the company in China, its second-largest market. Coverage did not establish a direct causal link between the leadership change and the union campaign beyond their timing.

The human dimension

Store employees experience a company through daily details: staffing levels during peak hours, scheduling predictability, the pace of mobile orders and how managers respond to concerns. When those details accumulate into frustration, and employees feel their voice is not heard through existing channels, some seek collective representation. That choice is a behavioral signal about trust and agency, not only about pay.

Leadership changes during such periods carry symbolic weight. Employees, investors and customers interpret who is chosen, what they emphasize and how they engage with organizers. A returning founder-era leader can bring credibility and continuity, but also expectations shaped by the past. The behavioral challenge is to listen to what the organizing signal is saying, not only to respond to the organizing itself.

The signals that mattered

The most useful signals for leadership usually precede formal petitions. Customer-facing businesses generate rich operational and people data that can show where frontline strain is building.

Organizing activity is best understood as a lagging indicator. By the time employees file petitions, frustrations have usually been building for months, often visible in turnover, absenteeism and feedback that did not lead to change. Measuring these signals well requires validated survey instruments, consistent store-level data and a willingness among leaders to look at results that may be uncomfortable, then respond in ways employees can see.

  • Store-level turnover and time-to-fill for open shifts
  • Gaps between scheduled and needed staffing during peak demand
  • Engagement survey scores on being heard and treated fairly
  • Volume of complaints and escalations from frontline employees
  • Changes in order complexity and speed expectations from digital channels

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders, the lesson is that employee voice is a performance input. Organizations that measure and act on frontline experience can address problems before they escalate into public disputes. For investors, the reported investor letter shows that labor relations are now a governance topic. Boards can ask whether management tracks frontline signals with the same rigor as sales per store.

There is a practical benefit as well. Frontline teams usually see operational problems first, from equipment failures to bottlenecks created by new ordering channels. Leaders who create reliable ways to hear those observations gain earlier warning of issues that affect customers and margins, whatever form employee representation takes.

  • Integrate people data and operational data at the store or team level
  • Use validated surveys to measure voice, fairness and workload, and act on results
  • Treat labor law compliance and neutrality commitments as governance matters
  • Align leadership transitions with a clear, credible plan for frontline experience

The public-good dimension

Retail and food service jobs are a major source of employment and a common entry point into the workforce. The quality of those jobs affects health, financial stability and family life for millions of people. Employee voice, whether through direct channels or collective representation, is part of a healthy labor market. Companies that treat frontline feedback as valuable information, rather than as a threat, tend to build more resilient workplaces and better customer experiences. Listening well is also a form of respect that people notice and remember.
