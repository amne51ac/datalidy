Ascension Ransomware Attack Forces Hospitals Onto Paper for Weeks

When a ransomware attack hit Ascension in May 2024, clinicians across its hospitals returned to paper, phones and faxes. Their accounts reveal how a cyber incident becomes a patient safety and workforce problem.

Event: May 2024 · Organizations: Ascension
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A May 8 ransomware attack cut Ascension clinicians off from electronic records and ordering systems, NPR and KFF Health News reported.
  • Clinicians described near-misses and workarounds such as handwritten notes, faxes and spreadsheets during the outage.
  • Downtime procedures are behavioral systems that depend on practice, staffing and clear escalation.
  • Hospitals can track downtime readiness and staff strain as leading indicators of patient safety risk.

What happened

On May 8, 2024, a ransomware attack struck Ascension, a Catholic health system that operates about 140 hospitals, according to reporting by KFF Health News and Michigan Public published by NPR. The attack locked providers out of electronic health records, some phones and the systems used to order tests, procedures and medications. Staff turned to handwritten notes, faxes, sticky notes and spreadsheets.

Ascension said on May 9 that its care teams were "trained for these kinds of disruptions." In a June 3 email to reporters, a spokesperson said, "We are confident that our care providers in our hospitals and facilities continue to provide quality medical care." Electronic health record access was restored in mid-June, NPR reported, though Ascension said records from May 8 onward might be temporarily inaccessible. Ascension declined to comment on specific clinician accounts.

Ascension's Wikipedia entry, citing contemporaneous reporting, notes that the company brought in Mandiant to assist and that in December 2024 Ascension began notifying approximately 5.6 million people whose information was compromised. NPR reported that the FBI found the health sector had the largest share of ransomware attacks among critical infrastructure sectors in 2023, and that the Department of Health and Human Services said large ransomware-related breaches had risen 264% over five years.

The human dimension

Clinicians who spoke to NPR and KFF Health News described a stressful shift in how care was delivered. Nurses reported near-misses involving medication doses that could not be scanned as usual, and an emergency physician described a patient receiving a narcotic meant for someone else. A nurse described a patient who died after lab results were delayed for hours. These accounts were not independently verified, and Ascension did not comment on them, but they illustrate how quickly safety checks built into digital systems disappear when those systems go dark.

Downtime places the burden of safety on human memory, handwriting and communication at the exact moment workload rises. Barcode scanning, order checks and alerts are designed to catch the errors that tired people make. NPR reported that at least 125 staff at one Michigan hospital signed a petition asking for fewer elective surgeries and nonemergency admissions, a sign that clinicians felt capacity had not been adjusted to match the new conditions. The gap between a policy that says staff are trained and the lived experience of working without tools is where risk accumulates.

The signals that mattered

Downtime readiness can be measured before an attack and monitored during one. The most useful indicators combine technical recovery with the behavior of the people delivering care.

  • Frequency and realism of downtime drills, including multi-week scenarios
  • Near-miss reports and medication variances during periods without electronic systems
  • Turnaround time for lab results and orders under paper processes
  • Staff requests to reduce elective volume or pause admissions
  • Nurse-to-patient workload and overtime during the outage period

Lessons for leaders and investors

For health system boards and investors, the lesson is that cyber resilience is a clinical quality issue. NPR reported that there were no federal requirements for hospitals to prevent or prepare for cyberattacks, which leaves preparation largely to each organization's leadership. Investments in security controls matter, and so do investments in the human systems that keep care safe when controls fail.

Communication is part of that system. Ascension's public statements emphasized training and confidence in care, while clinicians interviewed by NPR described strain and near-misses. Neither account has to be wrong for the gap between them to matter. Leaders who build fast, two-way channels with frontline staff during an incident gain better information for decisions such as diverting patients or reducing scheduled procedures.

  • Run extended downtime exercises that test staffing, communication and clinical safety checks.
  • Give clinical leaders authority to reduce elective volume quickly during a prolonged outage.
  • Create rapid channels for frontline staff to report safety concerns during an incident.
  • In diligence, review downtime plans and drill results alongside cybersecurity controls.

The public-good dimension

Patients cannot choose whether their hospital is targeted, and they bear the risk when care slows or safety checks lapse. Clinicians bear a heavy burden too, working longer and under greater uncertainty. Preparing people, not only systems, for extended downtime is a public health investment that protects both. Datalidy regards clinician wellbeing and patient safety as two sides of the same resilience plan.
