PowerSchool Breach Exposes Student Data Through a Support Portal Login

In January 2025, education software provider PowerSchool disclosed that an attacker used stolen credentials to reach a customer support portal and download student and teacher records. The case shows how one login can expose a sector.

Event: January 2025 · Organizations: PowerSchool
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • PowerSchool said an attacker used stolen credentials to access its PowerSource customer support portal, BleepingComputer reported.
  • The attacker claimed data on tens of millions of students and teachers from thousands of districts.
  • PowerSchool acknowledged paying a ransom, which offers no lasting guarantee that stolen data is gone.
  • Support tools with broad access to customer data deserve the same scrutiny as core production systems.

What happened

On January 7, 2025, PowerSchool, which provides student information software to K-12 schools, disclosed that a threat actor had used stolen credentials to access its PowerSource customer support portal, BleepingComputer reported. According to the company's disclosures as reported by BleepingComputer, the attacker used a customer support maintenance access tool to download student and teacher data from school districts' databases. The breach took place on December 19, 2024, using a subcontractor's stolen credentials, BleepingComputer later reported.

In an extortion demand, the attacker claimed to have taken data from 6,505 school districts in the United States, Canada and other countries, and BleepingComputer was told the breach affected more than 62 million students and more than 9.5 million teachers. PowerSchool said the data varied by district and that, for a subset of students, it included Social Security numbers, medical information and grades. The company said it expected more than three quarters of affected individuals did not have Social Security numbers taken.

PowerSchool acknowledged in a customer FAQ that it paid a ransom to prevent public release and received a video of the attacker claiming to delete the data. BleepingComputer reported the demand was $2.85 million in Bitcoin. In May 2025, individual districts began receiving extortion demands. A CrowdStrike investigation found the portal had also been accessed in August and September 2024 with the same credentials. In September 2025, the Texas Attorney General sued the company.

The human dimension

The entry point was not an exotic software flaw but a login. Credentials belonging to a subcontractor opened a support tool that could reach customer databases. Support systems are built for convenience: they let staff solve problems quickly on behalf of many customers. That design creates a concentration of access, and the behavior of every person who holds those credentials, including people outside the organization, becomes part of the security boundary.

The ransom decision reflects a difficult trade-off faced by leaders under pressure. Paying may reduce the immediate risk of public release, but it relies on trusting the attacker. The later extortion of individual districts, reported by BleepingComputer, shows why that trust is fragile. For school leaders, the incident added unexpected work: notifying families, answering questions and managing anxiety, often with limited information while the vendor's investigation continued.

The signals that mattered

According to the CrowdStrike findings reported by BleepingComputer, the same credentials had been used to access the portal months before the December breach. Earlier access is exactly the kind of signal that monitoring and access reviews are designed to catch.

  • Logins to support tools from unusual locations, times or contractor accounts
  • Volume of data exported through maintenance or support functions
  • Number of third parties holding credentials with broad customer data access
  • Time since last review of who can use support access tools
  • Repeat access by the same credentials outside normal support tickets

Lessons for leaders and investors

For investors in education technology and other software serving many customers, the lesson is that shared support infrastructure can turn one compromised account into a sector-wide event. Diligence should look beyond product security to how vendors, contractors and support staff access customer data, and how unusual access is detected.

School districts face a parallel lesson. Many rely on a small number of vendors for core records, so vendor risk management is a governance responsibility for district leaders and boards, not only for IT staff. Asking vendors how support access is granted, monitored and revoked is a practical step that does not require deep technical expertise.

  • Limit support tools to the minimum data access needed for each task.
  • Apply strong authentication and monitoring to contractor and subcontractor accounts.
  • Plan breach communications for customers who must in turn inform families.
  • In diligence, request evidence of access reviews and anomaly detection on support systems.

The public-good dimension

Schools collect student information because families trust them to protect it, and children have no say in which vendors hold their records. A breach of this scale affects that trust across communities, and the effects can last as long as the stolen data circulates. Education technology providers serve the public good when they treat student data with the care owed to the people it describes. Datalidy believes that trust is built through measurable safeguards, not assurances.
