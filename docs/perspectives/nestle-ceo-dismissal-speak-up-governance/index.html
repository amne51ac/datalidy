Nestlé's CEO Dismissal Puts Speak-Up Systems and Board Process in Focus

A year into his tenure, Nestlé's chief executive was dismissed after an investigation that began with a hotline complaint. The case shows why internal reporting channels and board follow-through matter as much as written codes of conduct.

Event: September 2025 · Organizations: Nestlé, Nespresso
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Nestlé said its chief executive breached its code of conduct through an undisclosed romantic relationship with a direct subordinate.
  • Reuters reported concerns first surfaced through an internal hotline, and a second investigation with an outside firm confirmed them.
  • Speak-up channels only work when people trust them and boards persist when a first inquiry is inconclusive.
  • Unplanned leadership changes raise strategic uncertainty, which makes succession readiness a material concern for investors.

What happened

On September 1, 2025, Nestlé dismissed its chief executive, Laurent Freixe, effective immediately. The company said an investigation found he had an undisclosed romantic relationship with a direct subordinate, which breached its code of conduct, according to the Associated Press. Chairman Paul Bulcke said, "This was a necessary decision," adding that "Nestlé's values and governance are strong foundations of our company." AP reported that Freixe had started as chief executive on September 1, 2024.

Reuters reported, citing a Nestlé spokesperson, that concerns first emerged in the spring through an internal company hotline. An initial board investigation was inconclusive. Because concerns persisted, a second probe overseen by the chair and the lead independent director, with help from an external firm, confirmed the relationship. Reuters reported that Freixe initially denied the relationship to the board and would not receive an exit package.

Nestlé named Philipp Navratil, who had led its Nespresso unit, as chief executive with immediate effect. AP reported he joined Nestlé in 2001 as an internal auditor. Reuters reported that Nestlé shares had fallen 17% over the prior year and quoted a JPMorgan note saying the change was "likely to keep afloat the question mark about the midterm direction of the company."

The human dimension

Reporting concerns about a senior leader is one of the hardest things an employee can do. It requires believing that the channel is confidential, that the concern will be taken seriously, and that speaking up will not lead to retaliation. According to Reuters, the process at Nestlé began with exactly that kind of report. The fact that it led to action, even after an inconclusive first inquiry, will shape how employees judge whether reporting is worth the risk.

Boards face their own pressures. Acting on allegations against a chief executive is disruptive, and an inconclusive investigation offers an easy reason to stop. Reuters reported that Nestlé's board instead pursued a second, externally supported review. Psychological safety research suggests that what leaders do after someone raises a concern teaches the whole organization more than any policy document does.

The signals that mattered

Governance health can be measured through behavior: how often people use reporting channels, how quickly concerns are resolved, and whether reporters trust the outcome. These indicators matter most at the top of an organization, where power differences make speaking up hardest.

Low reporting volume is often misread as a healthy culture. It can just as easily mean that people doubt the channel, fear consequences, or believe senior leaders are out of reach. Pairing usage data with survey measures of trust helps boards tell the difference.

  • Hotline usage rates and the share of reports involving senior leaders
  • Time from initial report to resolution, including follow-up investigations
  • Employee survey scores on confidence that concerns are acted on fairly
  • Board readiness for emergency succession, reviewed at least annually
  • Frequency of unplanned executive changes over a rolling period

Lessons for leaders and investors

Codes of conduct are only as strong as the behaviors that enforce them. For investors, Nestlé's handling shows a board willing to act on a difficult finding, while the market reaction reflected the cost of another leadership change. Both points belong in governance assessments.

For incoming leaders, the first months after an abrupt transition are a period of heightened attention. Employees watch for signals about whether values will be applied consistently and whether strategy will hold. Clear priorities, visible follow-through and structured support for the new chief executive can shorten that period of uncertainty.

  • Ask boards how speak-up reports involving executives are escalated and independently reviewed.
  • Treat emergency succession readiness as a standing agenda item, not a crisis task.
  • Assess how incoming leaders will rebuild strategic clarity for employees and investors.
  • Measure trust in reporting channels directly rather than assuming low volume means low risk.

The public-good dimension

Large employers set norms for millions of workers and suppliers. When a company acts on an internal report about its most senior leader, it signals that rules apply at every level, which supports fairness and safety across workplaces. Datalidy's perspective is that ethical culture is visible in behavior, especially in what happens after someone speaks up, and that measuring it is part of responsible leadership.
