FDA Approves Over-the-Counter Narcan Nasal Spray

On March 29, 2023, the FDA approved Narcan nasal spray for over-the-counter sale. Removing the prescription barrier was a milestone. Whether it saves lives depends on price, placement, confidence and the behaviors of bystanders.

Event: March 2023 · Organizations: U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Emergent BioSolutions
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • On March 29, 2023, the FDA approved 4 mg Narcan nasal spray as the first naloxone product for nonprescription use.
  • The FDA cited more than 101,750 reported fatal overdoses in the 12 months ending October 2022, BioSpace reported.
  • Removing a prescription addresses ability; motivation, prompts, price and stigma still shape whether people carry naloxone.
  • Success should be measured by readiness and equitable access in high-risk communities, not by units sold alone.

What happened

On March 29, 2023, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Narcan, a 4 milligram naloxone hydrochloride nasal spray, for sale without a prescription. According to BioSpace, it was the first naloxone product approved for nonprescription use. Narcan, made by Emergent BioSolutions, had first been approved as a prescription drug in 2015, and an FDA advisory committee voted unanimously in February 2023 to recommend the switch.

The FDA said the decision paved the way for the medication to be sold directly to consumers, including at pharmacies, convenience stores, grocery stores and gas stations, as well as online. FDA Commissioner Robert Califf said the approval "will help improve access to naloxone" and added, "We encourage the manufacturer to make accessibility to the product a priority." NPR, in a report republished by OPB, noted that other naloxone formulations and dosages still required a prescription, that the transition could take months, and that the manufacturer would set the price.

The FDA framed the decision against the scale of the overdose crisis. According to the agency, as reported by BioSpace, more than 101,750 fatal overdoses were reported in the 12 months ending October 2022, driven primarily by synthetic opioids such as illicit fentanyl. BioSpace reported that the manufacturer, not the agency, would set the timing and price of the over-the-counter product, a detail that would shape how quickly and how widely the change reached the people most at risk.

The human dimension

Access to a lifesaving product is not the same as use. For naloxone to save a life, someone nearby must have it, recognize an overdose, feel confident enough to act, and act quickly. Each step is a behavior, and each can be blocked by friction: a prescription requirement, a conversation at a pharmacy counter, a price, uncertainty about how to use the device, or worry about what carrying it implies to others.

Removing the prescription requirement addresses one of the most significant of those frictions, ability, in the language of the Fogg Behavior Model. Motivation and prompts matter too. People who do not see themselves as likely to witness an overdose, or who associate naloxone with stigma, may not buy it even when it sits on an open shelf. Placement, packaging, instructions and public messaging all shape whether availability turns into readiness.

The signals that mattered

The success of over-the-counter naloxone will be measured in behavior along the path from shelf to rescue. The most useful indicators follow that path step by step.

These measures matter because aggregate sales can rise while the communities with the highest overdose risk see little change. Tracking where, by whom and at what price naloxone is bought separates commercial success from public health success, and shows where outreach should go next.

  • Retail availability across store types and neighborhoods, not only total units shipped
  • Purchase patterns among first-time buyers compared with repeat buyers
  • Price relative to what people in high-risk communities can afford
  • Survey-measured confidence in recognizing an overdose and using the spray
  • Carriage rates among people most likely to witness an overdose

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders in pharmaceuticals, retail and public health, the switch from prescription to over-the-counter status is a behavioral design problem as much as a regulatory milestone. Shelf placement, price, packaging and instructions can be tested with the people most likely to need them. For investors, the case shows how regulatory change can open new channels, and how commercial and public health outcomes depend on the same behavioral conversion points. Health departments and community organizations can treat the retail shelf as one channel among several, alongside free distribution, so that price never becomes the deciding factor in an emergency.

  • Test packaging and instructions with first-time users under realistic stress
  • Track access equity alongside sales volume as a core performance measure
  • Align pricing decisions with the stated public health goal of broad access

The public-good dimension

Naloxone is among the clearest examples of a product where commercial success and public good can align. Every barrier removed between a bystander and a dose is a chance to save a life. The approval was a step; the outcome depends on whether manufacturers, retailers, health departments and community organizations design for the realities of the moment an overdose happens, and measure success by readiness, not only by sales.
