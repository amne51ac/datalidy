The European Parliament Sets Its Position on the AI Act

On June 14, 2023, the European Parliament adopted its negotiating position on the EU's proposed AI Act. The vote previewed a risk-based regime in which organizations would need to show how people design, deploy and oversee AI systems.

Event: June 2023 · Organizations: European Parliament, European Commission, Council of the European Union, Mozilla Foundation
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The Parliament's position framed AI governance around risk to people, which turns compliance into a question of human behavior.
  • Bans on emotion recognition in workplaces and schools show regulators scrutinizing AI that infers human states.
  • Organizations that inventory AI use and assign accountable owners early will adapt faster as rules are finalized.
  • Investors should treat AI governance maturity as a diligence item, since penalties under the position scale with global revenue.

What happened

On June 14, 2023, the European Parliament adopted its negotiating position on the AI Act, the European Union's proposed framework for regulating artificial intelligence, according to the law firm Cooley. The vote set the Parliament's starting point for three-way negotiations, called trilogues, with the European Commission and the Council of the EU. Cooley reported that trilogue negotiations were scheduled to begin later in June, with a deal expected by the end of 2023.

The Parliament's position took a risk-based approach, scaling obligations to the level of risk an AI system poses, according to Gibson Dunn. Cooley reported that it expanded the list of prohibited uses to include real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces, predictive policing based on profiling, untargeted scraping of facial images, and emotion recognition in law enforcement, border management, workplaces and schools. Gibson Dunn noted that the position added AI used to influence elections and recommender systems of very large online platforms to the high-risk category.

For foundation models, providers would need to assess and mitigate risks and register models in an EU database, and generative AI systems would face transparency requirements such as disclosing AI-generated content, Cooley reported. Maximum fines for prohibited practices would rise to the higher of 40 million euros or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover, according to both firms. The Mozilla Foundation welcomed several provisions but criticized what it described as a weakening of the process for classifying systems as high-risk. The final law would be shaped in later negotiations.

The human dimension

Much of the Parliament's position is about people rather than code. Bans on emotion recognition in workplaces and schools address what happens when systems infer human feelings in settings where people have little power to opt out. Requirements for complaints and explanations, which Cooley described, assume that affected individuals will need to understand and contest decisions made with AI. High-risk obligations depend on the daily behavior of the people who build, buy and operate these systems.

Inside organizations, that shifts the governance question. It is no longer only whether a model performs well, but whether teams know where AI is being used, who is accountable for it, and how human oversight works in practice. Many organizations adopt AI tools informally, one team at a time. That pattern is understandable, since early experimentation often happens before policy catches up, but it makes it difficult to answer the questions a risk-based regime will ask.

The signals that mattered

The direction of travel was visible well before the vote, through the Commission's original proposal and years of public debate. Organizations that tracked their own AI adoption behavior were better positioned to respond than those that waited for final text.

  • Share of AI tools in use that appear in a maintained internal inventory
  • Number of AI-assisted decisions affecting people that have a named accountable owner
  • Frequency with which human reviewers override or question AI outputs
  • Time required to explain a specific AI-assisted decision to an affected person

Lessons for leaders and investors

Regulation built around risk to people rewards organizations that already measure how their teams use AI. Leaders do not need to predict every detail of a final law to act sensibly: they can map current use, clarify ownership, and test whether human oversight is real rather than nominal.

For investors and boards, the practical question is not whether a portfolio company uses AI but whether it can describe that use clearly. Companies that cannot list their AI systems, name owners or explain decisions to affected people will face higher costs as rules settle, whatever the final text says. Treating AI governance as an operating capability, built through habits of documentation and review, positions organizations to adopt new tools with confidence rather than hesitation.

  • Build an inventory of AI use that includes informal and team-level adoption
  • Assign accountable owners for AI systems that affect customers, patients, students or employees
  • Test oversight processes by checking whether reviewers can and do challenge model outputs
  • In diligence, assess AI governance maturity alongside data security and privacy practices

The public-good dimension

The Parliament's position reflects a public interest in AI that respects people's rights, particularly in workplaces, schools and public services where individuals have limited choice. Transparency about AI-generated content and the ability to contest automated decisions both support public trust. Responsible adoption is not only a compliance exercise. It is a way of ensuring that new tools help people do what they intend to do, and Datalidy views clear human accountability as the foundation for using AI well.
