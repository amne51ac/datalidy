AT&T's Nationwide Wireless Outage and the Discipline of Change Control

On February 22, 2024, AT&T's wireless network went down across the United States. The company cited an incorrect process during network expansion, and an FCC review later described a misconfigured network element and insufficient safeguards.

Event: February 2024 · Organizations: AT&T, Federal Communications Commission
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • AT&T attributed the outage to an incorrect process used during network expansion, not a cyberattack.
  • According to the FCC, the outage blocked more than 92 million calls, including more than 25,000 attempts to reach 911.
  • Peer review and approval steps only protect a network when they are actually followed and verified before work begins.
  • Change control compliance is a measurable behavior and a strong leading indicator for reliability.

What happened

In the early morning of February 22, 2024, AT&T wireless customers across the United States lost service. Fox Business reported that the outage began at about 3:30 a.m. Eastern according to Downdetector, and that service was fully restored around 6 p.m. Eastern. The next day, AT&T said that "based on our initial review, we believe the outage was caused by the application & execution of an incorrect process used as we were expanding our network, not a cyberattack."

In July 2024, the FCC released the findings of its investigation. Reuters, as published by Claims Journal, reported that the outage affected more than 125 million devices, blocked more than 92 million voice calls and prevented more than 25,000 attempts to reach 911 over more than 12 hours. The FCC attributed the outage to a network change with an equipment configuration error and referred the matter to its Enforcement Bureau.

Broadband Breakfast reported that the FCC cited insufficient precautions, including weak safeguards to ensure network changes were approved before implementation, and that AT&T had since added peer review steps so maintenance cannot proceed until required reviews are confirmed. AT&T had apologized to affected customers after restoring service and said it was taking steps to ensure they would not experience the same problem again, according to Fox Business.

The human dimension

Change control processes exist because skilled people make occasional errors, especially during routine work at night. Peer review is a behavioral safeguard: a second person looks before a change goes live. Its value depends entirely on whether it happens consistently, and on whether the system makes skipping it difficult. When approvals can be bypassed, the process becomes a norm rather than a control, and norms erode under time pressure and familiarity.

Routine work carries its own risk. Engineers who have made many similar changes without incident reasonably build confidence in their process. That confidence is usually earned, which is why organizations need structural checks that do not rely on anyone's judgment in the moment. AT&T's reported addition of enforced peer review reflects that design principle.

The signals that mattered

Reliability failures in large networks are often preceded by measurable drift in how changes are made. Indicators worth tracking include the following.

The common thread is that each indicator measures whether safeguards are actually used, rather than whether they exist. A rising share of changes that skip review, or a growing gap between test and production setups, is a signal that norms are drifting even when nothing has yet gone wrong.

  • Share of network changes deployed with documented, verified peer review
  • Number of changes executed outside approved windows or procedures
  • Gap between lab test configurations and production configurations
  • Near misses and rollbacks reported per hundred changes
  • Time to notify public safety customers after a service disruption begins

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders, the lesson is that a process on paper is not a control until it is enforced by design and measured in practice. For boards and investors, change control compliance is a leading indicator of operational risk that can be tracked quarterly, well before an outage becomes a regulatory matter. Rewarding teams for near-miss reporting makes those signals more visible.

The same discipline applies in any organization that changes critical systems, from hospitals updating clinical software to banks adjusting payment platforms. Reliability is built from many small, repeated behaviors done the same way every time.

  • Build approval requirements into tools so changes cannot proceed without them.
  • Measure process compliance as a KPI, not only outage minutes.
  • Treat near misses as data and make reporting them safe and expected.
  • Ask management how often critical procedures are bypassed, and how they know.

The public-good dimension

Mobile networks are public infrastructure in practice: people use them to call 911, reach family and run their work. When they fail, the most serious effects fall on those in emergencies. Reliable change discipline is therefore a public safety practice. Datalidy's view is that measuring the human behaviors inside technical processes is one of the most direct ways to protect that public trust.
