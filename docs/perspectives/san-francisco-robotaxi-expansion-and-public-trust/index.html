California Expands Robotaxi Service, Then Asks Cruise to Cut Its Fleet

On August 10, 2023, California regulators allowed Waymo and Cruise to charge fares for driverless rides across San Francisco at any hour. About a week later, after a Cruise vehicle hit a fire truck, the DMV asked Cruise to halve its fleet.

Event: August 2023 · Organizations: California Public Utilities Commission, California DMV, Cruise, Waymo, City of San Francisco
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Technology adoption in public spaces depends on the trust of people who never chose to use the product, including first responders.
  • Interactions with emergency vehicles and unplanned stops were raised before approval, showing that concerns were visible in advance.
  • Regulatory approval and public acceptance are different outcomes and need to be measured separately.
  • Investors in autonomous systems should track edge-case interaction data and community sentiment as core operating metrics.

What happened

On August 10, 2023, the California Public Utilities Commission voted 3 to 1 to allow Waymo and Cruise to charge fares for driverless rides in San Francisco with no time restrictions, according to the law firm Davis Wright Tremaine. Before the vote, the companies could offer driverless rides but faced limits on charging fares. The CPUC found that each company had met requirements set in a 2020 decision, including a passenger safety plan.

During the meeting, public commenters and commissioners raised questions about interactions with first responders, sudden and unplanned stops, compliance with traffic laws, and accessibility for passengers with disabilities, Davis Wright Tremaine reported. The City of San Francisco filed motions to stay both authorizations, arguing that the city "will suffer serious harm." Commissioners asked CPUC staff for a follow-up report in about three months.

On August 17, a Cruise vehicle carrying a passenger collided with a fire truck operating with lights and sirens at a San Francisco intersection, Fox Business reported. The passenger was taken to a hospital with injuries not considered life-threatening. The next day, the California DMV asked Cruise to reduce its active fleet by 50 percent "until the investigation is complete," and Cruise agreed to operate no more than 50 driverless vehicles during the day and 150 at night. Cruise said its vehicle detected the sirens and began braking but was "ultimately unable to avoid the collision," noting that buildings blocked sightlines at the intersection.

The human dimension

Most technology adoption decisions are made by users. Autonomous vehicles are different: they operate among pedestrians, cyclists, other drivers and emergency crews who never opted in. Their acceptance depends on how those people experience the vehicles, especially in rare, high-stakes moments such as an emergency response. Each incident, whatever its cause, shapes public perception far more than routine trips that go well.

Regulators, companies and city officials each faced real constraints. The CPUC applied the criteria it had established. The companies were building a new service in a dense, complex city. City officials and first responders were concerned with the safety of their own operations. The DMV's response showed regulators using a proportional lever, reducing scale while an investigation proceeded, rather than an all-or-nothing decision. The challenge was not a lack of concern on any side but a lack of shared measures that all parties trusted.

The signals that mattered

The concerns raised at the CPUC hearing were early signals. Questions about first responder interactions and unplanned stops described exactly the kinds of edge cases that would later attract attention.

  • Frequency of interactions with emergency vehicles, and outcomes, reported in comparable terms
  • Number of unplanned stops that obstruct traffic or emergency access
  • Volume and themes of complaints from city agencies and first responders
  • Public sentiment trends in neighborhoods with the highest vehicle density

Lessons for leaders and investors

For technologies that operate in shared public space, the stakeholder map extends far beyond customers. Leaders who measure the experience of non-users, publish comparable safety data and test scaled rollouts in stages build the trust that approval alone cannot provide.

Investors in autonomous systems should recognize that regulatory permission can be narrowed quickly when public confidence shifts, as the DMV's request showed within days of the CPUC vote. Business plans that assume steady expansion after approval may underweight this risk. Companies that report comparable safety data, engage city agencies and first responders as partners, and scale in stages are more likely to keep the trust that sustains their license to operate.

  • Define and share edge-case metrics with regulators and city partners before expanding
  • Scale deployment in measured stages tied to trust and safety thresholds
  • Treat first responders as a key user group in design and testing
  • In diligence, evaluate community and regulatory relationships as operating risks, not just legal ones

The public-good dimension

Autonomous vehicles could eventually improve mobility for people who cannot drive, including some older adults and people with disabilities, which is why accessibility was raised at the hearing. Realizing that public benefit depends on earning trust from the communities where the vehicles operate. Transparent measurement of how vehicles behave in rare situations is a public-good investment, and Datalidy views it as essential to responsible technology adoption.
