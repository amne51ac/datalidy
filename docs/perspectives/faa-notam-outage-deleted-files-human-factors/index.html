How Deleted Files Grounded U.S. Flights: The FAA NOTAM Outage

On January 11, 2023, the FAA halted domestic departures after its NOTAM system failed. The agency later said contract personnel unintentionally deleted files. The real story is how system design turns ordinary slips into national disruptions.

Event: January 2023 · Organizations: Federal Aviation Administration, U.S. Department of Transportation
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The FAA paused all U.S. domestic departures from 7:30 to 9:00 a.m. Eastern on January 11, 2023, FedScoop reported.
  • The FAA said contract personnel unintentionally deleted files while synchronizing the live primary database with a backup.
  • Critical systems should be designed so that a single maintenance action cannot take down a national service.
  • The decision to pause departures reflected a safety-first culture; the upstream question is why that was the safest option.

What happened

Late on January 10, 2023, the Federal Aviation Administration's Notice to Air Missions system, which alerts pilots and airports to hazards such as runway construction, went down. According to FedScoop, after a reboot failed to fix the problem, the FAA paused all U.S. domestic departures from 7:30 to 9:00 a.m. Eastern on January 11. NPR reported that the system was fully restored by 9 a.m. and that airlines canceled more than 1,300 flights and delayed nearly 10,000 more.

On January 19, the FAA said a preliminary review found that contract personnel "unintentionally deleted files" while working to correct synchronization between the live primary database and a backup database. The agency said it had found no evidence of a cyberattack or malicious intent. NPR reported that the FAA said it had taken steps to make the system more resilient, although its statement did not specify those measures.

Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, as reported by NPR, defended the pause as a conservative but necessary step to ensure confidence in safety and said the government would own the problem and fix it. The disruption also drew sharp criticism from members of Congress, including a senator who called it completely unacceptable. FedScoop noted that on January 12 the FAA had said a preliminary investigation pointed to a damaged database file, an explanation the agency refined a week later when it described the file deletions.

The human dimension

It is tempting to reduce this event to one mistake by one team, but human factors research consistently points elsewhere. The work being done, reconciling a live database with its backup, is precisely the kind of routine maintenance task that becomes dangerous when live and backup environments are hard to tell apart, when actions cannot easily be reversed, and when safeguards depend on individual vigilance. The FAA's own description, unintentional deletion during synchronization, describes a system in which a single maintenance action could affect a safety-critical production service.

The decision to stop departures also deserves recognition. Leaders facing uncertain information about safety-critical data chose the conservative option and accepted significant disruption to protect safety margins. That choice reflects a mature safety culture. The harder question sits upstream: why the system's design allowed a maintenance action to reach the point where a national pause was the safest available response, and what would have made the error easier to catch or undo.

The signals that mattered

Infrastructure failures that begin with human action are usually preceded by weak signals in how maintenance work is done. These signals are rarely tracked as performance measures, which is why they stay invisible until something breaks. None of them requires new technology to collect. They require a decision to treat maintenance quality as a performance outcome that leaders review, rather than as background activity that becomes visible only after failure.

  • Frequency of synchronization mismatches between primary and backup systems
  • Share of maintenance tasks performed directly against production environments
  • Near-miss reports involving accidental changes that were caught in time
  • Time required to restore from backup in drills compared with real incidents
  • Depth of contractor onboarding and clarity of change approval procedures

Lessons for leaders and investors

For leaders responsible for critical systems, the lesson is to design for the predictable error rather than rely on the absence of error. Clear separation between live and backup environments, confirmation steps for irreversible actions, and rehearsed recovery are interface and process decisions, not only technical ones. For investors, government technology and aviation suppliers carry operational risk that rarely appears in revenue figures; diligence should examine how vendors manage change, not only what they build.

  • Ask whether a single action by one person can take down a critical service
  • Count near misses, and recognize the people who report them
  • Evaluate contractors on change management practices, not only delivery speed

The public-good dimension

Public infrastructure rests on shared trust. When an aviation safety system fails, travelers and workers need confidence that the response prioritizes safety and that the lessons will be learned without scapegoating individuals. Blameless review practices, long associated with aviation safety, make honest reporting possible, and honest reporting is what makes public systems safer over time. Designing interfaces and procedures that respect human limits is a public good in its own right.
