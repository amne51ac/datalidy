Binance Pleads Guilty and Agrees to Pay $4.3 Billion

On November 21, 2023, Binance pleaded guilty to anti-money laundering and sanctions violations, agreeing to pay about $4.3 billion. Its founder and chief executive pleaded guilty to a Bank Secrecy Act violation and stepped down.

Event: November 2023 · Organizations: Binance, U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. Department of the Treasury, Commodity Futures Trading Commission
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Compliance failures often reflect incentive structures that reward growth while treating controls as optional friction.
  • Prosecutors described a company that prioritized profits over required safeguards, a cultural finding as much as a legal one.
  • An independent monitor signals that regulators want sustained behavior change, not just a one-time payment.
  • Investors should evaluate compliance culture and leadership behavior as material risks, especially in fast-growing sectors.

What happened

On November 21, 2023, Binance Holdings and its founder and chief executive Changpeng Zhao pleaded guilty to anti-money laundering and U.S. sanctions violations under a settlement that allowed the exchange to keep operating, Bloomberg reported. Zhao pleaded guilty to violating the Bank Secrecy Act in federal court in Seattle, agreed to pay a $50 million fine and stepped down as CEO. The settlement involved the Justice Department, the Treasury Department and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, according to Bloomberg.

Binance agreed to pay about $4.3 billion, combining a fine and forfeiture, according to USA Today, as published by Yahoo Finance. The company also pleaded guilty to failing to register as a money transmitting business and to violating the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. The Justice Department alleged that Binance kept VIP U.S. customers after announcing in 2019 that it would block them, helping them register offshore accounts, USA Today reported. Binance will be subject to a monitor and reporting requirements.

Attorney General Merrick Garland said Binance "prioritized its profits over the safety of the American people," and added, "Using new technology to break the law does not make you a disrupter; it makes you a criminal," according to USA Today. Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen said Binance processed transactions linked to terrorist groups without filing suspicious activity reports. Richard Teng, previously Binance's global head of regional markets, was named chief executive.

The human dimension

Compliance failures at this scale rarely result from a single decision. They emerge from incentives, norms and leadership signals that tell people which goals matter most. When growth is celebrated and controls are framed as obstacles, employees learn to treat compliance as negotiable. The Justice Department's account of retaining customers the company had publicly said it would block describes a gap between stated policy and actual behavior, which is a defining feature of weak compliance cultures.

Fast-growing companies in new markets face genuine ambiguity about how existing rules apply, and the cryptocurrency sector developed quickly relative to regulatory frameworks. That context explains some uncertainty, but the plea agreement shows that core obligations, such as anti-money laundering programs and sanctions compliance, applied throughout. Culture determines whether people resolve ambiguity by asking what is required or by asking what they can get away with.

The signals that mattered

Compliance culture can be assessed before enforcement action. Investors, boards and partners can look for signals that show whether stated policies govern actual behavior.

  • Gaps between public compliance commitments and observed customer or transaction practices
  • Seniority, independence and resourcing of compliance leadership relative to growth teams
  • Volume and handling of suspicious activity reports compared with transaction growth
  • Employee willingness to raise compliance concerns without fear of consequences

Lessons for leaders and investors

The case is a reminder that compliance is a behavioral outcome shaped by leadership. Boards and investors should evaluate whether incentives reward adherence to controls, whether compliance leaders have real authority, and whether employees feel safe raising concerns.

For investors, the case shows that compliance risk can grow alongside commercial success when growth teams outpace controls. Penalties of this size, combined with leadership change and ongoing monitoring, can reshape a company's trajectory. Diligence that examines how compliance decisions are made, who can overrule them and whether stated policies match observed practice offers earlier insight than a review of written policies alone, which may look complete while behavior tells a different story.

  • Align incentive structures so growth targets do not reward bypassing controls
  • Give compliance leaders independence and direct access to the board
  • Measure speak-up culture as a leading indicator of misconduct risk
  • In diligence, test whether stated policies match observed operational behavior

The public-good dimension

Anti-money laundering and sanctions rules protect the public from fraud, terrorism financing and other harms. When major financial platforms fail to apply them, the costs fall on society, not only on the company. Building cultures where compliance is part of how work gets done supports both market integrity and public safety. Datalidy views compliance culture as a measurable set of behaviors that boards and investors can and should examine.
