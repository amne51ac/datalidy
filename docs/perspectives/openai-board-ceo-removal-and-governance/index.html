OpenAI's Board Removes Its CEO, Then Reinstates Him Days Later

On November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board said its chief executive had not been "consistently candid" and removed him. After days of turmoil, including a letter signed by most employees, he was reinstated and the board was reconstituted.

Event: November 2023 · Organizations: OpenAI, Microsoft
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Board decisions communicated without explanation can trigger stakeholder reactions that overwhelm the decision itself.
  • Employee collective action showed that workforce commitment is a decisive governance factor in talent-intensive firms.
  • Major partners learning of leadership changes at the last minute signals gaps in stakeholder communication.
  • Investors should assess whether governance structures can absorb conflict without threatening the organization's continuity.

What happened

On Friday, November 17, 2023, OpenAI announced that its board had removed chief executive Sam Altman after a "deliberative review process" concluded that he "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities," according to Computerworld. The board named chief technology officer Mira Murati interim CEO. Computerworld reported that co-founder Greg Brockman was removed as board chair and subsequently left the company.

Microsoft, which GovInfoSecurity reported had invested more than $13 billion in OpenAI, learned of the decision one minute before it was announced. Over the following days OpenAI had two interim chief executives, and a letter backing a board change was signed by 95 percent of the workforce, according to GovInfoSecurity. On November 22, the company announced Altman would return as CEO under a new initial board led by Bret Taylor and including Larry Summers, with Adam D'Angelo the only remaining member of the prior board. Microsoft's chief executive called the changes "an essential step on a path to more stable, well-informed and effective governance."

As part of the agreement, Altman agreed to an independent investigation into the events that led to his removal, Semafor reported on November 29. The board had not publicly provided specifics about its concerns. Semafor reported that a week after his return, the lack of detail had left some employees uneasy, and that some staff had explored jobs at other AI companies.

The human dimension

The board acted within its authority, but its communication left a vacuum. A brief statement citing a lack of candor, without further detail, invited employees, partners and the public to supply their own explanations. In moments of uncertainty, people look to one another for cues, and collective sentiment can form quickly. The employee letter showed how strongly the workforce identified with its leadership, and how that identification became a source of power in the governance dispute.

Board members operate under real constraints, including legal considerations, confidentiality and fiduciary duties to a specific mission. Those constraints can make full disclosure difficult. Yet governance depends on the trust of the people who must carry out its decisions. When a decision affects thousands of employees and a major partner, the way it is communicated becomes as consequential as the decision itself.

The signals that mattered

Governance strain rarely appears without warning. The board's own statement pointed to concerns about communication between directors and management, which is the kind of relationship that can be assessed before a crisis.

  • Quality and consistency of information flow between management and the board
  • Alignment between key partners and the board on governance expectations
  • Employee identification with individual leaders versus the institution and its mission
  • Board composition and capacity to manage conflict in a high-growth organization

Lessons for leaders and investors

Boards of fast-growing, mission-driven organizations face unusual pressure. The episode shows the value of assessing board and management relationships regularly, planning communication for high-stakes decisions, and understanding how employees and partners are likely to respond before acting.

For investors, the episode showed that governance structures can carry risk even when operating performance is strong. Unusual structures, such as nonprofit boards overseeing commercial entities, may have clear mission logic but uncertain dynamics under stress. Understanding who holds authority, how disagreements are surfaced and how key stakeholders would react to a leadership change is part of evaluating any organization where a small group of leaders and employees drives most of the value.

  • Conduct regular, structured assessments of board and management trust
  • Plan stakeholder communication before, not after, major leadership decisions
  • Map key partner dependencies and how governance changes would affect them
  • In diligence, examine governance structures for clarity of authority and conflict resolution

The public-good dimension

OpenAI's work affects how millions of people learn, work and access information, and its governance matters beyond its own investors and staff. Stable, transparent governance supports public confidence that powerful technology is being developed responsibly. When leadership disputes play out without explanation, public trust in that oversight can suffer. Datalidy sees clear, accountable leadership communication as an essential part of responsible AI development.
