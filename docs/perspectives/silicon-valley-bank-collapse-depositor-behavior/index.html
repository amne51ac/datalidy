Silicon Valley Bank's Collapse and the Speed of a Modern Bank Run

California regulators closed Silicon Valley Bank on March 10, 2023. The Federal Reserve's review later described mismanagement, lax supervision and a run that moved in hours. The behavioral lessons reach boards, LPs and portfolio companies.

Event: March 2023 · Organizations: Silicon Valley Bank, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, Federal Reserve
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A California filing said depositors initiated withdrawals totaling $42 billion before regulators closed SVB on March 10, 2023.
  • The Fed review cited 31 unaddressed supervisory warnings, about three times the peer average, Banking Exchange reported.
  • Concentrated, highly networked depositor communities can turn individual prudence into collective risk within hours.
  • The number and age of unresolved risk findings is itself a governance KPI boards should track.

What happened

On March 10, 2023, California regulators closed Silicon Valley Bank, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation became receiver. Reporting from The National Desk, carried by KUTV, described it as the largest bank closure since the 2008 financial crisis. The bank had told investors earlier that week that it needed about $2 billion to balance its books, which triggered panic among clients, and a California filing said investors and depositors initiated withdrawals totaling $42 billion. The FDIC said insured depositors would have full access to their money by the following Monday morning.

Two days later, regulators also seized Signature Bank, according to the Associated Press. On April 28, the Federal Reserve released a review led by Vice Chair for Supervision Michael Barr. AP reported that the review attributed the failure to "extremely poor bank management," weakened regulations and lax government supervision, and cited "critical deficiencies in the firm's governance, liquidity, and interest rate risk management."

Banking Exchange reported that the bank had 31 unaddressed safe and soundness supervisory warnings when it failed, about three times the average for peers, and that management failed its own internal liquidity stress tests and changed risk assumptions rather than addressing the underlying risks. According to AP, the review also found that social media helped drive a run that unfolded in hours rather than days, and that supervisors were reluctant to press management as problems grew.

The human dimension

SVB's depositor base was unusually concentrated in one community: technology startups and the investors who advise them. That concentration created both loyalty and fragility. In a tightly networked group, information and fear travel quickly, and each depositor's sensible decision to protect payroll becomes a reason for the next depositor to do the same. The Fed's observation that the run unfolded in hours describes behavior shaped by mobile banking and fast-moving online conversation as much as by balance sheets.

Inside the institutions, the review describes a different behavioral pattern: warnings that accumulated without resolution. Supervisors identified problems but did not push hard enough, and management measured risk in ways that made it look smaller. Both are familiar ways organizations normalize unresolved issues. A warning that has been open for months stops feeling urgent, even as the underlying exposure grows.

The signals that mattered

Many of the relevant signals were available before March. The question is whether anyone treated them as predictive rather than procedural.

  • Count and age of unresolved supervisory or audit findings
  • Concentration of deposits within a single, highly networked customer community
  • Share of deposits above insurance limits and how quickly they could move
  • Changes to risk model assumptions that reduce measured exposure
  • Speed of customer sentiment shifts in community channels during periods of stress

Lessons for leaders and investors

For boards, the review suggests that the number and age of open findings is itself a governance KPI. For venture investors and limited partners, SVB was also a lesson in portfolio behavior: when many portfolio companies share the same bank and the same advisers, their reactions are correlated, which turns individual prudence into collective risk. Venture and private equity firms can map these shared dependencies across a portfolio before stress reveals them.

Leadership development has a role as well. Executives rewarded for growth can find it hard to hear warnings that imply slowing down, and boards can help by making it safe, and expected, for management to surface unresolved risks early rather than manage them out of view.

  • Report the age of unresolved risk findings to the board, not only their count
  • Map shared counterparties and advisers across portfolio companies
  • Plan treasury diversification and crisis communication before a run begins
  • Question risk assumption changes that improve metrics without new evidence

The public-good dimension

Bank runs harm people far from the institution: employees who might miss payroll, nonprofits and community organizations with deposits, and a public that ultimately bears the cost of financial instability. Understanding depositor behavior, including how quickly fear spreads through connected communities, is part of building a financial system that stays trustworthy when money can move in minutes. Datalidy's view is that behavioral signals of this kind belong alongside financial ratios in any serious risk dashboard.
