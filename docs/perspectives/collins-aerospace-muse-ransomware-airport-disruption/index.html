Ransomware at a Check-In Vendor Disrupts Major European Airports

When a single vendor's check-in and baggage software was hit by ransomware, several of Europe's busiest airports reverted to manual processes. The incident shows how shared systems concentrate risk, and how people carry operations when technology fails.

Event: September 2025 · Organizations: Collins Aerospace, RTX, Heathrow Airport, Brussels Airport, Berlin Brandenburg Airport, ENISA, National Crime Agency
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • ENISA said disruption at Heathrow, Brussels and Berlin airports was caused by a third-party ransomware incident affecting Collins Aerospace's MUSE software.
  • RTX told the SEC that MUSE systems run on customer-specific networks and that it did not expect a material financial impact.
  • Staff fell back on manual check-in, which shows why practiced fallback procedures are as important as technical recovery.
  • Concentration on a shared vendor is a measurable risk that boards and investors in transport infrastructure should track.

What happened

Starting on Friday, September 19, 2025, a cyberattack disrupted check-in and baggage systems at several European airports, including London Heathrow, Berlin Brandenburg and Brussels, Bloomberg reported via Claims Journal. The affected platform was MUSE, run by Collins Aerospace, which supports airline check-in, boarding and baggage processing. On September 22, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, said the outage was "caused by a third-party ransomware incident."

With kiosks and bag-drop machines offline, Brussels Airport asked passengers to check in online in advance. By Monday, delays in London and Berlin had eased, while Brussels remained significantly disrupted, with 40 of 277 scheduled departures canceled that day, according to an airport spokesperson quoted by Bloomberg. Collins' parent company, RTX, said it was working to restore "full functionality to our customers as quickly as possible."

RTX said in a filing with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission that on September 19 it became aware of a ransomware incident on systems supporting MUSE, that those systems operate on customer-specific networks outside the RTX enterprise network, and that it did not expect a material financial impact. On September 24, the UK National Crime Agency said it had arrested a man in his forties in West Sussex on suspicion of Computer Misuse Act offences and released him on conditional bail. The agency said its investigation "is in its early stages and remains ongoing."

The human dimension

When automated systems fail, people become the system. Airline and airport staff shifted to manual check-in and baggage handling, work that is slower, more error-prone and more stressful, while managing frustrated travelers. How well that shift goes depends on whether staff have practiced the fallback, whether instructions are clear, and whether frontline workers have the authority to improvise sensibly.

Passengers also had to change behavior quickly, checking in online and arriving early. Clear, consistent communication across airports, airlines and the vendor shapes whether travelers comply or crowd terminals. Shared platforms spread operational risk across many organizations, which can blur who is responsible for communicating and when. There is also a fairness dimension. Disruptions of this kind fall unevenly, and travelers who are less comfortable with online check-in, or who need assistance, depend most on staff at the counter. Fallback plans that account for these travelers protect both service quality and the people most likely to be left behind.

The signals that mattered

Concentration on a single vendor is visible long before an incident. Organizations that depend on shared platforms can measure their exposure and their readiness to operate without them. Investigations into the attack itself were ongoing at the time of the reporting cited here, so lessons about prevention should be drawn cautiously.

  • Number of critical passenger processes that depend on one external platform
  • Date and outcome of the last full manual fallback drill
  • Time required to process a typical departure bank without automation
  • Clarity of incident communication roles across airport, airline and vendor

Lessons for leaders and investors

Infrastructure investors and boards increasingly own businesses whose operations rest on software they do not control. The Collins incident shows the value of measuring third-party dependence and rehearsing degraded operations as seriously as normal ones.

For vendors, the incident underlines that customers judge them by how they communicate during a crisis, not only by how quickly systems return. Regular, specific updates that tell airports and airlines what to expect help partners make decisions about staffing, cancellations and passenger messaging.

  • Map single points of failure in vendor relationships and report them to the board.
  • Fund regular fallback drills, and measure throughput and error rates during them.
  • Agree on joint communication plans with critical vendors before an incident occurs.
  • Include vendor resilience in diligence on airports, airlines and logistics assets.

The public-good dimension

Air travel connects families, workers and patients, and disruption falls hardest on travelers with tight connections, disabilities or limited means. Shared digital infrastructure brings efficiency, but its failures are shared too. Datalidy's perspective is that resilience is a human capability as much as a technical one, built through practice, clear roles and communication that helps the public know what to do.
