Credit Suisse's Restructuring Plan and the Challenge of Rebuilding Confidence

Credit Suisse reported a large quarterly loss and planned to raise about 4 billion Swiss francs, carve out its investment bank and cut about 9,000 jobs. Its chairman said the bank had become unfocused, a cultural diagnosis as much as a financial one.

Event: October 2022 · Organizations: Credit Suisse, Saudi National Bank, CS First Boston
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The bank's chairman described it as having become unfocused, framing the problem as one of direction and culture, not only capital.
  • Restructurings ask employees, clients and investors to change behavior at a moment when trust is low.
  • Market reaction on announcement day showed how confidence, not only the plan's content, determines outcomes.
  • Boards should track client, employee and investor behavior as leading indicators during turnarounds.

What happened

On October 27, 2022, Credit Suisse reported a third-quarter net loss of about 4 billion Swiss francs and announced a restructuring plan, according to Al Jazeera and Fortune. The plan included raising about 4 billion Swiss francs through a share issue to qualified investors and a rights offering, with Saudi National Bank committing up to 1.5 billion francs for a stake of up to 9.9 percent.

Fortune reported that the bank planned to carve out its investment bank under the CS First Boston name, sell a majority of its securitized products group to a consortium led by Apollo Global Management, reduce its cost base by about 2.5 billion francs and cut about 9,000 positions, bringing headcount from roughly 52,000 to about 43,000 by the end of 2025. The bank's chief executive called it "a historic moment for Credit Suisse."

Investor reaction was negative. Al Jazeera reported that shares opened down more than 7 percent in Zurich, and Fortune reported that the bank's American depositary receipts fell more than 19 percent that day. Al Jazeera noted that the bank had faced a series of scandals and quoted the chairman saying the bank had "become unfocused" in recent years. Fortune reported that the bank's market value stood at about $16.4 billion, down from more than $22 billion in 2021.

The human dimension

An overhaul of strategy at this scale is, at its core, a request for behavior change from several groups at once. Employees must stay engaged through uncertainty about their roles. Clients must decide whether to keep assets and relationships with the institution. Investors must decide whether to commit new capital. Each group watches the others, and their decisions reinforce one another, for better or worse.

The chairman's description of a bank that had become unfocused points to cultural drift: decisions about risk, priorities and accountability that accumulated over years. Cultures do not reset with an announcement. They change through consistent leadership behavior, clear decision rights and incentives that reward the conduct the new strategy requires. That takes time, and confidence must be sustained while it happens.

The signals that mattered

In a turnaround, the most informative signals are often behavioral and visible before financial results confirm them. Fortune reported that the bank had already faced comparisons to Lehman Brothers and that executives had sought to reassure clients and investors about liquidity, an indication of how much confidence itself had become a risk factor.

Repeated reassurance is a particularly important signal. When leaders must keep stating that an institution is stable, the statements themselves indicate that stakeholders are questioning it, and the behavior of clients and counterparties deserves closer measurement.

  • Client asset flows and relationship attrition in core businesses
  • Voluntary departures among senior bankers and control function staff
  • Frequency of public reassurance statements about liquidity or stability
  • Repeated conduct or risk incidents across different business units
  • Gap between announced strategy and investor reaction on announcement day

Lessons for leaders and investors

Restructuring plans are often judged on capital ratios and cost targets, but their success depends on whether people believe and act on them. Leaders who measure confidence directly, through client, employee and investor behavior, can see earlier whether a plan is taking hold.

Investors in financial institutions can also ask how a restructuring plan addresses the behaviors that led to the need for it. Cost cuts and asset sales change the shape of a business; they do not by themselves change how risk is identified, escalated and acted upon. Evidence of changes in those behaviors is an important leading indicator.

  • Define behavioral milestones for a turnaround, not only financial targets.
  • Retain and align the leaders whose behavior signals the new culture to others.
  • Communicate uncertainty candidly; repeated reassurance can signal stress.
  • Monitor risk culture with surveys and incident data, tracked over time.

The public-good dimension

Large banks are deeply connected to the financial system, and their stability affects depositors, employees, counterparties and economies well beyond their shareholders. That makes risk culture and governance a matter of public interest. Institutions that measure and strengthen the behaviors underlying sound risk management protect not only their own franchise but the broader system that relies on them.
