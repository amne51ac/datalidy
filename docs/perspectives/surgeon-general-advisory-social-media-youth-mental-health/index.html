Surgeon General Issues Advisory on Social Media and Youth Mental Health

On May 23, 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health. It acknowledged benefits, warned of serious risks and asked technology companies to share data. The implications reach product design and investment.

Event: May 2023 · Organizations: Office of the U.S. Surgeon General, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The advisory warned social media can pose a profound risk of harm to young people's mental health, WebMD reported.
  • It asked technology companies to assess effects on adolescents, be more transparent and share data with independent researchers.
  • Engagement features are persuasive technology; placing the full burden of self-control on teenagers and parents is unrealistic.
  • Pairing engagement KPIs with validated wellbeing measures is becoming a governance and diligence issue for consumer technology.

What happened

On May 23, 2023, U.S. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health. According to WebMD, the 25-page advisory warned that social media can pose "a profound risk of harm to the mental health and well-being" of young people, while also acknowledging benefits, particularly self-affirming ones for members of marginalized groups who find support and connection online.

The advisory reported that social media use is nearly universal among 13 to 17 year olds, that one in three teens say they use it almost constantly, and that as many as 40 percent of children ages 8 to 12 use social media, according to WebMD. Healio reported that the advisory linked platform use to risks including body dissatisfaction, eating disorders and low self-esteem, and that Murthy called social media an important driver of the youth mental health crisis. Murthy said, "At this point, we do not have enough evidence to say with confidence that social media is sufficiently safe for our kids."

The advisory addressed several groups. Healio reported that it asked policymakers to strengthen safety standards, asked technology companies to better assess their platforms' effects on adolescents, be more transparent and share data with independent researchers, and encouraged parents to set household plans for technology use. WebMD reported that it recommended family media plans and tech-free zones, and it called on researchers to prioritize studies of social media and youth mental health.

The human dimension

Healio noted the advisory's emphasis that adolescents are at a critical stage of brain development, a period of heightened sensitivity to social feedback and comparison. Platforms built around feeds, notifications and visible social reactions interact directly with those sensitivities. Many common engagement features are well-known persuasive technology patterns, and the same mechanisms that keep users returning can make it harder for young people to keep the boundaries they may want to keep.

The advisory's call for shared responsibility reflects a behavioral reality: asking individual teenagers and parents to manage risks built into product design places the burden on those with the least control over that design. Family media plans and tech-free zones can help, but they compete with systems designed by large teams to capture attention. Durable change is more likely when product defaults, policy and household norms point in the same direction.

The signals that mattered

The advisory's call for independent data access points to a measurement gap. Indicators that would help researchers, companies and families see risk earlier include the following. Many can be studied without collecting more personal data than necessary, through the kind of independent research partnerships the advisory encouraged.

  • Late-night use among minors and its relationship to sleep
  • Validated measures of compulsive checking and difficulty disengaging
  • Exposure to harassment and harmful content, especially among girls and transgender youth
  • Independent measures of how often teen safety settings are used and whether they work

Lessons for leaders and investors

For technology leaders, the advisory signals that engagement metrics alone are an incomplete and potentially misleading measure of product success where young users are involved. Wellbeing measures, designed and validated with the same rigor as growth metrics, offer a more complete picture. For investors, regulatory and reputational exposure in consumer technology increasingly depends on how products affect young users, which makes youth safety a material diligence topic rather than a communications concern.

Schools and health systems also have a role, since they see effects on attention, sleep and mood firsthand. Simple, validated screening questions about technology use can give clinicians and educators an earlier view of which young people may need support.

  • Pair engagement KPIs with validated wellbeing measures for younger users
  • Make safe, age-appropriate settings the default rather than an opt-in
  • Include youth safety practices in technology diligence and portfolio reviews
  • Treat independent research access as a sign of credible governance

The public-good dimension

Youth mental health is a public health priority, and the advisory frames social media as one factor that can be changed. Ethical persuasive design, which helps people do what they already want to do, is directly at stake. When product teams, schools, families, clinicians and policymakers share evidence and goals, technology can support connection and learning while reducing harm. The standard for success should be whether young people are better off, not only whether they keep scrolling.
