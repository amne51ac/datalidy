Heathrow's Closure After a Substation Fire and the Question of Resilience

Losing one of three power supplies shut Europe's busiest airport for most of a day. Reviews later backed the decision to close, while raising harder questions about how critical infrastructure plans for failures it considers unlikely.

Event: March 2025 · Organizations: Heathrow Airport, National Grid, National Energy System Operator
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A fire at the North Hyde substation closed Heathrow on March 21, 2025, cancelling more than 1,300 flights, Euronews reported.
  • An airport-commissioned review found the closure decision reasonable and said alternatives would not have materially changed the outcome.
  • Euronews later reported findings that a fault at the substation had been known since 2018 and not repaired.
  • Resilience depends on how organizations judge low-probability risks and how they coordinate across ownership boundaries.

What happened

On March 21, 2025, Heathrow Airport closed after a fire at the North Hyde electrical substation in west London knocked out one of the three substations that supply it. Euronews reported that more than 1,300 flights were cancelled and about 200,000 passengers were disrupted. Heathrow said hundreds of critical systems had to be safely powered down before restarting, and operations resumed after about 18 hours. The airport and National Grid offered differing views. National Grid's chief executive told the Financial Times, as reported by Euronews, that each substation individually could provide enough power to Heathrow. The UK government ordered a review of wider lessons for energy resilience in critical national infrastructure, and police found no evidence of foul play.

On May 28, Heathrow published a review it had commissioned, which concluded that the airport made the right decisions in exceptionally difficult circumstances. In July, Euronews reported that the energy system operator's investigation found a fault at the substation had been identified in 2018 but not repaired. Heathrow's summary of its review said the airport had contingency plans in place and that further planned investment in energy resilience was key to reducing the impact of similar events.

The human dimension

Decisions to close large facilities are made under uncertainty, time pressure and public scrutiny. Leaders on the night had to judge whether systems could be restored safely and how quickly, with incomplete information about the power supply. Euronews reported that the Transport Secretary declined to endorse or criticize the decision, noting she did not have all the information available to those making it. That restraint is a useful model for anyone assessing decisions after the fact.

The longer-term human dimension concerns how organizations judge rare risks. Planning for the loss of one supply among three can feel unnecessary until it happens. When responsibilities are split between an airport, a grid operator and distributors, each party may assume another has covered a scenario. Deferred maintenance, such as the unrepaired fault reported in July, often reflects competing priorities and budget trade-offs rather than a single poor choice.

The signals that mattered

Resilience shows up in data before a crisis. The useful signals describe dependencies, maintenance backlogs and the quality of coordination between organizations that share critical systems.

Most of these indicators already exist in maintenance logs, engineering studies and exercise reports. The challenge is that they sit in different organizations. Bringing them together into a shared view of resilience, reviewed by leaders on both sides of a dependency, is what turns isolated data into a warning that someone can act on.

  • Age and count of open faults on critical supply equipment
  • Tested time to switch critical loads to alternative supplies
  • Joint exercises between operators of shared infrastructure
  • Clarity of decision rights during off-hours emergencies
  • Dependence of restart procedures on a single power configuration

Lessons for leaders and investors

Boards overseeing critical operations should ask not only whether contingency plans exist but whether they have been tested against realistic failures, including those judged unlikely. Investors in infrastructure and the businesses that rely on it can request evidence of maintenance backlogs, cross-party drills and recovery times. Clear decision rights matter too, because the first hours of a crisis leave little room to debate who decides.

The episode also shows how quickly public debate turns to blame between organizations. Agreeing in advance on how incidents will be reviewed, and by whom, helps parties focus on learning rather than on defending positions. Independent investigation and shared publication of findings support that goal.

  • Rehearse the loss of any single critical supply, not only total failure.
  • Track deferred maintenance on shared assets as a board-level risk.
  • Agree decision rights and communication protocols across organizations in advance.
  • Measure recovery time, not just prevention, as a resilience KPI.

The public-good dimension

Airports, power grids and hospitals are shared public systems even when privately operated, and a failure in one quickly becomes a disruption for many. Transparent investigations, published findings and coordinated investment in resilience protect travelers, workers and communities, and help the public trust that critical services will be restored safely when something fails.
