FTX's Collapse: Governance, Diligence and the Speed of a Run

Within about nine days, a report questioning an affiliated trading firm's balance sheet led to mass withdrawals, a failed rescue and a Chapter 11 filing covering FTX and more than 100 related companies. The episode is a case study in governance, trust and diligence.

Event: November 2022 · Organizations: FTX, Alameda Research, Binance
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A single report about an affiliated firm triggered a run that unfolded in days, showing how quickly concentrated trust can reverse.
  • A potential acquirer withdrew after about a day of reviewing the balance sheet, a stark contrast to prior investor confidence.
  • Governance structures, related-party ties and controls are diligence questions, not formalities, even in fast-growing companies.
  • Customers without deposit insurance bore exposure they may not have understood, raising questions about disclosure.

What happened

On November 11, 2022, cryptocurrency exchange FTX, its affiliated trading firm Alameda Research and about 130 related companies filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Delaware, Al Jazeera reported. The founder resigned as chief executive and a new chief executive was appointed to oversee the process. In its petition, FTX reported more than 100,000 creditors. The new chief executive said the group had valuable assets that could be administered effectively only through an organized joint process.

According to a timeline published by The Block, the sequence began on November 2, when CoinDesk reported concerns about Alameda Research's balance sheet. On November 6, Binance said it would sell its holdings of FTX's token. Withdrawals from FTX appeared to pause on November 8, the same day Binance announced a non-binding agreement to acquire FTX's non-U.S. business. On November 9, Binance withdrew after reviewing FTX's finances; a crypto lending executive quoted by Al Jazeera said the rival walked away after "only 24 hours of due diligence."

The Block reported that investors were told of a shortfall of up to $8 billion and that The Wall Street Journal had reported FTX lent about $10 billion to Alameda Research. Al Jazeera quoted the founder saying, "I'm really sorry, again, that we ended up here." Investigations into what happened were only beginning at the time. The Block noted that the founder's personal fortune, estimated by the Bloomberg Billionaires Index at about $16 billion, had been wiped out.

The human dimension

The speed of the collapse reflects how trust works in financial platforms. Customers, counterparties and investors relied in part on reputation and on the confidence of other sophisticated parties. When a credible report raised questions, and a large rival signaled doubt, many people acted on the same information at once. Their withdrawals were individually reasonable and collectively decisive.

The episode also raises questions about how governance behaves under rapid growth. Close ties between an exchange and an affiliated trading firm, and decision-making concentrated among a small group, are structures that make independent challenge harder. Investors, auditors and board members face real constraints in fast-moving private markets, including competition for allocations and limited information rights. Those pressures can make it difficult to ask uncomfortable questions.

The signals that mattered

Several governance and behavioral signals are relevant to any fast-growing financial business. None proves wrongdoing by itself, but each warrants scrutiny in diligence and monitoring.

Some of these features are common in early-stage companies and are often accepted as the price of speed. The FTX case suggests that when a company holds customer assets, the tolerance for informal governance should fall sharply, and investors should expect controls to mature as quickly as the business grows.

  • Material financial ties between a platform and affiliated trading entities
  • Limited board independence or investor information rights
  • Reliance on a proprietary token as a significant balance sheet asset
  • Concentration of decision-making authority in a small leadership group
  • Speed of customer withdrawals relative to available liquid assets

Lessons for leaders and investors

The FTX collapse reinforced that governance and controls are not optional for high-growth companies, especially those holding customer assets. For investors, diligence that reads leadership behavior, decision rights and related-party structures is as important as diligence on market opportunity.

Boards and investors also benefit from asking how leaders respond to challenge. A management team that welcomes difficult questions, shares information readily and documents decisions is easier to oversee. Resistance to scrutiny, however it is explained, is itself a signal worth weighing.

  • Require independent board members and clear information rights before investing.
  • Map related-party relationships and how customer assets are segregated.
  • Assess management team decision-making and openness to challenge.
  • Monitor customer flow data as a leading indicator of confidence.

The public-good dimension

Many people affected by the collapse were individual customers who could not assess the risks behind the platform they used. Clear disclosure, segregation of customer assets and robust oversight protect those customers and the wider credibility of financial innovation. At Datalidy, we see the episode as a reminder that leadership behavior and governance are measurable, and that measuring them carefully serves both investors and the public.
