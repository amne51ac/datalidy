UPS and the Teamsters Reach a Deal Before a Nationwide Strike

On July 25, 2023, UPS and the Teamsters reached a tentative five-year agreement covering more than 340,000 workers, averting a strike set for August 1. The deal addressed part-time pay, a two-tier driver system and heat safety in delivery vehicles.

Event: July 2023 · Organizations: UPS, International Brotherhood of Teamsters
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Perceived fairness, including two-tier pay structures, shapes workforce behavior as strongly as absolute wage levels.
  • Heat safety became a bargaining issue, showing how physical working conditions surface in labor relations.
  • Workforce signals such as strike authorization votes and public bargaining positions are leading indicators of operational risk.
  • Investors in labor-intensive businesses should monitor contract cycles and frontline sentiment as part of risk assessment.

What happened

On July 25, 2023, UPS and the International Brotherhood of Teamsters reached a tentative five-year agreement, averting a strike by more than 340,000 workers that had been scheduled to begin August 1, according to Retail Brew. The Associated Press, in a report published by WHYY, said the agreement covers U.S. Teamsters-represented employees in small-package roles and that the union described it as "historic." The deal was subject to a ratification vote by members from August 3 to August 22.

Under the tentative agreement, current part-time and full-time workers would receive a raise of $2.75 an hour in 2023, rising to $7.50 over the contract, and new part-time workers would start at $21 an hour, Retail Brew reported. The AP reported that the agreement ended a two-tier wage system for drivers that the union had called unfair. UPS said it would add air conditioning to U.S. small delivery vehicles purchased after January 1, 2024, and Retail Brew reported the deal included 7,500 new full-time positions.

UPS chief executive Carol Tomé called it a "win-win-win agreement" that gives the company the "flexibility we need to stay competitive," according to Retail Brew. Teamsters General President Sean O'Brien said UPS "put $30 billion in new money on the table"; UPS did not confirm that figure. The AP noted that UPS profits had grown substantially since the last contract, during the pandemic surge in home deliveries, and that the union argued its members had carried much of that growth.

The human dimension

Two of the central issues were about fairness and physical strain rather than headline pay alone. Two-tier systems, in which people doing similar work are paid differently based on when they were hired, can erode trust and cohesion even when overall compensation is competitive. Workers compare themselves with colleagues on the same route or shift, and perceived inequity can affect morale, retention and willingness to go beyond minimum requirements.

The air conditioning commitment made heat in delivery vehicles an explicit part of the contract. Physical working conditions affect safety, fatigue and attention, which in turn affect service quality. Both sides faced real constraints: the company needed cost flexibility in a competitive market, and the union represented members who had worked through an intense period of demand. Reaching agreement before the deadline reflected a shared interest in avoiding a disruption that could have affected businesses and households nationwide.

The signals that mattered

Labor negotiations rarely surprise anyone who is watching the workforce closely. Strike authorization votes, public bargaining positions and frontline complaints about working conditions all signaled the stakes months before the deadline.

  • Turnover and tenure differences between workers on different pay tiers
  • Frequency of frontline reports about heat, fatigue or physical strain
  • Participation and margins in union authorization or ratification votes
  • Gap between company performance messaging and frontline perceptions of shared gains

Lessons for leaders and investors

Contract cycles in labor-intensive businesses are predictable events with unpredictable outcomes. Leaders can reduce that uncertainty by measuring frontline sentiment and working conditions continuously, rather than only during negotiation, and by addressing structural fairness issues before they become bargaining flashpoints.

For boards, the deal illustrates that labor costs and labor risk are different things to manage. Terms can be modeled in advance, but the likelihood of disruption depends on trust, perceived fairness and the quality of day-to-day relationships between managers and frontline workers. Organizations that invest in those relationships between contract cycles often find negotiations less adversarial, because both sides start from a shared understanding of conditions on the ground rather than from competing narratives.

  • Track perceived fairness and working-condition data as ongoing people analytics, not crisis inputs
  • Model the operational cost of a disruption alongside the cost of proposed terms
  • Communicate how performance gains are shared in terms frontline workers find credible
  • In diligence, review labor contract timelines and workforce sentiment data for portfolio companies

The public-good dimension

Delivery networks are part of everyday infrastructure, carrying everything from household goods to medications. A stable agreement protects not only the company and its workers but the communities that depend on reliable service. Attention to heat safety reflects a broader public health concern about working conditions as temperatures rise. Organizations that measure and improve frontline conditions protect people as well as performance, and Datalidy regards that alignment as the core of sound people strategy.
