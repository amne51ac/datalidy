Air Canada CEO to Retire After Backlash Over English-Only Condolences

Days after two pilots died in a runway collision in New York, Air Canada's chief executive faced national criticism for a condolence message delivered almost entirely in English. His retirement announcement shows how crisis communication is judged on empathy, not only content.

Event: March 2026 · Organizations: Air Canada, Jazz Aviation, National Transportation Safety Board
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • In a moment of grief, the form of a message can matter as much as its words, especially for a bilingual national carrier.
  • Language had been a known issue since the CEO's 2021 appointment, a visible early signal that went unresolved.
  • Air Canada's board said it will consider the ability to communicate in French when assessing successors.
  • Boards should assess crisis communication capability as part of CEO selection and succession planning.

What happened

On March 22, 2026, an Air Canada Express flight operated by Jazz Aviation collided with a fire-and-rescue vehicle on a runway at New York's LaGuardia Airport, killing the two pilots, CBS News and the Associated Press reported. The Irish Times reported that the U.S. National Transportation Safety Board has opened an investigation and said an electronic system failed to warn air traffic control of the imminent collision. The investigation is ongoing, and no final cause has been determined.

Air Canada's chief executive recorded a condolence video in English with French subtitles, and, according to The Irish Times, spoke only "bonjour" and "merci" in French. One of the pilots who died was a French-speaking Quebecer. Prime Minister Mark Carney said he was "very disappointed," and the Office of the Commissioner of Official Languages received hundreds of complaints, CBS reported. The CEO apologized, saying that despite many lessons he was still unable to express himself adequately in French.

On March 30, Air Canada announced that its CEO would retire by the end of September. The board said it would consider performance criteria for candidates "including the ability to communicate in French," The Irish Times reported. Air Canada is required by law to serve customers in both English and French. Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon thanked the CEO and said the government would work with the airline to provide bilingual service, CBS reported.

The human dimension

Grief changes how people listen. After a tragedy, families, colleagues and the public are not evaluating a message for its information; they are asking whether the speaker understands their loss. For many in Quebec, hearing condolences for a French-speaking pilot delivered almost entirely in English signaled distance rather than solidarity. The CEO's apology acknowledged the shortfall directly, and the reaction made clear that the issue was respect for the bereaved, not linguistic skill alone.

The episode also reflects a long-standing expectation. The Irish Times noted that the CEO faced calls to resign after a 2021 speech given almost entirely in English, and Quebec's premier noted an earlier promise to learn French. When a known gap persists, a crisis can turn it from a reputational irritant into a defining moment. Executives under intense stress tend to fall back on their most practiced behaviors, which is why preparation matters more than intention.

The signals that mattered

Leadership risks of this kind are visible long before a crisis. They show up in stakeholder feedback, complaint data and the gap between commitments and observed behavior.

Few of these signals would appear on a conventional executive dashboard. Yet each was visible in public reporting or stakeholder feedback before the crash, and each spoke to whether leadership could communicate effectively with the people it serves in the moments that matter most.

  • Public commitments by executives tracked against observable progress over time
  • Official language complaints and their trend before any crisis
  • Stakeholder sentiment in key regions about leadership representation
  • Readiness of crisis communication protocols for multilingual audiences
  • Board review of CEO capabilities against the company's legal and cultural obligations

Lessons for leaders and investors

Crisis communication is a leadership competency that can be assessed, developed and rehearsed. For companies with cultural, linguistic or community obligations, those obligations should be built into crisis playbooks so that the first public message reflects them, regardless of who delivers it.

Executive development has a role here as well. Language skills and crisis communication take sustained practice, and leaders benefit from structured coaching, rehearsal and honest feedback long before they need those skills under pressure. Boards can support that development while also holding leaders accountable for progress.

  • Include crisis communication and cultural fluency in CEO succession criteria.
  • Pre-plan who speaks, in which languages, in the first hours after a tragedy.
  • Treat unmet public commitments by leaders as governance risks, not personal matters.
  • Keep the focus of crisis messaging on those affected, not on the organization.

The public-good dimension

Two crew members lost their lives, and the most important work ahead is the safety investigation and the support of their families and colleagues. National carriers also hold a public trust that extends beyond transportation, including respect for the communities and languages they serve. Leaders who communicate with empathy in their stakeholders' own terms help people grieve, and help institutions keep the trust they will need during the long work of improving safety.
