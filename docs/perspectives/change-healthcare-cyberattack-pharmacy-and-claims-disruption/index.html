Change Healthcare Cyberattack Disrupts Pharmacies and Payments

UnitedHealth Group disclosed in February 2024 that Change Healthcare's systems had been compromised. Pharmacies struggled to process prescriptions and providers faced payment backlogs, showing how one hidden dependency shapes patient and clinician behavior.

Event: February 2024 · Organizations: Change Healthcare, UnitedHealth Group
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • A single claims processor sat behind a large share of pharmacy and physician transactions, so one outage spread across the system.
  • When systems fail, patients and pharmacists improvise, and those workarounds carry clinical and financial costs.
  • Mapping hidden dependencies is a behavioral exercise as much as a technical one, because workflows reveal them.
  • Investors in health care should weigh concentration and continuity risk alongside scale efficiencies.

What happened

In a Form 8-K filed with the SEC, UnitedHealth Group disclosed that on February 21, 2024, it identified that a suspected nation-state associated cybersecurity threat actor had gained access to some Change Healthcare information technology systems. The company said it isolated the affected systems and could not yet estimate the duration or extent of the disruption. UnitedHealth said it believed the incident was isolated to Change Healthcare and that its other systems were operating normally.

CNN reported on February 23 that pharmacies across the United States were having trouble processing some prescriptions, including at military hospital pharmacies. According to Bloomberg reporting published by Insurance Journal on February 28, Change Healthcare processes insurance claims and pharmacy requests for more than 340,000 physicians and 60,000 pharmacies, and the disruption created growing backlogs of claims and payments. CNN reported delays and refill problems at some military pharmacies.

UnitedHealth said most pharmacies had changed how they process claims to work around the outage, and described reports of members unable to obtain prescriptions as minimal, according to Bloomberg. The same reporting said UnitedHealth attributed the attack to the group known as BlackCat or ALPHV after initially suspecting nation-state links. Investigations and recovery continued well beyond February. The full scope of the incident was not known at the time.

The human dimension

Health care runs on routines. A pharmacist submits a claim, a payer responds, a patient pays a copay and leaves with medication. When that routine breaks, people adapt quickly: pharmacists switch processors, patients pay cash or delay a fill, practices hold claims. Each workaround is sensible. Together they create a hidden backlog of delayed therapy, unpaid work and administrative strain that rarely shows up in a single dashboard.

The behavioral cost falls hardest on people with the least slack. A patient deciding whether to pay out of pocket or wait is making a medication adherence decision under stress. A small practice waiting on payments is making staffing and access decisions. Those choices are where a technical outage becomes a public health and financial story.

The signals that mattered

Concentration risk is often invisible until it fails, but the workflows that depend on it can be observed. Useful indicators include the following.

Most of these indicators live outside any one organization's systems, which is part of the challenge. Pharmacies, practices, payers and patients each see only their piece of the disruption. Bringing those views together, even through simple shared measures, helps leaders recognize when a single dependency is carrying more of the system than anyone intended.

  • Share of claims and prescriptions routed through a single intermediary
  • Time needed for pharmacies and practices to switch to an alternate processor
  • Prescription abandonment and delayed fills during service interruptions
  • Days of cash on hand for practices dependent on a single payment channel
  • Frequency of tested downtime procedures among front-line staff

Lessons for leaders and investors

For health system leaders, the lesson is to map dependencies through how work actually gets done, not only through vendor contracts. For investors, the case highlights that the efficiencies of consolidation come with correlated risk. Diligence on health care platforms and their customers should include continuity planning, workaround capacity and the human readiness to operate in downtime.

Payers and large platforms also have a role. Clear, frequent communication with pharmacies and practices during an outage shapes how quickly they adopt workarounds and how much trust remains afterward. Communication quality is itself a measurable part of resilience.

  • Map critical workflows end to end and identify single points of failure.
  • Rehearse downtime procedures with the staff who would carry them out.
  • Track patient-level effects such as delayed fills during any disruption.
  • In diligence, value resilience and redundancy alongside scale and margin.

The public-good dimension

When claims and prescriptions stop moving, the people affected are patients waiting for medication and clinicians trying to keep doors open. The public good depends on resilient infrastructure and on measuring the human consequences of disruption, not only system uptime. Datalidy's perspective is that patient adherence and provider stability are behaviors that deserve the same monitoring as servers.
