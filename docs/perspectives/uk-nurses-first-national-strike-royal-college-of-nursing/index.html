UK Nurses Strike for the First Time in the RCN's 106-Year History

On December 15, 2022, Royal College of Nursing members struck over pay and patient safety. The dispute shows how retention and workload signals accumulate, and why health systems need to read them early.

Talk to us

Event: December 2022 · Organizations: Royal College of Nursing, National Health Service, UK Department of Health and Social Care
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Up to 100,000 RCN members in England, Wales and Northern Ireland walked out on December 15, 2022, The New Arab reported.
  • The government said the independent pay review award would not be reopened, while the union cited retention and patient safety.
  • Early-career attrition, overtime reliance and intention-to-leave scores are leading indicators of labor conflict in health systems.
  • Clinician wellbeing deserves the same measurement rigor as waiting lists, because it shapes patient care and workforce supply.

What happened

On December 15, 2022, members of the Royal College of Nursing walked out in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the first strike in the union's 106-year history. The New Arab, reporting on the day, said up to 100,000 members stopped work from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. after rejecting the government's pay award, with a second strike date planned for December 20. The New Statesman reported that members would strike in at least 177 NHS trusts after a ballot of about 300,000 members held between October 6 and November 2.

The union described the action as a last resort. According to the RCN, as reported by The New Arab, chemotherapy, dialysis, intensive care and neonatal and pediatric intensive care were protected, while other services were reduced to Christmas staffing levels. The RCN sought a pay rise well above inflation, which The New Arab noted had reached a 41-year high of 11.1 percent in October.

Health Secretary Steve Barclay called the strikes deeply regrettable. He said the award recommended by the independent NHS Pay Review Body, a rise of at least 1,400 pounds on top of a 3 percent rise the previous year, would not be reopened, though he was open to talks on wider issues, and he argued that further increases would divert money from frontline services. England's chief nursing officer warned that applying night staffing levels to day shifts could affect patient safety, while NHS Providers said trusts were working hard to limit the impact on patients.

The human dimension

Nurses do not strike lightly. The profession's identity is built around duty to patients, and the RCN itself framed the walkout as a last resort. When a workforce with that identity votes to walk out, it signals that the psychological contract, the unwritten exchange of effort, recognition and fairness, has been under strain for some time. Unions told The New Arab that members were skipping meals, that staff shortages were overworking nurses, and that newly qualified nurses often left after a year or two.

Both sides faced real constraints. The government framed its position around an independent pay process and fiscal tradeoffs; the union framed its position around retention and patient safety. In effect, each side was reading different indicators. Pay review processes weigh affordability and comparators, while nurses experience workload, vacancies and the daily gap between the care they are trained to give and the care they are able to give. When those two views of reality diverge for long enough, formal processes lose legitimacy with the people they are meant to serve.

The signals that mattered

Strikes in essential services are rarely sudden. The precursors are usually visible in workforce data well before a ballot is called, if leaders look for them and treat them as predictive.

  • Early-career attrition, especially nurses leaving within one or two years of qualifying
  • Rising reliance on overtime, agency staff and unfilled shifts
  • Engagement survey items on fairness, recognition and intention to leave
  • Ballot turnout and participation across a union's full membership
  • Frontline reports of moral distress about care staff feel unable to deliver

Lessons for leaders and investors

For health system leaders, the lesson is that retention is a behavioral outcome with leading indicators, and those indicators can be tracked monthly rather than discovered in a ballot result. For investors in health care staffing, private providers and health technology, labor relations in public systems shape demand, workforce availability and the adoption of tools that claim to reduce workload. Products that genuinely return time to clinicians address a root cause; products that add documentation burden may deepen it.

  • Track intention to leave and early attrition as predictive KPIs, not annual HR statistics
  • Pair affordability analysis with measures of workload and staff experience
  • Evaluate health technology investments by whether they reduce clinician burden in practice

The public-good dimension

The patients most affected by staffing strain are often those with the fewest alternatives. A nursing workforce that feels valued, safe and able to practice well is a public health asset, not merely a budget line. Measuring clinician wellbeing with the same rigor applied to waiting lists would give governments, unions and the public a shared and earlier view of risk, and a better basis for negotiating before patients feel the consequences.
