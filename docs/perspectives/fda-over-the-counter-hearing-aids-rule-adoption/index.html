FDA Creates Over-the-Counter Hearing Aids: An Adoption Challenge

The FDA finalized a rule creating a category of over-the-counter hearing aids for adults with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss. The policy removes a structural barrier, but adoption will depend on how people recognize hearing loss, choose devices and keep using them.

Event: August 2022 · Organizations: U.S. Food and Drug Administration, American Speech-Language-Hearing Association
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The rule removed the need for a medical exam, prescription or professional fitting for adults with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss.
  • Access is necessary but not sufficient: recognizing the need, choosing a device and sustained use are separate behaviors.
  • Professional groups welcomed much of the rule while raising concerns about assessment and support when devices disappoint.
  • Product design, labeling and onboarding will shape whether new buyers keep using devices or abandon them.

What happened

On August 16, 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration announced a final rule establishing a new regulatory category for over-the-counter hearing aids. According to the FDA's announcement, as published by AudiologyOnline, adults aged 18 and older with perceived mild to moderate hearing impairment can buy these devices without a medical exam, prescription or fitting by an audiologist. Devices for severe impairment or for users under 18 remain prescription only. The rule appeared in the Federal Register on August 17.

The FDA said close to 30 million adults in the United States could potentially benefit from hearing aids and that it expected the rule to make devices less expensive. HHS Secretary Xavier Becerra called the action "a significant milestone in making hearing aids more cost-effective and accessible." Consumers could see devices in stores as early as mid-October 2022. The rule took effect 60 days after publication, and manufacturers of devices already on the market received a transition period to comply.

Compared with the proposed rule, the final rule lowered maximum sound output, required a user-adjustable volume control and simplified labeling, according to the FDA. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association said it was pleased with many provisions, but noted the rule contained no gain limit and little mention of consulting an audiologist. ASHA said it would work with allied professional groups to educate the public.

The human dimension

Removing a prescription requirement addresses one barrier, but hearing aid adoption involves a chain of behaviors. People must first notice and acknowledge hearing loss, which often develops gradually and carries stigma. They must then choose among unfamiliar devices, fit and adjust them, and persist through an adaptation period in which sound can feel unnatural. Each step is a possible point of abandonment.

The rule's own terms reflect this. Eligibility rests on perceived hearing loss, so self-assessment becomes the gateway. ASHA emphasized the importance of audiological assessment before fitting any hearing aid and said it planned to educate the public about consulting professionals. These positions are not necessarily in conflict with broader access; the open question is how to design journeys in which people who need more help find it, while people who can manage on their own are not held back.

The signals that mattered

For manufacturers, retailers, clinicians and policymakers, the measures that will show whether the policy is working are behavioral, and many can be tracked from the first months of sale.

Unit sales will rise as products reach shelves, but sales alone cannot show whether people benefit. Sustained use and satisfaction after the adaptation period are the indicators most likely to predict long-term value for consumers and for the companies serving them.

  • Return rates and reasons, segmented by buyer type and device features
  • Daily wear time in the weeks after purchase, where devices can report it
  • Share of buyers who later seek professional assessment or support
  • Completion rates for in-app setup and self-fitting steps
  • Customer language about stigma, comfort and sound quality in reviews

Lessons for leaders and investors

New consumer health categories often reward companies that treat onboarding and sustained use as the product, not as an afterthought. The OTC hearing aid market creates opportunities for device makers, retailers and digital health companies, but the winners are likely to be those who design for the full adoption journey and measure it honestly.

  • Design onboarding around the first weeks of adaptation, when abandonment risk is highest.
  • Make it easy to escalate from self-care to professional care without friction or shame.
  • Track sustained use, not unit sales, as the leading indicator of value.
  • Test labeling and setup instructions with older adults through usability research.

The public-good dimension

Untreated hearing loss can affect communication, work and social connection, so lowering cost and access barriers is a meaningful public-good step. The benefit will be realized only if people actually wear the devices and get help when they need it. Clear, plain-language guidance, honest marketing and research that follows real-world use can turn a regulatory change into improved quality of life. At Datalidy, we see the rule as a reminder that access opens a door and good behavioral design helps people walk through it.
