Shopify Makes AI Use a Baseline Expectation for Every Employee

Most organizations encourage AI experimentation. Shopify chose to require it, linking use to reviews and hiring decisions. The memo is a clear case study in using prompts, norms and incentives to drive technology adoption at scale.

Event: April 2025 · Organizations: Shopify
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Shopify's CEO called effective AI use a fundamental expectation of everyone at the company, BetaKit reported on April 7, 2025.
  • Teams must show why AI cannot do the work before requesting more headcount or resources, according to the memo.
  • Adding AI questions to performance reviews turns encouragement into a norm, which can accelerate adoption and raise anxiety.
  • Leaders should measure the quality of AI-assisted outcomes, not only usage, to avoid rewarding activity over results.

What happened

On April 7, 2025, Shopify chief executive Tobi Lütke published an internal memo on X, saying it was about to leak, BetaKit reported. The memo, dated March 20, stated that reflexive AI usage was now a baseline expectation and that using AI effectively was a fundamental expectation of everyone at Shopify. It said Shopify would add AI usage questions to performance and peer review questionnaires.

The memo also changed how teams request resources. According to BetaKit, it said teams must demonstrate why they cannot get what they want done using AI before asking for more headcount and resources. Lütke wrote that his earlier encouragement to experiment with AI had been too much of a suggestion. BNN Bloomberg, reporting from The Canadian Press, described AI as now an expectation that would factor into performance reviews and hiring. BetaKit noted that research on AI and productivity is mixed, citing a study of customer support agents in which gains were largest for less experienced workers.

The human dimension

Technology adoption is a behavior change problem. Encouragement tends to reach early adopters, while most employees wait for clearer signals about what matters. Shopify's memo used three powerful levers at once: a public prompt from the top, a social norm that applies to everyone including executives, and an incentive linked to reviews and resources. In the language of the Fogg Behavior Model, it raised motivation and supplied a prompt; the remaining question is ability, meaning whether people have the time, tools and training to use AI well.

Mandates also carry emotional weight. Linking AI use to headcount decisions may lead some employees to wonder whether their roles are at risk, which can encourage either learning or quiet resistance. How leaders frame the change, and whether they share examples of good use and honest failures, shapes whether people experience it as investment in their skills or as pressure.

The signals that mattered

Before and after a mandate like this, the most informative signals describe the depth and quality of adoption, not only the number of people logging in.

Usage alone is a vanity metric for AI adoption. A team can report high usage while producing work that needs heavy correction, or while avoiding the hardest tasks where AI might help most. Combining behavioral, quality and sentiment measures gives a more honest picture of whether adoption is creating value.

  • Share of teams using AI in core workflows rather than occasional tasks
  • Quality and error rates of AI-assisted work compared with prior baselines
  • Employee confidence and skill self-ratings over time
  • Time freed by AI and how teams reinvest it
  • Voluntary attrition and engagement after the policy change

Lessons for leaders and investors

Clear expectations can move an organization faster than optional pilots, but expectations need support and measurement. Leaders adopting a similar approach can provide structured learning, set guardrails for sensitive data, and measure outcomes such as quality, speed and customer impact. Investors evaluating AI strategies can ask how companies distinguish real productivity gains from activity, and how they protect the judgment and expertise that AI depends on.

The memo is also a reminder that leadership behavior is the strongest prompt in any organization. By stating that the expectation applies to executives as well, the company signaled that adoption is a shared norm rather than a demand placed only on frontline staff, a choice that tends to increase credibility and reduce resistance.

  • Pair adoption mandates with training time and practical examples.
  • Measure outcomes of AI-assisted work, not just usage counts.
  • Set clear rules for data privacy and responsible use from the start.
  • Watch engagement and retention for signs that the change feels threatening.

The public-good dimension

As employers make AI skills a job requirement, access to learning becomes a question of fairness. Workers who receive training and time to practice will adapt, while others may fall behind. Companies that invest in broad, inclusive skill building, and that are transparent about how AI affects hiring, help ensure that productivity gains are shared and that adoption strengthens rather than narrows opportunity.
