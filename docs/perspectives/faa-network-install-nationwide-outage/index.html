FAA Network Install Causes Nationwide Outage

On November 13, 2026, the FAA halted flights nationwide after its air traffic network was flooded with connection attempts. Officials pointed to a newly installed router at a remote site. The episode is a study in cutovers, technical debt and visibility.

Event: November 2026 · Organizations: Federal Aviation Administration, U.S. Department of Transportation, L3Harris Technologies, Hewlett Packard Enterprise
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Officials reported a newly installed router as the trigger, but the root cause remains under investigation and not settled.
  • Routine, repeated modernization work can still carry outsized risk when it touches an aging, tightly coupled network.
  • Manual maintenance logs slowed diagnosis, showing that visibility into recent changes is a safety capability.
  • Leaders should treat cutovers as experiments with timing, monitoring and rollback decided in advance.

What happened

On Friday, November 13, 2026, the Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop after a telecommunications outage disrupted voice and radar communications across the national airspace. According to Al Jazeera, the instability reached 17 of the FAA's 22 en route centers. CBS News reported that the stop began in mid-afternoon Eastern time and was lifted that evening after more than four hours. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said roughly 12,000 scheduled flights did not operate, and CBS News, citing FlightAware, reported more than 9,000 cancellations that day, with disruption continuing through the weekend.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a preliminary investigation pointed to work at a remote FAA site, where contractor L3Harris was replacing aging copper lines with fiber and installing a new router. Duffy said the router began flooding other routers with about 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, CBS News and FedScoop reported. An FAA FAQ cited by FedScoop identified two potential locations, El Dorado, Arkansas, and Amarillo, Texas. Officials said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. Tom's Hardware, citing Wall Street Journal reporting, said FAA staff turned off the router feature involved across the agency's Juniper routers. Accounts of the equipment differ: CBS News, citing senior government sources, reported that the problem was isolated to older Cisco routers made between 2001 and 2006.

The cause is reported, not settled. Duffy has framed the question as contractor error or faulty equipment, and Bedford asked publicly whether the problem was a faulty installation, a faulty configuration or the technology itself, according to Tom's Hardware. Hewlett Packard Enterprise, which makes Juniper routers, said its devices did not fail and appeared to work as intended. L3Harris said it is supporting the investigation in close coordination with the FAA, Al Jazeera reported. Bedford said the agency will release a root cause analysis when it is complete.

The human dimension

Whatever the final finding, the event sits where three kinds of work meet: a legacy network that must keep running, a modernization program replacing it piece by piece, and contractors performing installations in the field. Al Jazeera reported that L3Harris had completed more than 70 percent of the planned copper-to-fiber conversion and that an FAA official said a safety assessment was conducted before the work. Repeated, routine work builds justified confidence. It can also make each new site feel like the last one, even when local conditions, such as how new equipment interacts with older equipment, differ in ways no checklist anticipated.

The response shows how hard diagnosis is under pressure. Bedford said the FAA spent about 45 minutes trying to identify the source without success, and that maintenance is logged manually, which slowed the search, FedScoop reported. When responders cannot quickly see what changed, where and by whom, they must choose between continuing to hunt and acting conservatively. Bedford said he stands behind the decision to stop traffic out of an abundance of caution. Duffy said there will be a reevaluation of when this kind of work is done, a recognition that the timing of a cutover is a decision, not a detail.

The signals that mattered

The FAA's telecommunications network had been sending warnings throughout 2026. In September, FedScoop noted, a construction crew cut a fiber line in New Jersey, and the resulting outage halted flights across the Northeast. Officials have repeatedly described the underlying system as aging and underfunded. Each event had a different proximate cause, which makes it tempting to treat them separately. Read together, they describe a tightly coupled system with a thin margin for error, in which any change, whether a severed cable or a new router, can travel further than expected.

  • Time for responders to identify the most recent change during an incident
  • Share of field changes logged digitally in real time rather than manually
  • Minor instabilities observed after earlier site cutovers, tracked as near misses
  • Share of cutovers scheduled during peak traffic versus low-traffic windows
  • Behavior of new equipment tested against legacy equipment before broad rollout

Lessons for leaders and investors

Technical debt is usually discussed as a cost line. This event shows it is also a behavioral load: every manual log, workaround and mixed-generation network asks people to hold more in their heads at the moments when attention is scarcest. Change management for infrastructure is not only a matter of approvals. It means designing the handoff between the contractor who installs, the operator who monitors and the leader who decides, so that each can see what the others know.

  • Treat every cutover as an experiment, with a defined blast radius, monitoring plan and rollback trigger.
  • Make the most recent change visible to responders within seconds, not after a manual search.
  • Ask boards and investors to review incident diagnosis time, not only uptime averages.
  • Hold owners and contractors to shared, measurable handoff standards rather than assigning blame afterward.

The public-good dimension

Air travel is a public system that people rely on for work, family and medical care, and the costs of a ground stop fall unevenly, often on travelers with the least flexibility. Halting traffic when communications became unreliable placed safety ahead of schedules, which is the right order. The broader lesson for public infrastructure is that modernization is itself a period of risk, and that the public deserves a transparent, timely root cause report. Datalidy sees the same pattern in organizations replacing legacy systems: the decisive signals are often behavioral, in how changes are logged, communicated and reviewed.
