Starbucks Changes CEOs as Customer Visits Decline

Starbucks announced on August 13, 2024, that its chief executive would step down immediately and that Chipotle's CEO would take over. The decision followed quarters of declining customer transactions, offering a case study in how behavioral signals inform leadership succession.

Event: August 2024 · Organizations: Starbucks, Chipotle Mexican Grill
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Falling transaction counts, not revenue alone, were the behavioral signal that customers were changing their habits.
  • Average ticket growth can mask declining visit frequency, so boards should read both measures together.
  • Abrupt CEO transitions create uncertainty for frontline employees, who shape the customer experience every day.
  • Investors rewarded the change, but sustained value depends on rebuilding customer routines, which takes time to measure.

What happened

On August 13, 2024, Starbucks announced that Brian Niccol, then chairman and CEO of Chipotle Mexican Grill, would become its chairman and chief executive officer, starting September 9, according to the company's press release filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The company said Laxman Narasimhan had stepped down as CEO and as a board member, effective immediately, and that its chief financial officer would serve as interim CEO until the new leader arrived. The board chair moved to the role of lead independent director.

The announcement came two weeks after Starbucks reported its fiscal third-quarter 2024 results. In that release, dated July 30, the company said global comparable store sales declined 3 percent, driven by a 5 percent decline in comparable transactions, partially offset by a 2 percent increase in average ticket. In North America, comparable transactions fell 6 percent, and in China comparable store sales fell 14 percent.

The Associated Press, in a report carried by KSL, noted that investors had grown unhappy with weak sales, competition from lower-cost rivals in China and boycotts in some markets, and that Starbucks shares jumped more than 13 percent before the market opened on the day of the announcement. Starbucks' founder was quoted in the company release calling the new CEO the leader the company needed at a pivotal moment.

The human dimension

A coffee habit is one of the most studied consumer routines: a predictable time, a familiar place and a reliable reward. When transactions decline while average ticket rises, the pattern suggests that fewer visits are being made even as each visit costs more. Behavioral science would ask what changed in the cues, friction and rewards around that routine. Wait times, mobile order congestion, price perception and the feeling of the store itself can each nudge a customer to skip a visit, and once a habit is interrupted it can be hard to restore.

Leadership transitions also affect the people who deliver the experience. Store employees, whom Starbucks calls partners, are the face of any turnaround, and an abrupt change at the top can create uncertainty about priorities, staffing and standards. The company's release credited the departing CEO with work on the partner experience, supply chain and store operations. Sustaining those gains while a new leader sets direction is a quieter but critical part of any succession.

The signals that mattered

Starbucks' own reporting contained the most important signal: transactions moving in a different direction from revenue per visit. That divergence is a classic leading indicator in consumer businesses, because visit frequency tends to predict long-term revenue more reliably than price increases do. Boards can ask for these behavioral measures to be broken out by segment and daypart.

  • Comparable transactions trending down while average ticket trends up
  • Visit frequency among previously loyal, high-frequency customers
  • Order wait times and abandonment during peak periods, especially for mobile orders
  • Frontline employee turnover and engagement in stores with the steepest traffic declines
  • Regional divergence suggesting competitive or reputational pressure in specific markets

Lessons for leaders and investors

The Starbucks CEO change shows that the most actionable customer signals are behavioral, not attitudinal. Survey sentiment can lag, but visits, wait times and repeat purchases move quickly. For boards and investors, succession decisions are stronger when they are grounded in a shared view of which behaviors the next leader must change, and how progress will be measured in the first year.

  • Report transaction counts and visit frequency alongside revenue so behavior changes are visible early.
  • Define the customer and employee behaviors a new CEO is expected to shift, with measurable milestones.
  • Plan frontline communication for leadership transitions so store teams understand what will and will not change.
  • Treat early share price reactions as sentiment, and judge turnarounds on rebuilt customer routines.

The public-good dimension

Large retail brands employ hundreds of thousands of people and serve as everyday gathering places in many communities. How a company like Starbucks manages a turnaround affects wages, schedules and working conditions for its store teams, and the quality of a small daily ritual for millions of customers. Leadership that pursues growth by making the experience genuinely better for employees and customers, rather than by squeezing either group, tends to produce results that are both durable and good for the communities those stores serve.
