23andMe's Bankruptcy and What Happens to Trust When Genetic Data Is an Asset

When a consumer genetics company seeks a buyer in bankruptcy, its most valuable asset may be data that customers shared for personal reasons. The 23andMe filing shows how trust, privacy and business models intersect in health technology.

Event: March 2025 · Organizations: 23andMe, California Department of Justice
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025 to facilitate a sale, and its co-founder and chief executive resigned, Fox Business reported.
  • California's attorney general reminded residents they could ask the company to delete genetic data and destroy samples.
  • A prior breach affecting 6.9 million customers, reported by Engadget, shaped how the public interpreted the bankruptcy.
  • Health data businesses need predictive measures of trust, not only of sales, because trust governs both retention and consent.

What happened

On March 23, 2025, consumer genetic testing company 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection to facilitate a sale, the Associated Press reported, and said it would continue operating during the process. Its co-founder and chief executive resigned to pursue the company as an independent bidder after a board committee rejected earlier proposals, and the chief financial officer became interim CEO. The company said it had secured about 35 million dollars in debtor-in-possession financing.

Days earlier, California Attorney General Rob Bonta issued a consumer alert reminding residents that they could direct the company to delete their genetic data, destroy test samples and withdraw consent for research use, according to Fox Business. The company said there were no changes to the way it stores, manages or protects customer data, and its board committee chair said data privacy would be an important consideration in any transaction. Engadget reported that a 2023 breach had exposed information on 6.9 million customers, which the company later settled for 30 million dollars, and that the company had made significant layoffs in late 2024.

The human dimension

People who sent saliva samples to a genetics company did so for personal reasons: curiosity about ancestry, interest in health risks, or a desire to find relatives. Those motivations rest on trust that the data will be handled as promised. A bankruptcy sale turns an abstract privacy policy into a concrete question about who will hold the data next, and many customers reconsider their consent at that moment. A public official urging people to consider deletion is a powerful prompt that can change behavior quickly.

Inside the company, leaders faced the dual challenge of reassuring customers while pursuing a sale under financial pressure, after layoffs and a costly breach. Communicating continuity in data protection is necessary, but statements alone rarely restore trust once it has been shaken. Customers judge companies by consistent behavior over time, especially when the information involved is as personal as a genome.

The signals that mattered

Trust erodes gradually and then becomes visible all at once. Consumer health companies can track behavioral signals that reveal shifts in trust long before they appear in revenue.

Each of these indicators reflects a decision customers make about their relationship with a company. Taken together, they form an early picture of trust that revenue figures cannot provide, because people often withdraw consent or stop engaging long before they stop paying or start complaining publicly.

  • Rate of data deletion and research consent withdrawal requests
  • Repeat engagement after the initial test or purchase
  • Customer sentiment after security incidents and their remediation
  • Share of revenue dependent on one-time purchases versus ongoing services
  • Public and regulatory attention to data practices in the sector

Lessons for leaders and investors

For investors in consumer health and data businesses, diligence should test whether the business model depends on a single purchase and whether customer trust is measured as rigorously as revenue. Data treated as an asset may lose value quickly if customers withdraw consent, so retention and consent behavior are predictive KPIs. Leaders can design privacy choices that are clear and easy to use, which tends to build durable trust rather than undermining growth.

Founders building in digital health can take a practical lesson from this case. Trust is not a marketing message. It is the accumulated result of how a company handles data, communicates during difficulty and honors customer choices, and it is far easier to sustain than to rebuild after a breach or a public warning.

  • Measure consent and deletion behavior as leading indicators of business health.
  • Stress-test how customers would respond to a sale or ownership change.
  • Treat breach remediation as a trust rebuilding program, not a legal event only.
  • Align data monetization plans with what customers believed they agreed to.

The public-good dimension

Genetic information is unusually personal and shared with relatives who never consented. Clear rights to deletion, accessible explanations of what happens to data in a sale, and strong security serve the public interest and preserve willingness to participate in research that can advance health. When health technology companies honor the trust people place in them, they protect both their customers and the scientific value that responsible data sharing can create.
