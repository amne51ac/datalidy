The UAW Strikes All Three Detroit Automakers at Once

On September 15, 2023, about 13,000 UAW members walked out at three plants, the first time the union struck all three Detroit automakers at once. The targeted "stand-up" strike expanded over six weeks before contracts with 25 percent base wage increases were ratified.

Event: September 2023 · Organizations: United Auto Workers, General Motors, Ford, Stellantis
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Workers' sense of fairness, shaped by past concessions and company profits, drove the dispute as much as specific wage figures.
  • The targeted strike strategy created uncertainty for management, showing how labor tactics shape decision-making under pressure.
  • Close ratification margins at some plants signaled that frontline sentiment varied widely even after agreement.
  • Investors should monitor labor relations and workforce sentiment as material risks, especially during industry transitions.

What happened

Shortly after midnight on September 15, 2023, about 13,000 United Auto Workers members walked off the job at General Motors' plant in Wentzville, Missouri, Ford's Michigan Assembly Plant in Wayne, and a Stellantis Jeep complex in Toledo, Ohio, according to the Associated Press, as published by Fortune. It was the first time in the union's 88-year history that it struck all three companies simultaneously. NPR, as published by Michigan Public, reported that the initial walkout involved less than 9 percent of the nearly 150,000 UAW members at the three companies, under a "stand-up strike" strategy that targeted selected plants and could expand.

The AP reported that the union sought 36 percent raises over four years, along with restored cost-of-living adjustments, an end to tiered wages, restored pensions for new hires and a shorter workweek. GM and Ford had offered 20 percent and Stellantis 17.5 percent at that point. UAW President Shawn Fain said the companies were very profitable and could afford the raises. Ford chief executive Jim Farley said meeting the union's demands could have led to bankruptcy, and GM chief executive Mary Barra described GM's offer as historic, according to the AP.

The strike grew to more than 45,000 workers at its peak and lasted into a sixth week, Fox Business reported. Tentative agreements were reached in late October, and the contracts included 25 percent base wage increases through April 2028 and restored cost-of-living adjustments. GM workers ratified their agreement with about 55 percent approval, and seven of GM's 11 assembly plants voted against it, according to Fox Business.

The human dimension

The union's demands drew heavily on history. Workers sought to recover wages and benefits given up during the 2008 financial crisis, NPR reported, and the union contrasted company profits with worker pay. Perceived fairness, how people judge the distribution of gains relative to their sacrifices, is a powerful driver of behavior. When workers believe earlier concessions were never repaid, even generous offers can feel insufficient, and trust becomes harder to rebuild than wages are to raise.

The stand-up strategy also changed management's decision environment. Rather than facing a single, predictable walkout, executives had to plan for escalation at any plant at any time. That uncertainty shaped bargaining on both sides. The companies faced genuine pressure: they were investing heavily in electric vehicles while producing gas-powered models, and they worried that higher labor costs could leave them less competitive with foreign automakers' U.S. plants, the AP reported.

The signals that mattered

The dispute did not emerge suddenly. Workers' focus on past concessions, the contrast between profits and pay, and concern about jobs in the electric vehicle transition were all visible well before the contracts expired.

  • Workforce perceptions of how past concessions and recent gains have been shared
  • Sentiment differences across plants and job classifications, including tiered roles
  • Worker concerns about job security during technology transitions
  • Ratification margins as a signal of residual frustration after agreement

Lessons for leaders and investors

Labor agreements settle terms, but they do not automatically settle sentiment. The close GM vote suggests that many workers remained dissatisfied even with significant gains. Leaders who track workforce sentiment by site and role can identify where trust needs active rebuilding after a contract is signed.

For investors, the strike showed that labor relations can shape strategy during major industry transitions. Plans for electric vehicles and new battery plants depend on a workforce that trusts how the transition will be managed. Companies that understand how workers perceive fairness, job security and shared gains can plan transitions with fewer surprises. Those that rely only on wage benchmarks may find that the decisive variables were never measured.

  • Measure perceived fairness regularly, not only during contract negotiations
  • Treat plant-level ratification results as a map of where engagement work is needed
  • Involve workers early in planning for technology transitions that affect jobs
  • In diligence, assess labor relations history and sentiment as part of operational risk

The public-good dimension

Auto manufacturing supports families and communities across the industrial Midwest and beyond. Agreements that restore a sense of fairness can strengthen those communities, while prolonged conflict affects suppliers, dealers and local economies. As industries change, the way companies and workers share the costs and benefits of transition shapes public trust in that transition. Datalidy sees measured, honest attention to workforce sentiment as part of sustaining both performance and community wellbeing.
