The Optus Data Breach and the Behavior of Customer Trust

Australian telecom Optus said a cyberattack had exposed names, dates of birth, contact details and, for some customers, license and passport numbers. The breach raised questions about why so much identity data was retained and how customers learn they are at risk.

Event: September 2022 · Organizations: Optus, Singtel, Australian Cyber Security Centre, Office of the Australian Information Commissioner
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The data most valuable to attackers, such as identity document numbers, was data many customers may not have realized was still held.
  • Customers who learned of a breach from the news rather than the company experienced a second failure of trust.
  • Data minimization is a behavioral and cultural practice as much as a technical control.
  • Investors should treat retained personal data as a liability on the balance sheet of trust.

What happened

On September 22, 2022, Optus, the Australian unit of Singapore Telecommunications, disclosed that it had been the target of a cyberattack. Reuters, as published by Insurance Journal, reported that customer names, dates of birth, phone numbers and email addresses may have been exposed, along with driver's license numbers for some customers and passport numbers in a smaller number of cases. Optus said payment details and account passwords were not compromised.

Light Reading reported that up to 10 million customers could be affected, while Reuters noted an earlier newspaper report of up to 9 million and said Optus could not yet confirm the number. Optus said it had shut down the attack after discovering it and was working with the Australian Cyber Security Centre and federal police. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner said it would work with Optus to inform customers.

Chief executive Kelly Bayer Rosmarin apologized and said the company was "devastated" by the attack, according to Light Reading. A senator quoted by Light Reading described it as one of the most serious cyberattacks on an Australian business. The method of the attack and the identity of those responsible were under investigation at the time of the initial reports. Reuters reported that Optus said it was notifying customers at heightened risk as well as key financial institutions.

The human dimension

A breach is experienced by customers as a sequence of moments: learning that something happened, understanding what it means for them and knowing what to do next. Light Reading reported that customers complained on social media that they had learned of the breach from the media and had received no advice from Optus. Whatever the operational constraints of an active investigation, that gap shapes how people judge an organization's competence and care.

The breach also points to a quieter organizational behavior: retention. Identity document numbers are collected for legitimate verification purposes, but holding them long after they are needed increases what can be lost. Decisions about what to keep are rarely made in a single meeting. They accumulate through defaults, system designs and the absence of anyone accountable for deletion, which makes data minimization a cultural habit as much as a policy.

The signals that mattered

Many breach risks are visible in how an organization treats data long before an attacker arrives. Leaders can monitor these as leading indicators rather than learning of them in an incident report.

Several of these signals concern habits rather than technology. Retention defaults, unclear ownership and untested communication plans persist because no one is measured on them. Turning them into tracked indicators gives leaders a way to see risk accumulating before it is realized.

  • Volume of identity data retained beyond its original verification purpose
  • Systems exposing customer records without clear ownership or access review
  • Absence of a tested, customer-facing breach communication plan
  • Time from detection to direct customer notification in past incidents
  • Customer complaints about unclear privacy or data-handling practices

Lessons for leaders and investors

Boards should ask not only how data is protected but why it is held at all. Investors evaluating consumer businesses can treat the scale and sensitivity of retained personal data as a risk exposure that grows silently. And every organization should rehearse how it will tell customers, plainly and quickly, what happened and what they should do.

Communication deserves particular attention. In the first days of a breach, facts are incomplete and legal and investigative constraints are real. Even so, customers can be told what is known, what is not yet known, what the company is doing and what they can do now. Clarity under uncertainty is a skill organizations can rehearse.

  • Set and enforce retention limits for identity documents and sensitive fields.
  • Assign a named owner for each customer data store, including deletion.
  • Design breach notifications with customers, testing clarity and next steps in advance.
  • Include data inventory and retention practices in commercial due diligence.

The public-good dimension

When identity data is exposed, the burden of protection shifts to customers, who may need to replace documents, monitor accounts and stay alert to fraud. People with fewer resources often find that burden heaviest. Organizations that collect only what they need, delete what they no longer need and communicate clearly when things go wrong protect the public as well as their own reputation. At Datalidy, we see customer trust as a behavior that responds to every one of those choices.
