The Key Bridge Collapse: A Loose Wire, a Fast Warning and a Missed Risk Review

The early-morning collapse of Baltimore's Francis Scott Key Bridge closed a major port and took six workers' lives. The NTSB's findings point to a chain of small technical and organizational gaps, and to a fast human response that saved lives.

Event: March 2024 · Organizations: National Transportation Safety Board, Maryland Transportation Authority, Maersk
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The NTSB found a loose wire caused blackouts that left the Dali without propulsion or steering near the bridge.
  • Quick action by pilots, dispatchers and the transportation authority stopped bridge traffic, which the NTSB said prevented greater loss of life.
  • The NTSB said a recommended vulnerability assessment could have identified protective countermeasures for the bridge.
  • Risk assumptions made decades ago need routine review as the environment around an asset changes.

What happened

At about 1:29 a.m. on March 26, 2024, the containership Dali lost propulsion and steering after an electrical blackout and struck a pier of the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore, according to the National Transportation Safety Board. The bridge collapsed. Six of the seven highway maintenance workers on the bridge died, and one survived with serious injuries, the NTSB said. CNBC reported that the ship issued a mayday call shortly before the collision.

In its findings, adopted at a public meeting on November 18, 2025, the NTSB determined that a loose signal wire connection caused a breaker to open unexpectedly, starting a chain of events that produced two blackouts. The board found that wire-label banding had kept the wire from fully seating in a terminal block. NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy compared finding the single wire to locating a loose rivet on the Eiffel Tower and said the accident was preventable.

The collapse closed the channel into the Port of Baltimore. CNBC reported that Maersk, which chartered the Dali, said it would skip Baltimore for the foreseeable future, and that Maryland Gov. Wes Moore said more than 52 million tons of foreign cargo worth about $80 billion had moved through the port the previous year. Moore later called the collapse "a national economic catastrophe," according to CNBC.

The human dimension

Two human stories sit inside the NTSB's account. The first is one of speed and coordination: the NTSB said the Dali's pilots, shoreside dispatchers and the Maryland Transportation Authority acted quickly to stop traffic onto the bridge, which prevented greater loss of life. In the minutes available, people on the ship and on shore recognized the danger, communicated it and acted on it. The second is sobering: the NTSB listed the lack of effective, immediate communications to warn highway workers to evacuate as a contributing factor. The warning reached the road but not everyone on the span.

The board also pointed to a slower kind of human decision. According to the NTSB, the bridge lacked countermeasures to reduce its vulnerability to ship strikes, and a vulnerability assessment recommended by AASHTO could have identified them. The NTSB noted that ships have grown much larger since the bridge opened in 1977, and found that many bridge owners were likely unaware of their collision risk. Risk judgments made for one era quietly persisted into another.

The signals that mattered

Investigators were careful to separate the immediate mechanical cause from the conditions that turned it into a catastrophe. For leaders, both layers offer indicators that could be tracked before an event rather than reconstructed after it.

  • Age of the last formal risk assessment relative to changes in operating conditions
  • Growth in the size of vessels or loads an asset is exposed to
  • Time needed to warn every person in a hazard zone, tested through drills
  • Electrical maintenance findings involving connections and installation practices
  • Status of industry guidance adopted versus guidance acknowledged but not applied

Lessons for leaders and investors

Infrastructure owners, insurers and investors share an interest in the NTSB's central point: risk is not static. The board sent letters to 30 bridge owners about collision risk and said all had responded. Any organization that operates long-lived assets can ask the same question of its own portfolio, and can test whether its warning systems reach every person who needs them.

  • Schedule risk reassessments when the operating environment changes, not only on fixed cycles.
  • Drill emergency communications end to end, including people working in temporary or remote roles.
  • Treat small maintenance practices as potential single points of failure in critical systems.
  • Recognize and reinforce the fast, coordinated responses that limit harm.

The public-good dimension

The six workers who died were maintaining a public road in the middle of the night, work that most travelers never see. Honoring that loss means taking the NTSB's recommendations seriously across the country, from bridge risk reduction plans to motorist warning systems. It also means recognizing the people whose quick decisions that night kept the toll from being higher. At Datalidy, we view this as a reminder that resilience is a behavioral property of systems: it depends on what people notice, say and do when seconds count, and on the risk reviews that prepare them.
