Adobe and Figma End Their Merger After Regulatory Opposition

On December 18, 2023, Adobe and Figma said they would terminate their merger agreement, citing no clear path to approval in the UK and EU. The episode offers lessons on deal theses, user behavior and integration risk.

Event: December 2023 · Organizations: Adobe, Figma, UK Competition and Markets Authority, European Commission
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Regulators assessed the deal partly by how designers actually use competing tools, which makes user behavior evidence central to deal risk.
  • A long review period leaves both companies, their teams and their customers operating under uncertainty that affects behavior.
  • A large reverse termination fee prices regulatory risk into the deal, but not the human cost of prolonged limbo.
  • Investors should test whether a deal thesis depends on assumptions about competition that customer data could confirm or refute.

What happened

On December 18, 2023, Adobe and Figma announced that they had mutually agreed to terminate the merger first announced on September 15, 2022, under which Adobe would have acquired Figma for cash and stock. According to Adobe's release, the companies shared the view that there was no clear path to receive the necessary regulatory approvals from the European Commission and the UK Competition and Markets Authority.

Engadget reported that the deal was valued at 20 billion dollars and that Adobe would pay Figma a previously agreed one billion dollar termination fee. In provisional findings, the CMA said the merger would "eliminate competition between two main competitors," according to Engadget, and it had been considering remedies including blocking the deal or requiring divestment. The European Commission had sent a Statement of Objections the previous month.

Adobe said that "no realistic remedy would satisfy the concerns the CMA is maintaining," according to Engadget, and the company's chief executive said in the joint statement that both firms "strongly disagree with the recent regulatory findings" but would move forward independently. The outcome ended one of the most closely watched technology acquisitions of the period and returned both companies to operating as independent competitors in design software.

The human dimension

Competition reviews ultimately turn on behavior: which tools designers choose, how easily teams switch, and whether a dominant firm would have reason to keep innovating. Those are empirical questions about how real people work. The regulators' concerns, as reported, centered on the possibility that the deal would remove a key competitor, a question that hinges on observed product choices and development plans.

There is also an internal dimension. For roughly fifteen months, employees at both companies worked under a pending transaction. Uncertainty of this kind tends to affect retention, hiring, roadmap decisions and customer confidence. Both companies had to keep serving customers while the outcome remained open, a constraint that deserves acknowledgment rather than judgment.

The signals that mattered

Deal teams can track signals about how regulators, users and employees are likely to respond long before a final ruling. Many of these are behavioral, and some sit in data the companies already hold.

These signals are useful because they are observable during the review rather than after it. A deal team that sees heavy overlap in how customers use two products, or rising attrition at the target, has early evidence about both regulatory exposure and integration risk, and can adjust its strategy, communication or valuation accordingly.

  • Overlap in active users and teams who use both products for the same tasks
  • Customer switching behavior between the acquirer's and target's competing tools
  • Tone and specificity of early regulatory questions and published issue statements
  • Voluntary attrition and offer acceptance rates at the target during the review
  • Community and customer sentiment about the deal in user forums and support channels

Lessons for leaders and investors

For acquirers and investors, the lesson is that a deal thesis rests on assumptions about customer behavior that can be examined in advance. Commercial due diligence that reads how customers actually use competing products can surface the same evidence a regulator will weigh. Leaders also need a plan for the people side of a long review, including how to keep teams engaged when the future is unclear.

Founders of acquired or pending targets face a parallel challenge. Their teams take cues from leadership about whether to keep building at full speed. Clear, honest internal communication about what is known and unknown helps people keep doing their best work, whatever the outcome.

  • Test competition assumptions against real usage and switching data before signing.
  • Plan for employee retention and engagement through extended regulatory reviews.
  • Communicate with customers consistently while ownership remains uncertain.
  • Treat termination fees as financial protection, not protection against cultural drift.

The public-good dimension

Competition oversight exists to protect the choices of the people who use products, in this case designers, students and small teams who depend on affordable, capable tools. Regardless of one's view of the outcome, the case shows that evidence about real user behavior sits at the center of how markets are judged. Organizations like Datalidy that study behavior in data see that as a reason to measure customer choice carefully and honestly.
