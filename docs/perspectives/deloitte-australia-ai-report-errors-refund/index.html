Deloitte's Partial Refund Over AI Errors Raises the Bar for Human Review

A government-commissioned review contained citations to research that did not exist and a fabricated court quote. The case is less about the technology than about the review habits that should surround it.

Talk to us

Event: October 2025 · Organizations: Deloitte Australia, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • AP reported the report, commissioned for about A$440,000, contained a fabricated court quote and references to nonexistent research.
  • A revised version disclosed use of a generative AI model, and Deloitte agreed to repay the final contract installment.
  • The department said the report's substance and recommendations were unchanged, though the errors damaged confidence.
  • Human review of AI-assisted work is a behavior that can be designed, measured and audited.

What happened

In early October 2025, Deloitte Australia agreed to partially refund the Australian government for a report containing apparent AI-generated errors, the Associated Press reported. The 237-page review, prepared for the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations and first published in July, examined IT systems used to automate penalties in the welfare system. AP reported the government paid about A$440,000 for it.

A University of Sydney researcher of health and welfare law told AP he found up to 20 errors in the first version, including a fabricated quote from a federal court judgment and references to nonexistent reports and books. A revised version removed those items and added a disclosure that Azure OpenAI, a generative AI system, was used in writing the report. The department said Deloitte "confirmed some footnotes and references were incorrect" and had agreed to repay the final installment under its contract.

The department said the "substance" of the report was maintained and its recommendations were unchanged, according to AP. Deloitte said the "matter has been resolved directly with the client." CFO Dive later reported that the refund was over A$97,000. An Australian senator told the Australian Broadcasting Corp. that the full amount should be refunded. AP reported that Deloitte did not respond when asked whether the errors were generated by AI.

The human dimension

Generative AI produces fluent text that looks finished, which changes how people review it. Reviewers tend to check whether writing sounds right rather than whether each claim is supported, especially under deadline pressure. Behavioral science calls this automation bias: people over-trust output from a system that is usually reliable. Citations are particularly vulnerable because they look like evidence, and checking them requires effort that a polished document seems not to need.

Accountability structures matter too. In professional services, work passes through several hands before it reaches the client, and each reviewer may assume someone else verified the sources. An accounting professor quoted by CFO Dive said AI output "must be reviewed as if it were prepared by an intern or new hire." That framing turns review from a formality into a defined responsibility with a clear owner.

The signals that mattered

Organizations adopting AI in research and advisory work can measure whether review behaviors keep pace with use. These indicators are simple to collect and reveal risk before a client or the public finds it.

Disclosure is itself a behavioral signal. When teams expect to tell clients how AI was used, they tend to document and check that use more carefully. When AI use is informal and undisclosed, review practices tend to vary by individual rather than by standard.

  • Share of AI-assisted deliverables with documented citation verification
  • Rate of reference errors found in internal quality review samples
  • Whether AI use is disclosed to clients at the start of an engagement
  • Time allotted for verification relative to drafting time on AI-assisted work

Lessons for leaders and investors

The productivity gains from AI are real, and many firms are right to pursue them. The Deloitte episode shows that the gains depend on review practices designed for how AI fails. For investors in professional services and knowledge businesses, AI governance is now part of quality and reputational risk.

Buyers of advisory services have a role as well. Clients can ask at the outset how AI will be used, who will verify sources, and how errors will be handled. Contracts that specify these behaviors make quality expectations explicit for both sides.

  • Assign a named owner to verify every factual claim and citation in AI-assisted work.
  • Set disclosure norms for AI use with clients and regulators before engagements begin.
  • Audit samples of AI-assisted deliverables and report error rates to leadership.
  • Include AI quality controls in diligence on professional services firms.

The public-good dimension

This report concerned automated penalties in a welfare system, an area where policy decisions affect people with limited means. Public trust in evidence used by government depends on that evidence being real. Datalidy's perspective is that responsible AI adoption is a behavioral design problem: the goal is to make careful verification the easy, expected habit, so that AI expands capacity without eroding the accuracy the public depends on.
