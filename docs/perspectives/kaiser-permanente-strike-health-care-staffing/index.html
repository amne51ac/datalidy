Kaiser Permanente Workers Stage a Historic Health Care Strike

On October 4, 2023, more than 75,000 Kaiser Permanente workers began a three-day strike over staffing and wages. The dispute showed how workforce strain, and disagreement about how to measure it, can become a defining issue for a health system.

Event: October 2023 · Organizations: Kaiser Permanente, Coalition of Kaiser Permanente Unions, SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • Workers and management described staffing through different measures, showing why shared, trusted workforce metrics matter.
  • Frontline perceptions of understaffing can diverge from system-wide turnover figures, and both deserve attention.
  • Clinical workforce strain is a leading indicator of patient experience, access and quality concerns.
  • Investors in health care should evaluate workforce sentiment and staffing data with the same rigor as financial performance.

What happened

On October 4, 2023, more than 75,000 Kaiser Permanente workers began a strike at hospitals and medical offices in California, Colorado, Oregon, Virginia, Washington and Washington, D.C., according to CBS News, which described it as the largest walkout by health care workers in U.S. history. The strike was scheduled to last three days and involved nurses, lab technicians, pharmacists and other staff. CBS reported it could affect nearly 13 million people by curtailing nonessential care such as routine checkups.

The Coalition of Kaiser Permanente Unions, whose contract expired September 30, argued that chronic understaffing was harming patients and staff, according to Chief Healthcare Executive. CBS reported that the coalition sought a $25 hourly minimum wage plus annual raises, while Kaiser offered a $25 minimum in California and $23 in other states. Kaiser said talks "unfortunately ended without a settlement," though the sides had reached "a number of tentative agreements." Chief Healthcare Executive reported that both sides had made progress on protections against outsourcing.

Kaiser kept hospitals and emergency departments open, staffed by physicians, managers and contingent workers, CBS reported. The organization said it was doing its best in an industry facing worker shortages. In response to union claims about staff departures, Kaiser said the average coalition-represented employee stays nearly 11 years and that turnover was 7 percent, below the industry average, according to Chief Healthcare Executive.

The human dimension

At the heart of the dispute was a disagreement about what staffing data meant. Workers described the daily experience of doing more with fewer people, while Kaiser pointed to tenure and turnover figures that compared favorably with the industry. Both can be true at once. Average turnover can look stable while specific units, roles or shifts are under acute strain, and system-level averages rarely capture how stretched people feel during a given shift.

Health care work carries unusual emotional and physical demands. When staff believe they cannot provide care the way patients deserve, the resulting moral distress can affect engagement, retention and safety. Kaiser faced real constraints, including industry-wide shortages and the need to keep care affordable. The workers faced their own constraints, including fatigue and rising living costs. A walkout of this scale reflects a gap that had grown over time between how the work felt and how it was measured.

The signals that mattered

The issues behind the strike were long-running and widely discussed across the industry. The signals that best predict this kind of conflict sit at the unit and role level, not in system-wide averages.

  • Vacancy and overtime rates by unit, role and shift rather than system-wide averages
  • Frontline reports of delayed or deferred care attributed to staffing
  • Burnout and intent-to-leave measures from validated, regularly repeated surveys
  • Use of contingent or temporary staff as a share of scheduled hours

Lessons for leaders and investors

When labor and management cite different numbers, the underlying problem is often measurement, not just money. Health system leaders who build workforce metrics that frontline staff recognize as accurate create a shared basis for decisions and a better chance of resolving disputes before they escalate.

For investors in health care services, the strike highlights that workforce data is not a back-office concern. Staffing strain affects access, patient experience and reputation, and disputes over its measurement can escalate into large-scale disruption. Organizations that pair system-wide turnover figures with unit-level strain indicators and validated surveys of staff wellbeing gain earlier warning, and they give boards a more accurate picture of operating risk than any single average can provide.

  • Develop staffing metrics with frontline input so both sides trust the data
  • Report workforce strain by unit and role to boards, not only system averages
  • Link workforce indicators to patient access and experience measures
  • In diligence, examine staffing models and clinician sentiment as drivers of quality and risk

The public-good dimension

Kaiser Permanente serves millions of members, and the wellbeing of its workforce is inseparable from the care those members receive. Sustainable staffing protects patients, supports clinicians and strengthens public trust in health care. Measuring workforce strain honestly and early is a public health priority, not just an employment matter. Datalidy sees clinician and staff wellbeing as one of the clearest places where better measurement serves both performance and the public good.
