FTC Bans Rite Aid From Facial Recognition After Shoppers Were Misidentified

A five-year ban on facial recognition at Rite Aid turned an abstract debate about biased algorithms into a concrete question for every retailer: who checks the machine, and what happens to customers when nobody does?

Talk to us

Event: December 2023 · Organizations: Rite Aid, Federal Trade Commission
This analysis examines the event as it unfolded, based on the reporting cited below. Later developments may not be reflected.

Key takeaways

  • The FTC alleged Rite Aid's system produced thousands of false matches that led staff to confront innocent shoppers.
  • The agency's complaint centers on missing safeguards: no accuracy testing, poor images, no monitoring and inadequate employee training.
  • An AI alert becomes a human action at the store counter, so frontline judgment and training are part of the system.
  • False-positive rates and their distribution across communities are leading indicators that boards should require before deployment.

What happened

On December 19, 2023, the Federal Trade Commission announced a proposed order that bans Rite Aid from using facial recognition technology for surveillance purposes for five years. According to the FTC, the drugstore chain used AI-based facial recognition from 2012 to 2020 to identify customers it suspected of shoplifting or other problematic behavior, and the system generated thousands of false-positive matches. CNBC reported that the technology was used in hundreds of pharmacies across several states.

The FTC said employees acted on those alerts by following customers, searching them, ordering them to leave, calling the police and publicly accusing them, sometimes in front of friends or family. The agency said false positives were more likely in plurality-Black and Asian communities. Its complaint also alleged that Rite Aid violated a 2010 data security order by failing to oversee its service providers adequately.

Rite Aid told CNBC it disagreed with the allegations, described the effort as a pilot program in a limited number of stores, and said it stopped using the technology more than three years earlier, before the FTC investigation began. Because Rite Aid had filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October 2023, CNBC noted, the settlement required court approval. Samuel Levine, director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, said the company's "reckless use of facial surveillance systems left its customers facing humiliation and other harms."

The human dimension

The FTC's account reads less like a story about a flawed algorithm than a story about the human system around it. According to the agency, Rite Aid did not test the technology's accuracy before deployment, did not ask its vendors about their testing, allowed low-quality images into a database of tens of thousands of photos drawn from security cameras, employee phones and news stories, and did not track false-positive rates. Each of those gaps is a decision, or a missing decision, made by people.

The most consequential moment came at the store level. The FTC said employees were not adequately trained or warned that matches could be wrong, and that even after a "bad match" reporting tool was introduced, the company did not ensure staff used it. When an alert arrives with the authority of technology and no guidance about uncertainty, people tend to treat it as fact. Automation bias is a well-documented behavioral pattern, and here it reached customers as public accusations. The FTC also said employees were discouraged from telling customers the technology was in use, which removed a natural check from the people most affected.

The signals that mattered

The relevant indicators were measurable long before any regulator arrived. A system that produces alerts can also produce data about how often those alerts are wrong, where, and what staff do next. According to the FTC, those measures were not being collected, which meant leaders lacked the very evidence that would have shown whether the tool was working.

  • False-positive rate per store, tracked over time and reviewed by leadership
  • Differences in match and error rates across neighborhoods and customer groups
  • Share of alerts escalated to confrontations, bans or police calls
  • Usage rate of the bad-match reporting tool by frontline staff
  • Image quality standards and audit results for the enrollment database

Lessons for leaders and investors

For boards and investors, the case shows that AI risk is operational risk. The FTC's proposed order requires deletion of images and of algorithms developed from them, consumer notice, and a data security program overseen by senior executives with annual CEO certification. Remediation of that kind is expensive, and it lands on companies that cannot show they measured what their systems were doing to customers. Diligence on any AI deployment that touches customers should ask how accuracy was tested, how errors are detected, and how frontline staff are trained to respond to uncertain outputs.

  • Require pre-deployment accuracy testing, including testing across the populations the system will serve.
  • Treat frontline training and escalation protocols as part of the technology, not an afterthought.
  • Make error rates a standing board-level metric for any customer-facing AI system.
  • In diligence, ask for vendor testing evidence and monitoring data, not only feature descriptions.

The public-good dimension

Pharmacies are places people visit when they are unwell or caring for someone who is, and being wrongly accused there carries real costs to dignity and trust. The FTC's emphasis on notice, complaint handling and deletion reflects a principle that applies well beyond retail: people affected by automated decisions deserve to know about them and to have a way to correct them. At Datalidy, we see measurement as the first ethical safeguard, because a system that is not measured cannot be held accountable to the people it affects.
