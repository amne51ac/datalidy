Philanthropy Advisory

Our philanthropy advisory helps foundations, family offices, donor-advised fund holders and corporate funders clarify their goals, design grantmaking strategies and practices that respect grantees, and learn from their giving over time.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Philanthropic resources are significant, but strategies are often shaped by founders' early interests, board preferences or the accumulation of legacy grants. Funders may spread grants thinly across many issues, impose application and reporting requirements out of proportion to grant size, or change priorities abruptly. Families face additional complexity when generations hold different values, while corporate foundations balance community goals with business expectations. Without clarity of purpose and approach, philanthropy can produce goodwill without the sustained change donors hope to achieve.

Power dynamics shape every grantmaking relationship. Grantees often feel unable to speak candidly about challenges for fear of losing funding, so funders receive optimistic reports rather than useful information. Restricted, short-term grants make it difficult for nonprofits to invest in staff, systems and resilience. Communities affected by funding decisions are rarely involved in making them. Thoughtful philanthropy advisory addresses not only what to fund but how to fund: the practices, relationships and accountability that determine whether giving strengthens or strains the field.

Our approach

How we deliver philanthropy advisory.

  1. 01Listen

    Clarifying values and hearing grantees

    We interview trustees, family members, staff and leaders to clarify values, goals, time horizon and appetite for risk. We also gather candid feedback from current and former grantees, through anonymous surveys and third-party interviews, and from community leaders, so the strategy reflects how the funder's practices are actually experienced in the field.

  2. 02Measure

    Reviewing the grant portfolio

    We analyze the grant portfolio by issue, geography, grant size, duration, restriction and grantee characteristics, and assess what is known about results. We compare the funder's application, reporting and payment practices against its grant sizes, and estimate the burden placed on grantees, which often reveals easy opportunities to reduce friction and redirect staff time to relationships.

  3. 03Design

    Designing strategy and practice

    We help the board choose focus areas, a theory of change for each and a grantmaking approach: general operating or project support, multiyear commitments, capacity building, participatory grantmaking, program-related investments or policy engagement. We also design grant practices consistent with trust-based philanthropy principles, such as streamlined applications, flexible funding and reporting that serves learning.

  4. 04Deliver

    Transitioning and launching

    We support the transition from current to future portfolio, including responsible exit plans with adequate notice and tapering for grantees who fall outside new priorities. We help revise guidelines, update grants management workflows, design community advisory structures where appropriate and prepare staff and trustees for new roles in relationship-based grantmaking.

  5. 05Sustain

    Learning and accountability

    We set up a learning framework that combines grantee feedback, outcome evidence and periodic strategy reviews, with indicators the board can track without imposing new reporting on grantees. Regular grantee perception feedback holds the funder accountable for its own practices, and a cycle of review allows strategy to evolve as conditions and evidence change.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We advise foundation chief executives and boards, family foundations and family offices, donor-advised fund holders with significant giving, corporate foundations and community foundations. Typical triggers include a generational transition in a family foundation, a significant new gift or liquidity event, a new foundation president, a decision to spend down or extend the foundation's life, grantee feedback revealing problematic practices, or a board desire to concentrate resources for greater impact and better learning.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Philanthropic practices shape the health of the nonprofit sector and the communities it serves. Flexible, multiyear funding lets grantees invest in staff, fair compensation and resilience. Streamlined processes return time to mission delivery. Including community voices in grantmaking shifts some decision-making power to the people most affected. And funders who learn honestly from grantees make better decisions, so each philanthropic dollar contributes more fully to lasting social good.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is trust-based philanthropy?

Trust-based philanthropy is an approach to grantmaking that seeks to address power imbalances between funders and grantees. Its commonly cited practices include providing multiyear unrestricted funding, doing the homework so grantees do not have to, simplifying paperwork, being transparent and responsive, soliciting and acting on feedback, and offering support beyond the check. It does not mean abandoning accountability; it means building accountability through relationships and learning.

Should a foundation provide general operating support or project grants?

General operating support gives grantees flexibility to invest where they see the greatest need, including staff, systems and reserves, and is generally more aligned with nonprofit sustainability. Project grants can suit time-limited initiatives or new programs with clear boundaries. Many funders use a mix. The right balance depends on the funder's goals, relationships and confidence in grantees' leadership and strategy.

What is participatory grantmaking?

Participatory grantmaking shifts some or all funding decisions to people affected by the issues being funded, such as community residents, people with lived experience or grantee peers. Models range from community advisory committees that inform staff recommendations to panels that make final decisions. It requires careful design around eligibility, compensation, conflicts of interest and support, but it can improve both decisions and legitimacy.

How can a family foundation manage different generations' priorities?

We start by listening to each generation separately and together, identifying shared values and areas of difference. Options include a shared core strategy with discretionary grants for individual family members, next-generation committees, or distinct focus areas led by different branches. Clear governance, documented donor intent where relevant and facilitated decision-making help families sustain both the foundation and their relationships.
