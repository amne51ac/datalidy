Process Improvement

Our process improvement consulting combines lean methods, value stream mapping and frontline co-design to reduce delay, rework and cost, while making work simpler for the employees who do it and the customers it serves.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Most organizations carry processes that grew by exception. A control was added after an error, an approval was inserted after a dispute, a workaround became standard practice when a system could not handle a common case. Each change made sense at the time, but together they create long lead times, multiple handoffs and high rework. Customers wait, costs rise and capacity is consumed by checking, chasing and correcting rather than by the work customers actually value.

Employees experience this as frustration. They spend time hunting for information, re-entering data across systems and resolving problems created upstream, often without authority to fix the root cause. Improvement programs that are imposed from above, focused on headcount reduction or driven by consultants who do not understand the work can deepen cynicism. Effective process improvement consulting engages the people closest to the work, focuses on flow and quality and treats freed capacity as something to redeploy thoughtfully.

Our approach

How we deliver process improvement.

  1. 01Listen

    Going to where the work happens

    We observe work directly, following requests, cases or products through the process, and interview the employees who perform each step. Customers or internal clients describe their experience and what they value. This gemba-style observation reveals the workarounds, waiting and informal knowledge that never appear in process documentation or system reports.

  2. 02Measure

    Mapping the value stream

    We build current-state value stream maps showing each step, handoff, wait time and rework loop, and calculate lead time, process time, first-pass yield and demand patterns. Failure demand, meaning work caused by earlier errors or unclear communication, is quantified. This baseline distinguishes value-adding work from waste and highlights the constraints that limit flow.

  3. 03Design

    Co-designing the future state

    In improvement workshops, cross-functional teams design a future-state process: eliminating unnecessary steps, reducing handoffs, standardizing work where variation causes errors, and redesigning roles and decision rights so issues are resolved at first contact. Automation is considered after the process is simplified, so technology does not encode existing waste.

  4. 04Deliver

    Piloting and implementing

    We run rapid improvement cycles using plan-do-study-act methods, testing changes in a pilot area before broader rollout. Updated standard work, training, visual management boards and job aids support adoption. Where capacity is released, we work with leaders to redeploy it toward customer-facing, higher-value or backlog work, with clear and honest communication to employees.

  5. 05Sustain

    Building a continuous improvement culture

    We establish daily or weekly huddles, process performance metrics and tiered escalation, and train internal improvement leads and managers in lean problem-solving. Process owners are named and accountable for performance. This builds a sustainable improvement system in which employees identify and solve problems routinely, rather than depending on periodic external projects.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Process improvement affects people on both sides of a transaction. Customers receive faster, more reliable service with fewer errors. Employees gain workflows that respect their time and expertise, and a voice in how work is designed. When improvement is used to redeploy capacity rather than only to cut cost, organizations can expand services, reduce burnout and invest in growth, creating value for employees, customers and communities that extends beyond the financial results.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is value stream mapping?

Value stream mapping is a lean technique for visualizing every step required to deliver a product or service, from request to completion, including the flow of information and materials. It records process times, wait times, handoffs and rework, distinguishing value-adding activity from waste. Teams use the current-state map to identify problems and design a future-state map that improves flow.

What is the difference between lean and Six Sigma?

Lean focuses on improving flow and eliminating waste, such as waiting, rework, unnecessary movement and overprocessing. Six Sigma focuses on reducing variation and defects using statistical methods. Many organizations combine them. We select methods based on the problem: lean for flow and handoff issues, statistical analysis where variation and quality defects are the main concern, and service design methods where customer experience is central.

Will process improvement lead to job cuts?

Not necessarily, and we recommend that leaders decide and communicate their intentions early. Many organizations redeploy released capacity to backlogs, growth, customer service or new capabilities. Where workforce reductions are being considered, honesty matters, because employees will not share improvement ideas if they believe doing so threatens their colleagues. Clear commitments about how capacity will be used are essential to engagement.

Should we automate processes before or after improving them?

Generally after. Automating a poorly designed process can make waste faster and harder to change. We first simplify and standardize the process, remove unnecessary steps and clarify decision rules. Then we identify which remaining steps are well suited to automation or AI support, and design how employees will work alongside those tools, including exception handling and oversight.
