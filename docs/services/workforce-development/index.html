Workforce Development Programs

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Employers report persistent difficulty filling skilled roles, while many workers remain in low-wage jobs with few routes to advancement. Workforce programs exist to close that gap, but many are designed from the supply side: a training provider builds a curriculum, enrolls participants and hopes employers will hire graduates. Without employer commitment to hiring, aligned credentials and realistic job quality, graduates struggle to find work that uses their new skills, and employers continue to see programs as disconnected from their needs.

The people barriers are equally real. Participants juggle training with work, caregiving, transportation and financial pressure, and many drop out for reasons that have nothing to do with ability. Employers' hiring practices may screen out qualified candidates through degree requirements or rigid scheduling. Supervisors may be unprepared to support new hires. Effective workforce development consulting addresses the full system: employer demand, training design, wraparound supports, hiring practices and the job quality that determines whether people stay and advance.

Our approach

How we deliver workforce development programs.

  1. 01Listen

    Understanding employers and job seekers

    We interview employers about specific skill needs, hiring practices and retention challenges, and conduct research with current and prospective participants about their goals and barriers. Conversations with training providers, community organizations and workforce boards reveal the existing ecosystem, where referrals break down and which supports participants rely on to complete training and keep their jobs.

  2. 02Measure

    Analyzing demand, outcomes and job quality

    We analyze labor market data for target occupations, assess wages, benefits, scheduling and advancement potential, and review existing program data on enrollment, completion, placement, retention and earnings, disaggregated to reveal equity gaps. A skills analysis compares what employers require with what current credentials and curricula actually teach.

  3. 03Design

    Building the sector partnership and pathway

    We co-design an employer-led sector partnership with a shared governance model, then design career pathways with stackable credentials, work-based learning such as apprenticeships, and wraparound supports for transportation, childcare and income. Employers commit to skills-based hiring, supervisor preparation and clear advancement steps, so training leads to quality jobs and progression.

  4. 04Deliver

    Launching and supporting implementation

    We support the launch of pilot cohorts, employer onboarding, partner agreements, data sharing arrangements and participant recruitment through trusted community channels. Supervisors and mentors receive training to support new hires. We also help prepare funding proposals and braided funding plans that combine public workforce, philanthropic and employer resources to sustain the program.

  5. 05Sustain

    Tracking outcomes and improving

    We establish shared outcome measures across partners, focused on placement, retention, wage growth and advancement, with dashboards that inform regular partnership meetings. Participant feedback loops identify barriers early. A continuous improvement process helps the partnership adapt curricula and supports as employer needs change and evidence accumulates about which elements work.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We work with employer human resources and talent acquisition leaders, workforce development boards, community colleges, training intermediaries, economic development agencies and foundations that fund economic mobility. Engagements often begin with a new public or philanthropic funding opportunity, an employer facing persistent hiring shortages, a regional economic development project, a program with strong enrollment but weak placement or retention outcomes, or a coalition seeking to formalize an informal partnership into a governed sector strategy.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Effective workforce development changes lives and local economies. Participants gain skills, credentials and access to jobs with better wages, benefits and advancement potential. Employers gain a pipeline of prepared workers and supervisors who can retain them. Communities benefit from greater economic mobility and reduced barriers for residents who have been excluded from good jobs. And because programs are designed with employers and participants, public and philanthropic investment is more likely to produce lasting results.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is a sector partnership in workforce development?

A sector partnership brings multiple employers within an industry together with training providers, workforce boards, community organizations and sometimes labor organizations to address shared talent needs. Employers lead in defining skills and committing to hiring, while partners coordinate training, recruitment and support services. Sector strategies are widely regarded in workforce research as one of the more promising approaches for improving both earnings and employer outcomes.

How do you measure the success of a workforce development program?

We focus on outcomes rather than outputs. Enrollment and completion matter, but the critical measures are placement in quality jobs, retention over time, wage growth and advancement. We disaggregate results by demographic group to reveal equity gaps, combine program data with employer and participant feedback, and track job quality indicators such as benefits and scheduling stability.

What is skills-based hiring?

Skills-based hiring evaluates candidates on demonstrated abilities rather than proxies such as degrees or years of experience. It can widen talent pools and improve equity, but it requires redesigning job descriptions, assessments and interview processes, and training hiring managers to apply them consistently. We help employers identify which roles are suitable and build the practices that make skills-based hiring work in practice, not only in policy.

How are workforce development programs funded?

Most successful programs braid several funding sources: public workforce funds, education and apprenticeship funding, philanthropic grants and employer contributions such as wages during training, equipment, instructors or tuition support. Each source carries different eligibility and reporting requirements. We help partnerships design a funding plan that covers core coordination costs, avoids dependence on any single source and aligns reporting across funders.
