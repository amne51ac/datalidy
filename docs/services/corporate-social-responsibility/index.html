Corporate Social Responsibility

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Many corporate social responsibility programs are a collection of sponsorships, matching gifts, volunteer days and causes selected by different executives over many years. They generate goodwill but rarely produce measurable outcomes, and they are often disconnected from how the company actually affects people through its products, workforce, supply chain and footprint. As expectations from investors, regulators and employees rise, companies find that philanthropy alone no longer answers the question of whether the business itself is a responsible actor.

Employees notice the gap. Many want their employer to stand for something, yet they see CSR announcements that seem unrelated to their daily experience of pay, scheduling, safety or inclusion. Community partners notice when funding priorities shift with each leadership change. Communications teams worry about claims that cannot be substantiated. Corporate social responsibility consulting has to connect the external program with internal practice, so the company's commitments are consistent, credible and rooted in what the business does every day.

Our approach

How we deliver corporate social responsibility.

  1. 01Listen

    Hearing employees and communities

    We gather perspectives from employees across levels and locations, community partners, customers and leaders through interviews, focus groups and short surveys. We ask how people experience the company's role in their lives and communities, what they value in current programs and where they see inconsistencies between stated commitments and actual business practice.

  2. 02Measure

    Auditing the current footprint

    We inventory all current CSR activity, including giving, volunteering, in-kind support, sponsorships, supplier diversity and community investment, and map it against the company's most significant social impacts through operations, workforce and value chain. A peer and expectation scan identifies where your commitments trail stakeholder expectations or where your claims may be difficult to substantiate.

  3. 03Design

    Building a focused framework

    We help leadership choose a small number of focus areas where business capabilities, employee passion and community need intersect, and define how giving, volunteering, procurement and core practices will reinforce each other. Each focus area receives a theory of change, community partners selected through clear criteria and a governance model that survives executive turnover.

  4. 04Deliver

    Launching with employees

    We support the rollout of the refreshed program, including grant guidelines, skills-based volunteering design, employee resource group involvement, partner transitions handled with appropriate notice and internal communications. Managers receive talking points and tools so the program becomes part of how teams work, not an add-on announced once and then forgotten.

  5. 05Sustain

    Measuring and reporting credibly

    We develop outcome indicators for each focus area, simple partner reporting that does not burden small organizations, and an internal dashboard. Public reporting is aligned with relevant sustainability disclosure expectations and reviewed for substantiation, so the company can describe its contribution accurately and avoid overstated claims that damage trust.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Responsible business starts inside the company. Aligning CSR with core practices means employees benefit from commitments that reach their own pay, safety, development and inclusion. Communities gain partners who stay committed and contribute skills as well as funding. Customers can trust that the company's claims reflect reality. And a CSR program built around genuine business strengths tends to deliver more social good per dollar while strengthening the company's reputation and ability to attract talent.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is the difference between CSR and ESG?

Corporate social responsibility generally refers to a company's voluntary commitments to society, including philanthropy, volunteering and ethical practices. ESG refers to environmental, social and governance factors that investors and regulators use to assess risk, performance and disclosure. The two overlap substantially. A well-designed CSR strategy supports strong performance on the social dimension of ESG, while ESG frameworks add rigor to how CSR outcomes are measured and reported.

How do we choose CSR focus areas?

The strongest focus areas sit where three things meet: a significant community need, a distinctive company capability such as expertise, products or networks, and genuine employee interest. We also look at where the company's operations already affect people, positively or negatively, because commitments related to those impacts are more credible. Listening to employees and communities is essential before narrowing options.

What is skills-based volunteering?

Skills-based volunteering asks employees to contribute their professional expertise, such as finance, legal, technology, marketing or operations, to nonprofit and community partners. Done well, it addresses real capacity needs for partners and offers employees development opportunities. It requires careful scoping, partner input and time commitments that respect the partner's capacity to host volunteers, which is why we design it with partners rather than for them.

How do we avoid greenwashing or impact-washing in CSR reporting?

Report outcomes alongside activities, describe contribution rather than claiming sole credit, disclose limitations and setbacks, and keep evidence on file for every public claim. Reporting should also cover the company's material negative impacts, not only its positive programs. We conduct a substantiation review of claims before publication and align reporting with relevant disclosure standards and advertising guidance.
