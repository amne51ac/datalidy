Patient Engagement & Medication Adherence

Improving medication adherence and patient engagement starts with understanding why patients do not take medications or attend follow-up as planned. We diagnose the real barriers and design interventions that help patients follow treatment plans they agree with.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Nonadherence is rarely a single problem. Some patients forget. Others intentionally skip doses because of side effects, cost, doubts about benefit or fear of dependence. Some never fill the first prescription. Others stop when they feel better, or when symptoms they expected never appear. Complex regimens, low health literacy, depression, cognitive difficulties and limited support at home all play a role. Treating these different patterns with the same reminder program helps some patients and frustrates many others.

Organizations also struggle to see adherence clearly. Pharmacy refill data, claims, device data and clinical notes sit in different systems, so the early signals of disengagement, such as a late refill or a missed appointment, are noticed too late. Engagement programs often measure enrollment or message opens rather than whether patients actually take medication or follow up. Without integrated data and behavioral insight, it is difficult to know which patients need what kind of support and when.

Our approach

How we deliver patient engagement & medication adherence.

  1. 01Observe

    Listen to patients and care teams

    We interview patients, caregivers, pharmacists, nurses and physicians, and observe how treatment plans are explained and experienced. We pay close attention to unintentional barriers, such as forgetting and complexity, and intentional ones, such as beliefs about medication, side effects and cost. Patients are treated as experts in their own lives.

  2. 02Signal

    Integrate data and find early signals

    We bring together refill, claims, appointment, app and clinical data where available to identify early predictors of disengagement and distinct adherence types, from first-fill abandonment to gradual drift. These patterns become predictive KPIs that let care teams intervene before nonadherence affects health outcomes, rather than discovering it months later.

  3. 03Design

    Design support for each barrier

    We design interventions matched to adherence types: regimen simplification and habit anchoring for forgetting, motivational interviewing conversations for ambivalence, cost navigation for financial barriers, and plain-language education for misunderstanding. Messages, outreach timing and channels are tailored, and care team workflows are designed so support is realistic within existing staff capacity.

  4. 04Test

    Pilot with real patients

    We test interventions with patient groups through pilots or randomized comparisons, measuring refill behavior, follow-up attendance and patient experience. Usability testing with patients of varied ages, abilities and literacy levels ensures that tools and materials work in practice. We collaborate with clinical and compliance teams to ensure tests are appropriate and safe.

  5. 05Scale

    Operationalize adherence support

    We help organizations embed successful interventions into care team workflows, pharmacy programs or digital products, train staff in brief motivational techniques and build dashboards that flag patients at risk. We design for sustainability, so support continues reliably after launch, and we establish regular reviews to refine the program as patient populations change.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Taking medication as prescribed can be the difference between controlled and uncontrolled chronic disease, between recovery and readmission. Better adherence support improves patients' health and quality of life, eases the burden on caregivers and reduces avoidable strain on clinicians. Designing for health literacy, cost barriers and diverse needs helps reach patients who are often least well served. Ethical, autonomy-supportive design ensures patients remain partners in their care decisions.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What are the most common barriers to medication adherence?

Barriers are often grouped into unintentional and intentional types. Unintentional barriers include forgetting, complex dosing schedules, cognitive difficulties and disrupted routines. Intentional barriers involve choices shaped by side effects, cost, doubts about benefit, worries about dependence or feeling well. Health literacy, depression, social support and access to pharmacies also matter. Effective programs identify which barriers apply to which patients.

How does motivational interviewing help with adherence?

Motivational interviewing is a collaborative conversation style that helps people explore their own ambivalence and strengthen their own reasons for change. For patients uncertain about a medication, it avoids arguing or lecturing, which can increase resistance. Clinicians and pharmacists can learn brief techniques that fit into short encounters. We train care teams and design conversation guides grounded in this approach.

Do reminders and apps improve medication adherence?

Reminders can help patients whose main barrier is forgetting, especially when tied to existing routines. They do little for patients who have intentional reasons for not taking medication, such as side effects or cost. Apps often show strong initial use that fades. We help organizations target digital tools to the patients they actually help and combine them with human support where needed.

How do you handle patient data privacy?

We work within our clients' privacy, security and compliance frameworks, including applicable health privacy regulations, and follow data minimization principles. Analysis typically uses de-identified or limited data sets under appropriate agreements. We also consider privacy from the patient's perspective when designing outreach, avoiding messages that could reveal sensitive health information to others.
