Mission-Driven Growth Strategy

Our mission-driven growth strategy work helps social enterprises, B Corps, mission-led companies and earned-revenue nonprofits find growth paths where commercial success and social impact reinforce each other, and protect the mission as they scale.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Mission-driven organizations face distinctive growth challenges. Investors and lenders expect growth, yet some paths to scale, such as moving upmarket, entering new channels or cutting costs in the supply chain, can pull the organization away from the people it was founded to serve. Leaders may not have a framework for evaluating whether a growth opportunity strengthens or weakens the mission, so decisions depend on individual instinct or are deferred until a crisis forces a choice.

Growth also strains culture and people. Early employees who joined for the mission may feel that new hires, processes or investors are changing the organization's character. Founders struggle to delegate. Impact measurement that worked at small scale breaks down. Customers and community partners notice when service quality slips or priorities shift. Mission-driven growth strategy must address the economics of scale as well as the governance, culture and accountability mechanisms that keep the mission central as the organization grows.

Our approach

How we deliver mission-driven growth strategy.

  1. 01Listen

    Understanding mission and market

    We interview founders, leaders, board members, investors, employees and customers, and conduct research with the communities the organization serves. We seek to understand what the mission means in practice, which customers or beneficiaries are core, and where stakeholders see risks of mission drift, as well as what customers value and where unmet demand exists.

  2. 02Measure

    Analyzing impact and economics together

    We analyze unit economics, customer segments, channel performance and market size alongside impact data, assessing how each segment and offering contributes to both revenue and mission outcomes. An impact-economics matrix shows which activities are self-sustaining, which require subsidy and which generate surplus that can fund mission work, informing realistic choices about growth.

  3. 03Design

    Selecting growth paths

    We develop and evaluate growth options, such as new segments, products, geographies, partnerships, licensing, franchising or acquisitions, against both financial and impact criteria. Mission guardrails define what the organization will not compromise. Customers and community members test concepts, and the strategy specifies the capital requirements and investor fit needed to pursue the chosen path.

  4. 04Deliver

    Scaling with integrity

    We support implementation of priority growth initiatives, including go-to-market planning, partnership agreements, organization design and talent strategy for scaling. We help founders and leaders adapt their roles, design onboarding that conveys mission and culture to new employees, and prepare investor and board communications about how growth and impact goals will be managed together.

  5. 05Sustain

    Governing mission and growth together

    We help establish integrated performance dashboards that track impact alongside financial metrics, governance mechanisms such as mission committees or legal structures that protect purpose, and regular reviews that test whether growth is advancing the mission. These mechanisms help preserve the organization's purpose through future funding rounds, leadership changes or ownership transitions.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

When growth and mission reinforce each other, scale means more people served, not only more revenue. Customers and beneficiaries gain wider access to products and services designed with their needs in mind. Employees keep the sense of purpose that drew them to the organization. Communities benefit from enterprises that remain accountable as they grow. And investors gain businesses whose mission strengthens customer loyalty, talent attraction and resilience, supporting long-term financial returns.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is mission drift and how can growing organizations prevent it?

Mission drift occurs when an organization gradually moves away from its core purpose, often because commercial pressures, funding requirements or growth opportunities pull activity toward more profitable or easier-to-serve segments. Prevention requires explicit mission guardrails, impact metrics reviewed alongside financial metrics, governance that holds leadership accountable for both, and investors whose expectations are aligned with the mission from the start.

How do social enterprises balance profit and purpose?

The most durable social enterprises design business models where impact is integral to value creation, so serving the mission and generating revenue rely on the same activities. Where the two diverge, they make deliberate choices, such as cross-subsidizing mission work with profitable segments or seeking blended capital. Clarity about which activities sustain the organization and which require subsidy is essential to managing the balance.

Does B Corp certification or benefit corporation status help protect mission during growth?

They can help. Benefit corporation status, where available, embeds stakeholder consideration in a company's legal duties, and B Corp certification provides an external standard and assessment. Neither substitutes for strategy, culture and governance, but both can reinforce mission commitments with investors, acquirers and employees. We help leaders evaluate whether these structures fit their circumstances, alongside advice from legal counsel.

What kinds of capital suit mission-driven growth?

Options include impact-oriented equity, revenue-based financing, debt from mission-aligned lenders, recoverable grants, program-related investments and blended structures that combine philanthropic and commercial capital. The right mix depends on the business model, growth timeline and how much control the founders want to retain. Investor alignment with the mission matters as much as terms, because investor expectations shape future decisions.
