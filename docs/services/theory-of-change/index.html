Theory of Change Development

Our theory of change consulting helps organizations articulate how their activities lead to lasting outcomes, surface the assumptions that link each step, and turn that logic into a practical measurement and learning plan.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Funders, boards and government partners increasingly ask organizations to submit a theory of change, and many respond by producing a diagram for the proposal that is filed away once the grant is awarded. The diagram often lists activities on the left and aspirational impacts on the right, with arrows standing in for the hardest questions: why would this activity produce that change, for whom, under what conditions, and how would we know? Without those answers, measurement defaults to counting outputs and strategy discussions lack a common reference point.

Teams also disagree, often silently, about how change happens. Program staff, executives, evaluators and community partners carry different mental models built from different experiences. When those models are never surfaced, the organization pursues several implicit strategies at once, and evaluation findings are interpreted to suit each perspective. A useful theory of change brings those models into the open, reconciles them with evidence and with the lived experience of participants, and becomes a working tool rather than a compliance artifact.

Our approach

How we deliver theory of change development.

  1. 01Listen

    Surfacing how people believe change happens

    We interview staff, leadership, partners and program participants, asking each to describe how they believe the work creates change and what gets in the way. Participants' accounts are essential: they often reveal steps, barriers and outcomes that program designers miss, such as transportation, trust or caregiving constraints that determine whether an intervention reaches anyone.

  2. 02Measure

    Testing the logic against evidence

    We review program data, prior evaluations and relevant research to assess the strength of each causal link. Assumptions are rated by how well evidence supports them and how critical they are to the overall logic, which identifies the few uncertain, high-stakes links that deserve priority attention in measurement, evaluation and program design.

  3. 03Design

    Building the theory and logic model

    In facilitated working sessions we work backward from long-term outcomes to define preconditions, then map the pathways, activities and outputs that contribute to them. The result is a narrative theory of change, a visual pathway diagram and a companion logic model, each stating assumptions, contextual factors and the boundary of what the organization contributes.

  4. 04Deliver

    Putting the theory to work

    We translate the theory into indicators for outputs, short-term and intermediate outcomes, select data sources and methods, and align program plans, grant proposals and staff roles with the pathways. Teams learn to use the theory when making design changes, so it guides decisions about what to start, adapt or stop.

  5. 05Sustain

    Learning and revising over time

    A theory of change should change as evidence accumulates. We set up a learning agenda with priority questions, a regular review where teams compare results to expectations, and a simple version history so updates are deliberate. Where useful, we prepare the ground for external evaluation by clarifying what can be credibly measured and attributed.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We work with nonprofit executive directors and program leaders, foundation program officers and evaluation staff, public agencies designing or commissioning programs, social enterprises and corporate impact teams. Common triggers include a funder requirement for a theory of change, preparation for an external evaluation, a strategic plan or program redesign, scaling a model to new sites, or a realization that staff and partners describe the organization's purpose and methods in noticeably different ways.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

A rigorous theory of change protects the people programs are meant to serve. Participants' experiences shape the logic, so programs address the barriers they actually face. Staff gain clarity about how their daily work connects to outcomes, which supports motivation and better judgment. Funders and communities receive honest accounts of what an organization can and cannot achieve. And resources move toward approaches with stronger evidence, increasing the social good each dollar and hour can produce.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is the difference between a theory of change and a logic model?

A theory of change explains why and how a set of activities is expected to produce long-term change, including the preconditions, causal pathways, assumptions and context involved. A logic model is usually a more linear, operational summary of inputs, activities, outputs and outcomes for a specific program. We typically develop both: the theory of change for strategy and learning, the logic model for management and reporting.

What is the difference between outputs and outcomes?

Outputs are the direct products of your activities, such as people trained, sessions delivered or grants made. Outcomes are the changes that result for people or conditions, such as new skills applied, employment obtained or health improved. Outputs are within your control and easy to count; outcomes are what actually matter to participants and funders, and they are where a theory of change should focus measurement.

How long does it take to develop a theory of change?

It depends on the complexity of the program and how many stakeholders need to be involved. A single, well-established program can move relatively quickly, while an organization-wide theory spanning several programs and partners requires more time for interviews, evidence review and consensus building. We prioritize participation over speed where the theory will guide significant decisions or investment.

Can a theory of change be used for evaluation?

Yes. A theory of change gives evaluators a framework to test whether each step in the expected pathway is occurring. Theory-based evaluation approaches, such as contribution analysis, use it to assess how plausibly a program contributed to observed outcomes, which is especially useful when randomized designs are impractical or inappropriate. We design theories with that future use in mind.
