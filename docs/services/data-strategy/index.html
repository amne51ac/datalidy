The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Organizations have invested heavily in data platforms, yet many leaders still cannot get reliable answers to basic questions. How many people did we serve, and with what results? What is our true cost to serve a customer? How does employee turnover affect quality? Data lives in disconnected systems, owned by different departments, with conflicting definitions. Reports disagree, and meetings are spent debating numbers rather than decisions. New technology purchases add capability without resolving the underlying issues of ownership, quality and purpose.

For organizations whose data describes people, such as employees, patients, students, clients or residents, the stakes are higher. Privacy regulations, consent obligations and ethical concerns about bias require careful governance. Mission-driven organizations face growing pressure from funders and boards to demonstrate outcomes, often with limited budgets and staff. Data strategy consulting should not produce a long technical document that sits on a shelf. It should define what decisions data must support, what foundations are needed, how data will be governed, and how capability will grow.

Our approach

How we deliver data strategy.

  1. 01Listen

    Start with decisions and people

    We interview leaders, program managers, analysts and frontline staff to understand the decisions they make, the information they rely on, and where they lack trust in data. Where data describes clients or communities, we include their perspectives on acceptable use. We identify priority use cases that would create the most value for the organization and the people it serves.

  2. 02Measure

    Assess current data maturity

    We assess data sources, systems, integrations, quality, definitions, governance, security, skills and culture. We profile key datasets for completeness, accuracy and consistency, and review privacy and compliance practices against applicable requirements. A maturity assessment across these dimensions shows where the organization stands and which gaps most limit priority use cases.

  3. 03Design

    Design the target state and roadmap

    We design a target state covering data architecture, master data and shared definitions, governance roles such as data owners and stewards, privacy and ethics practices, analytics and reporting, and skills. We sequence a roadmap that delivers value early through specific use cases while building foundations, with costs, staffing and options for build, buy or partner decisions.

  4. 04Deliver

    Build foundations through use cases

    We help launch priority use cases alongside foundational work, such as a shared metrics layer, a data catalog, or a governance council. We support technology selection without vendor bias, help establish data stewardship roles, and write policies people can follow. Delivering visible improvements early builds the support needed for longer-term investments.

  5. 05Sustain

    Govern and build data culture

    We help establish governance forums, data quality monitoring, and processes for approving new data uses, including ethical review for sensitive data. We support data literacy programs so managers and staff can interpret and question data. A roadmap review cadence keeps the strategy current as priorities, regulations and technologies evolve.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Data about people is ultimately about people. A sound data strategy protects privacy, reduces the risk of biased or harmful uses, and ensures data is collected for clear purposes. It allows mission-driven organizations to understand who they are reaching and who they are missing, and to direct resources where they create the most good. Employees spend less time reconciling reports and more time serving people. Communities benefit when organizations can show honestly what works and improve what does not.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is a data strategy?

A data strategy is a plan for how an organization will collect, manage, govern, analyze and use data to achieve its goals. It defines the decisions and use cases data must support, the architecture and quality standards required, governance roles and policies, privacy and ethics practices, and the skills and culture needed. A good strategy includes a sequenced roadmap so investments deliver value early while foundations are built.

What is data governance and why does it matter?

Data governance is the set of roles, policies and processes that determine who is accountable for data, how it is defined, who can access it, and how quality, privacy and security are maintained. Without governance, data becomes inconsistent and untrustworthy, and sensitive data may be misused. Governance should be proportionate to the organization's size and risk, focused on the data that matters most.

Do we need a new data platform?

Not necessarily. Many data problems stem from unclear ownership, inconsistent definitions and poor processes rather than technology. New platforms help when existing systems truly cannot support priority use cases. Our data strategy consulting assesses whether technology is the binding constraint before recommending investment, and when it is, we help define requirements and evaluate options independently, without ties to any vendor.

How do nonprofits approach data strategy with limited resources?

By focusing tightly. Nonprofits benefit most from identifying a small number of priority questions, often about reach, outcomes and program quality, and building just enough data foundation to answer them reliably. Shared definitions, a single source for client data and simple governance often deliver more than expensive tools. We help nonprofits phase investments realistically and align data strategy with funder reporting requirements to reduce duplicated effort.
