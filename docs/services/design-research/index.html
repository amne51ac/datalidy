The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Organizations make consequential decisions about products, services and programs with a surprisingly thin understanding of the people they serve. Customer knowledge often lives in survey scores, sales anecdotes and the opinions of whoever spoke last in the meeting. Surveys capture what people say in response to the questions asked, not what they do or what they would never think to mention. Focus groups favor confident voices. Analytics show behavior without explaining it. The result is design that reflects internal assumptions, and expensive surprises after launch.

When organizations do commission research, it often fails to influence decisions. Studies are scoped too broadly or too late, findings arrive as long reports that are hard to act on, and samples miss the people whose needs differ most from the mainstream. Research becomes a box to check rather than an input to strategy. Effective design research services are scoped around real decisions, use methods matched to the question, include people at the margins, and translate findings into clear implications for design.

Our approach

How we deliver design research.

  1. 01Listen

    Scope research around decisions

    We start by clarifying what decisions the research must inform and what leaders already believe. We review existing data and studies, interview internal stakeholders, and write explicit research questions and hypotheses. This prevents research that confirms what is already known and focuses fieldwork on genuine uncertainty. We also agree on how findings will be used and by whom.

  2. 02Measure

    Design a sound sample and method mix

    We select methods to fit the question: contextual inquiry and observation for workflows, diary studies for experiences that unfold over time, in-depth interviews for decision-making, card sorts and tree tests for information architecture, and surveys to size patterns. We design purposive samples that include edge cases and underrepresented groups, and we document recruitment criteria so findings can be interpreted honestly.

  3. 03Design

    Conduct fieldwork and synthesize rigorously

    Our researchers conduct fieldwork in homes, workplaces, clinics, branches and community settings, with informed consent and fair compensation. We synthesize through structured coding and affinity analysis, distinguishing patterns from individual anecdotes and noting how confident we are in each finding. Outputs include insight statements, mental models, archetypes and journey frameworks that translate directly into design opportunities.

  4. 04Deliver

    Turn insight into action

    We present findings in working sessions rather than lengthy reports, helping product, service and leadership teams translate insights into design principles, opportunity areas and priorities. We share video clips and quotes, with consent, to make participants' experiences tangible. Where useful, we move directly into concept testing so that research insights are validated against early solutions.

  5. 05Sustain

    Build a research repository and practice

    We help organizations create a searchable research repository so insights accumulate rather than disappear. We set up research operations basics: consent templates, participant panels, data retention rules and ethical review steps. We also train internal teams in interviewing and synthesis so they can run lighter-weight studies on their own and know when to call for deeper work.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Design research gives people a voice in decisions that affect them, especially people who are usually absent from boardrooms and planning meetings. Done ethically, with consent, fair compensation and care for participants' privacy and wellbeing, it treats participants as experts in their own lives. Better evidence leads to products, services and programs that fit people's real circumstances, reducing wasted resources and harm. For employees, shared research builds empathy and a common understanding of the people the organization exists to serve.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is design research?

Design research is the study of people's needs, behaviors, contexts and motivations in order to inform the design of products, services, programs and policies. It relies mainly on qualitative methods such as contextual inquiry, interviews, observation and diary studies, often combined with surveys or analytics. Unlike market research, which often measures preferences and attitudes, design research focuses on understanding behavior deeply enough to design something that works.

What is contextual inquiry?

Contextual inquiry is a research method in which a researcher observes and talks with people while they perform real tasks in their own environment, such as a nurse charting at a workstation or a small business owner doing payroll. It reveals workarounds, interruptions, tools and tacit knowledge that people rarely mention in interviews. It is especially valuable for understanding complex workflows and designing services or tools that fit real conditions.

When should we use a diary study?

Diary studies are useful when the experience you want to understand unfolds over days or weeks, such as onboarding to a new job, managing a chronic condition, or using a product in daily life. Participants record experiences as they happen through short entries, photos or voice notes, which reduces recall bias. Diary studies are often paired with interviews at the start and end to explore patterns in depth.

How many participants do you need for qualitative research?

There is no single number. Qualitative research aims for depth and saturation, the point where new interviews add few new themes, rather than statistical representativeness. The right sample depends on how varied the population and experiences are. We typically design samples around meaningful segments and edge cases, and we pair qualitative work with surveys or data analysis when leaders need to know how widespread a pattern is.
