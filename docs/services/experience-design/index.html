Experience Design

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Customers rarely judge an organization by its average interaction. They remember the peaks and the endings: the call where someone finally solved the problem, the confusing letter that arrived after a loss, the first day using a new product. Yet many organizations measure experience as a single satisfaction score and invest evenly across touchpoints. Effort goes to features that are easy to build rather than moments that shape loyalty, and experiences are designed channel by channel, so the tone, information and promises customers receive do not match.

The underlying problem is often a gap in understanding. Teams rely on personas built from demographics and assumptions rather than on the needs, motivations and contexts that actually drive behavior. Emotional and cognitive load, such as anxiety during a medical decision or confusion in a financial one, goes unaddressed because it does not appear in operational data. Without that understanding, design decisions default to internal convenience, and customers experience the organization as a set of disconnected departments rather than a coherent relationship.

Our approach

How we deliver experience design.

  1. 01Listen

    Understand needs and emotions

    We use in-depth interviews, contextual observation and diary studies to understand what people are trying to accomplish, what worries them, and how they make decisions over time. We frame findings using jobs to be done, focusing on the progress people seek rather than product features. This reveals the functional, emotional and social dimensions of the experience that surveys alone cannot capture.

  2. 02Measure

    Identify the moments that matter

    We combine survey data, behavioral analytics and operational records to find the moments with the greatest influence on trust, loyalty and outcomes. Driver analysis helps distinguish the interactions that shape overall perception from those that merely generate noise. We segment results by customer group so that the experiences of less-represented customers are visible rather than averaged away.

  3. 03Design

    Design experience principles and concepts

    We translate research into behavioral archetypes, experience principles and concepts for priority moments. Archetypes based on needs and mindsets tend to be more durable than demographic personas. We sketch, prototype and test concepts with real users, from conversation guides and communications to digital flows and physical environments, iterating until the experience is clear, accessible and emotionally appropriate.

  4. 04Deliver

    Bring the experience to life

    We work with product, marketing, operations and front-line teams to implement designs consistently across channels. That includes content and tone guidance, interaction specifications, staff scripts that allow judgment, and training on the intent behind the design. We support launch with structured testing so that teams can adjust quickly based on how real customers respond.

  5. 05Sustain

    Embed experience governance

    We help set experience measures tied to the priority moments, establish a cross-functional forum that reviews them, and create a design system or playbook so new touchpoints follow the same principles. Customer feedback loops are connected to teams with the authority to act, which keeps the experience coherent as products, channels and staff change over time.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Experience design shapes how people feel about institutions that hold significant power in their lives. Clear, respectful experiences reduce stress for customers facing difficult decisions and make services easier to use for people with limited time, literacy or digital confidence. Employees benefit when experiences are designed to support their judgment rather than script it. When an organization consistently treats people well at moments of vulnerability, it builds the kind of trust that benefits communities and sustains long-term relationships.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does an experience design consultant do?

An experience design consultant researches how people interact with an organization, identifies the moments that most shape their perceptions and outcomes, and designs better experiences across digital, physical and human touchpoints. The work spans research, concept development, prototyping and implementation support. Good experience design consulting is grounded in evidence about real user needs rather than internal assumptions, and it considers how front-line staff and systems will deliver the experience.

What is the difference between personas and archetypes?

Personas usually describe fictional individuals with demographic details, such as age, job and family. Archetypes describe patterns of need, motivation and behavior that cut across demographics, such as someone who wants control versus someone who wants reassurance. Archetypes are often more useful for design because they reflect why people behave as they do and are less likely to reinforce stereotypes. We use whichever form best supports the decisions your teams need to make.

How is experience design different from service design?

The two overlap considerably. Experience design focuses on how an interaction feels and functions from the user's perspective, including emotion, content and interaction quality. Service design extends further into the backstage operations, roles and systems that deliver the experience. Many engagements combine both: experience design defines what good looks like at key moments, and service design ensures the organization can deliver it reliably.

How do you measure the value of experience design?

We define measures before design begins, tied to the specific moments being improved. These may include task completion, customer effort, trust and satisfaction at that stage, downstream behaviors such as retention or adherence, and operational measures such as repeat contacts. We also track staff experience where employees deliver the change. Comparing pilot and control groups, where feasible, gives leaders credible evidence of what changed.
