Executive Coaching

Our executive coaching helps senior leaders and founders change the specific behaviors that limit their impact, with clear contracting, honest stakeholder feedback and progress measured against outcomes the business already cares about.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Senior leaders rarely receive candid feedback. The higher they rise, the more people filter what they say, and the more a leader's blind spots are amplified across the organization. Many executives also carry habits that served them well earlier in their careers, such as deep personal involvement in every decision, fast and forceful opinions, or a preference for harmony over conflict, which now quietly slow their teams down. Without a structured way to see these patterns, leaders keep working harder at the wrong things.

Executive coaching itself is often vague. Engagements begin without clear goals, sponsors are unsure what is happening behind closed doors, and success is judged by whether the leader enjoyed the conversations. Confidentiality is poorly defined, so leaders hold back. When the engagement ends, there is little evidence of change and no plan to sustain it. Good executive coaching requires the same discipline as any serious investment: explicit contracting, defined behaviors, credible measurement and a path to lasting habit.

Our approach

How we deliver executive coaching.

  1. 01Observe

    Contracting and discovery

    We begin with a three-way contracting conversation among the leader, the sponsor and the coach, agreeing on goals, roles and what stays confidential. We then interview stakeholders, review relevant context, and where useful administer validated assessments. The aim is a clear, shared picture of how the leader is experienced by others and what the business needs from them next.

  2. 02Signal

    Focus on few behaviors

    We synthesize 360 feedback and interview themes into a small number of development priorities, usually one to three observable behaviors. We connect each to signals the organization already tracks, such as team engagement, retention, decision speed or execution against plan, so that change can be noticed by stakeholders and reflected in the data rather than only felt by the leader.

  3. 03Design

    A practical coaching plan

    Together we design a coaching plan built around real situations on the leader's calendar: upcoming board meetings, difficult conversations, hiring decisions or reorganizations. We use behavioral design principles to make new habits easy to start and to attach them to existing routines, and we prepare the leader to enlist stakeholders as allies in noticing progress.

  4. 04Test

    Practice and feedback loops

    The leader tries new behaviors in live settings and we debrief what happened. Following a stakeholder-centered coaching approach, the leader checks in regularly with key colleagues to ask for brief, forward-looking suggestions. Midpoint pulse feedback shows whether others perceive change, so we can adjust the plan rather than waiting until the end to learn what worked.

  5. 05Scale

    Sustain and extend

    We close with a final stakeholder review, a comparison against the original goals and a written sustainability plan. Where appropriate, we extend gains to the leader's team through team coaching or alignment sessions, so that better leadership behavior becomes part of how the group operates rather than something that depends on the coach being present.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We coach chief executives, founders, senior vice presidents and high-potential leaders stepping into larger roles. Typical clients lead fast-growing technology and professional services firms, health organizations, financial services companies and associations. Engagements often begin at a trigger moment: a promotion into the executive team, a founder handing operations to a new leadership layer, feedback that a strong performer is creating friction, a merger or reorganization, or a board asking a leader to develop specific capabilities before the next stage of growth.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

When an executive changes how they listen, decide and delegate, the effect reaches far beyond the leader. Employees experience clearer priorities and more respect for their judgment, which supports engagement and wellbeing. Customers and patients benefit from teams that can focus on their needs instead of navigating internal friction. In mission-driven organizations, better leadership means scarce resources go further for the communities they serve. Coaching that respects autonomy and confidentiality also models the kind of ethical influence we want leaders to practice.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is executive coaching, and how is it different from therapy or consulting?

Executive coaching is a structured, goal-focused partnership that helps a leader change specific behaviors tied to their role and the organization's needs. Unlike therapy, it does not aim to treat clinical concerns or explore personal history in depth, though our clinical background helps us recognize when a referral is appropriate. Unlike consulting, the coach does not solve the business problem for the leader; the coach builds the leader's capacity to solve it.

How do you handle confidentiality with the sponsoring organization?

Confidentiality is agreed in writing at the start. Typically, the content of coaching conversations stays between the leader and the coach, while goals, the development plan and overall progress are shared with the sponsor in three-way meetings. The leader decides what personal material, if any, is disclosed. Clear boundaries let leaders speak openly and give sponsors enough visibility to support the investment.

How do you measure whether executive coaching worked?

We define success before coaching begins. Stakeholders who work closely with the leader provide input at the start, at the midpoint and at the end, rating perceived change on the specific behaviors chosen. We also look at relevant business signals, such as team engagement or retention, while being careful not to attribute every movement to coaching alone. The combination gives an honest view of progress.

How long does an executive coaching engagement last?

Most engagements run between six and twelve months, which is usually enough time for new behaviors to be practiced, noticed by colleagues and turned into habits. Sessions are typically every two to three weeks, with brief check-ins between them around important events. Shorter, focused engagements are possible for a specific transition, such as preparing for a new role or a major board presentation.
