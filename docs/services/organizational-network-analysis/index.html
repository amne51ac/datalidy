Organizational Network Analysis

Our organizational network analysis maps how collaboration, information and influence actually move across your organization, revealing the connectors, bottlenecks and silos that formal org charts hide, so leaders can design and change with precision.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Org charts show reporting lines, but most work happens through informal networks: the colleague people ask for advice, the analyst who connects two departments, the manager everyone copies on decisions. Leaders redesign structures, launch change programs and integrate acquisitions without seeing these networks, and they are often surprised when change stalls, key knowledge walks out the door with a departing employee, or a new structure creates bottlenecks no one anticipated. Hybrid and remote work has made these networks both more important and harder to observe.

Collaboration also has costs. A small number of people often carry a disproportionate share of requests, meetings and decisions, which leads to burnout and slows the organization. Silos between functions or locations persist despite repeated calls for collaboration. Newer employees and some demographic groups may be less connected to the networks where opportunities and information flow. Organizational network analysis makes these patterns visible and measurable, providing evidence for decisions about structure, roles, change leadership and inclusion that otherwise rely on intuition.

Our approach

How we deliver organizational network analysis.

  1. 01Listen

    Define the questions and build trust

    We work with leaders to define what the analysis should inform, such as a reorganization, merger integration, change program or inclusion goal. We engage employee representatives and communicate openly about purpose, data use and confidentiality, because network data is sensitive. Interviews with a sample of employees help us design relevant survey questions and interpret results later.

  2. 02Measure

    Collect and analyze network data

    We gather relational data through short network surveys, such as who people go to for information, advice or decisions, and where appropriate through aggregated, privacy-protected collaboration metadata. We calculate measures including degree, betweenness and eigenvector centrality, network density, brokerage and cross-group ties. We identify central connectors, overloaded individuals, isolated groups and structural holes.

  3. 03Design

    Interpret patterns and design interventions

    We interpret results with leaders and, where appropriate, with the people in the network. Findings inform specific interventions: adjusting structure to reduce bottlenecks, redistributing collaborative load, building bridges between siloed teams, designing onboarding to connect new hires faster, or enlisting informal influencers as change champions. We test proposed changes against the network to anticipate effects.

  4. 04Deliver

    Support targeted change

    We help implement interventions, from role redesign and meeting practices to change networks and mentoring programs. For reorganizations and integrations we help protect critical relationships during transition. We coach managers on supporting overloaded connectors and including peripheral team members, and work with HR to address retention risks among people who hold the network together.

  5. 05Sustain

    Track network health over time

    We design follow-up network measurements to evaluate whether interventions changed collaboration patterns as intended. We help establish governance for ongoing use of network data, including clear limits on individual-level use in performance decisions, and we build internal capability to run and interpret future analyses responsibly.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Network analysis can make organizations fairer and more humane. It reveals the often invisible work of people who connect others, mentor informally and keep things running, work that is frequently unrecognized and unevenly distributed. It shows when certain groups are excluded from information and influence, giving leaders evidence to address it. Reducing collaborative overload protects wellbeing. Used with clear ethical limits, ONA helps organizations design structures that serve both employees and the customers who depend on effective collaboration.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is organizational network analysis?

Organizational network analysis is a method for mapping and measuring the relationships through which work, information and influence flow in an organization. Data typically comes from short surveys asking who people collaborate with or seek advice from, sometimes supplemented by aggregated collaboration metadata. Network measures reveal central connectors, bottlenecks, silos and isolated individuals, informing decisions about structure, change, collaboration and inclusion.

What do centrality measures tell us?

Centrality measures describe a person's or group's position in a network. Degree centrality counts direct connections. Betweenness centrality shows how often someone sits on the paths between others, indicating brokers and potential bottlenecks. Eigenvector centrality reflects connection to other well-connected people, a signal of influence. Each measure answers a different question, so we select and interpret them based on the decisions the analysis is meant to inform.

Is organizational network analysis invasive?

It can be if designed poorly. We minimize data collection, communicate transparently with employees about purpose and use, and agree in advance on governance, including limiting individual-level results to purposes such as development and support rather than performance ratings. When using collaboration metadata, we analyze patterns in aggregate and never read message content. Done this way, ONA respects privacy while producing useful insight.

How is ONA used in mergers and reorganizations?

Before a merger or reorganization, ONA identifies critical relationships, knowledge holders and informal leaders who should be retained and engaged. It shows which teams depend on each other so new structures do not sever important connections. During integration, follow-up analysis tracks whether the combined organization is forming cross-legacy ties. This evidence helps leaders reduce disruption and preserve the capabilities the deal was meant to capture.
