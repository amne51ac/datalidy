Process Mapping & Improvement

Process mapping is most valuable when it shows how work really happens, not how the policy manual says it should. We map the people, handoffs and decisions behind your processes and redesign them for performance people will sustain.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Most organizations have process documentation. Few have documentation that matches reality. Over time, workarounds accumulate, systems are added without being integrated, and informal handoffs replace formal ones. The result is delay, rework and errors that no single team owns. Leaders sense the problem in missed deadlines and frustrated customers but cannot see where it originates, because each team's view of the process ends at its own boundary. The cost is borne by staff and customers alike.

Traditional process improvement often focuses on steps and systems while overlooking behavior. Yet processes are performed by people who make judgment calls, avoid tasks they find unclear and route around steps they distrust. A redesigned process that ignores those behaviors tends to drift back to the old way within months. Effective process mapping captures both the formal flow and the human decisions within it, so improvements address the real causes of friction and are designed to be adopted, not merely announced.

Our approach

How we deliver process mapping & improvement.

  1. 01Observe

    Walk the process in context

    We conduct contextual inquiry with the people who perform the work, observing tasks in real settings and following individual cases from start to finish. We capture the informal steps, workarounds and judgment calls that rarely appear in documentation, along with the frustrations that signal where the process is failing the people inside it.

  2. 02Signal

    Quantify delay and rework

    We combine system timestamps, ticket and case data, and observation to measure cycle time, wait time, rework loops and handoff failure points. Value stream mapping separates value-adding work from waiting and duplication. The analysis identifies the few bottlenecks and decision points that drive most of the delay, which is where redesign effort should focus.

  3. 03Design

    Redesign flow and handoffs

    We create swimlane maps of the future-state process with explicit ownership, decision rights and information transfer at each handoff. We design for behavior as well as flow: clear prompts, simplified forms, sensible defaults and checklists at error-prone moments. Changes are prioritized by impact and by how easily the people doing the work can adopt them.

  4. 04Test

    Pilot and measure

    We pilot the redesigned process with one team, region or case type and track the same measures used in the baseline. We also gather feedback from the people performing the work, since their experience often reveals adjustments needed before wider rollout. Results inform a final design grounded in observed performance.

  5. 05Scale

    Embed and sustain

    We support rollout with updated documentation, training, system configuration requirements and a dashboard of process health indicators. We coach process owners to run regular reviews and to treat the process as something that keeps improving. Clear ownership is what prevents the process from drifting back to old habits.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Better processes make work more humane. Employees spend less time chasing information, redoing tasks and absorbing blame for failures they did not cause. Customers and patients experience fewer delays and errors. In mission-driven organizations, efficient processes free staff time for the people they serve. We involve frontline staff in redesign because they understand the work best and deserve a voice in how it changes.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is the difference between a swimlane map and a value stream map?

A swimlane map shows who does what and when, with each role or team in its own lane, making handoffs and ownership visible. A value stream map focuses on flow and time, distinguishing value-adding work from waiting, rework and duplication. We often use both, since one clarifies accountability and the other reveals where time is lost.

Why include behavioral science in process mapping?

Processes are carried out by people who make choices. They skip steps they find unclear, create workarounds when systems are slow and avoid handoffs they do not trust. Process mapping that captures those behaviors leads to designs people will actually follow. Without that, improvements often look good on paper and fade within months.

Do you recommend technology changes?

When technology is part of the problem, yes. We document system requirements and integration gaps that the process redesign depends on. But we are technology-neutral and often find that clarifying ownership, simplifying steps and improving handoffs delivers meaningful gains before any new system is purchased. When new systems are warranted, a clear future-state process makes vendor selection and configuration far more effective.

How involved will our staff need to be?

Frontline involvement is essential. We need time with the people who do the work for observation and interviews, and we involve them in testing the redesigned process. We schedule this carefully to minimize disruption. Their participation also builds ownership, which is one of the strongest predictors that a new process will last.
