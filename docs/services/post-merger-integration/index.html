The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Acquisitions are often justified by cost and revenue assumptions that depend on two groups of people learning to work as one. Integration teams typically manage systems, finance and legal workstreams well, while the human questions arrive all at once: who leads which function, which roles change or disappear, whose processes prevail and how decisions will be made. Uncertainty spreads quickly, and the most marketable employees, often the ones the buyer most needs, are the first to take calls from recruiters.

Carve-outs add their own complexity. A business leaving a larger parent must stand up functions it never owned, define its own culture and leadership team, and manage transition service agreements while keeping customers served. Roll-up strategies compound the challenge by repeating integration many times, often with lean teams. In every case, culture clashes, unclear decision rights and poorly handled selection processes erode trust and productivity in ways that rarely appear in integration status reports until value is already lost.

Our approach

How we deliver post-merger integration.

  1. 01Listen

    Understand both organizations

    We interview leaders and employees across both organizations, and conduct focus groups in key functions, to understand how each company actually works: how decisions are made, how performance is managed, what people value and what they fear about the deal. This surfaces cultural differences that matter for integration rather than surface-level contrasts.

  2. 02Measure

    Integration baseline and risk map

    We compare organization structures, spans and layers, job architectures, compensation and benefits, and retention patterns across both businesses. We identify roles critical to deal value and employees at elevated flight risk. A short pulse survey establishes a baseline for sentiment that we track through integration.

  3. 03Design

    Combined organization and ways of working

    We design the combined organization top down, from leadership structure to functional design, and define decision rights for the integrated business. We develop a fair, transparent selection process for leadership and key roles, harmonization principles for pay and benefits, and a deliberate culture integration plan built with leaders from both sides.

  4. 04Deliver

    Integration management and change

    We support the integration management office on people workstreams, coordinate leadership selection, prepare managers for difficult conversations and deliver change communications. For carve-outs, we help build standalone functions and leadership. Day one and day one hundred milestones are planned in detail, with attention to how each change lands for employees.

  5. 05Sustain

    Track integration health

    We monitor retention of critical talent, sentiment, cross-team collaboration and adoption of new processes against the baseline. Integration governance uses this evidence to intervene early. When the formal integration closes, we hand over metrics and ownership to the combined leadership team. Lessons learned inform the next acquisition, which matters for serial acquirers.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Integrations affect people deeply: roles change, colleagues leave, familiar ways of working disappear. How a combined organization handles those changes signals what it values. Fair selection, honest communication and thoughtful support for affected employees preserve dignity and trust. Customers experience fewer service disruptions when the people serving them are stable and informed. For mergers involving hospitals, nonprofits or community institutions, careful integration protects access to services that communities depend on, which is a social obligation as well as a business priority.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

Why do mergers fail on the people side?

Common causes include delayed or opaque leadership decisions, loss of critical talent during uncertainty, unaddressed differences in how the two organizations make decisions, and integration plans that underestimate the change load on managers. Many of these issues are predictable. Assessing culture and talent risk early, and designing fair processes for selection and harmonization, reduces the chance that people issues erode the value of the deal.

How do you integrate two company cultures?

We start by describing each culture in specific, observable terms, such as how decisions are made, how conflict is handled and how performance is recognized. Leaders from both organizations then agree on which practices the combined business will adopt, which it will retire and which it will build new. These choices are embedded in structures, processes and leadership behavior, not only communicated through values statements.

How is a carve-out different from a typical integration?

In a carve-out, the business must build an independent organization rather than fold into an existing one. That means standing up functions previously provided by the parent, appointing a standalone leadership team, defining a distinct culture and managing transition service agreements. Employees often face uncertainty about their identity and career paths, so clear communication and early leadership decisions are especially important.

When should integration planning begin?

Integration planning ideally begins during diligence, when cultural and talent risks are first assessed, and accelerates between signing and close. Early planning allows leadership decisions to be announced quickly after close, which reduces uncertainty. Where regulatory constraints limit information sharing before close, we use clean team arrangements and plan around what is permissible.
