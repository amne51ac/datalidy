Social Impact Strategy

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Many organizations carry a portfolio of well-intended commitments that grew one decision at a time: a signature program here, a partnership there, a pledge made during a moment of public attention. Each makes sense on its own, but together they rarely add up to a coherent strategy. Boards ask what the organization is actually achieving, funders and investors request evidence the organization cannot produce, and staff spread across too many priorities to do any of them well. The result is activity that is hard to explain and harder to defend.

The people problem underneath is just as serious. Communities that were promised partnership often experience extraction instead: surveys without follow-up, pilots that end when funding does. Employees who joined for the mission lose confidence when impact claims outpace reality. Leaders sense the gap but lack a shared language to make trade-offs, so decisions default to whoever argues hardest or whichever initiative has the most visible sponsor. A credible social impact strategy has to repair those relationships as well as clarify the plan.

Our approach

How we deliver social impact strategy.

  1. 01Listen

    Mapping what you do and what others need

    We interview leaders, staff, partners, funders and community members, and review existing programs, grants and commitments. The aim is to understand the problem from the perspective of the people experiencing it, where your organization has genuine standing to act, and where community expectations differ from internal assumptions about your role and contribution.

  2. 02Measure

    Baselining assets, gaps and evidence

    We inventory current investments against outcomes, assess the strength of existing evidence, and scan the landscape of other actors working on the same issues. A materiality and contribution analysis shows where your resources, expertise and relationships are most likely to change outcomes, and where you would add little beyond what others already provide well.

  3. 03Design

    Choosing focus and building the logic

    With a cross-functional working group and community advisors, we set a small number of outcome priorities, test them against your capabilities, and build a logic model for each that links activities to outputs, short-term outcomes and long-term impact, with assumptions and risks stated plainly enough to be tested.

  4. 04Deliver

    Aligning resources, roles and partners

    We translate priorities into a multiyear roadmap, budget reallocation, governance structure and partner model. That includes deciding what to stop or sunset responsibly, clarifying decision rights between leadership and program teams, and preparing managers and partners to explain the strategy in their own words to staff, grantees and residents.

  5. 05Sustain

    Measuring, learning and reporting

    We set up an impact measurement framework with a manageable set of indicators, data collection that respects participants' time, and a learning cadence where leaders review evidence and adjust. Board and public reporting templates distinguish contribution from attribution, so the organization can report progress honestly and stand behind its claims.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We work with chief executives, chief impact and sustainability officers, foundation presidents, nonprofit boards and executive directors, and investor-backed companies with a stated mission. Typical trigger moments include a new chief executive or board chair, a strategic plan refresh, a major gift or endowment change, rising pressure from investors or regulators for impact evidence, or a recognition that the current portfolio of commitments has grown too diffuse to manage, explain or fund well.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

A focused social impact strategy changes more than a report. Communities receive sustained commitment instead of short pilots, and they gain a real voice in setting priorities. Employees see a clear connection between their work and the mission, which supports engagement and retention. Customers and partners can trust the organization's claims. And because the strategy is built on evidence and honest trade-offs, scarce resources go further toward the social good the organization exists to advance.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does a social impact strategy consultant do?

A social impact strategy consultant helps an organization decide which social outcomes to pursue, how its programs, investments and partnerships will contribute to them, and how progress will be measured. The work combines research with affected communities, analysis of existing activity and evidence, facilitated priority setting with leadership, and the design of governance and measurement so the strategy can be managed, reported and improved over time.

How is social impact strategy different from CSR?

Corporate social responsibility often describes a company's broader conduct and commitments, including ethics, environmental practices and community giving. Social impact strategy is narrower and more outcome-focused: it asks which specific changes in people's lives or conditions the organization will work toward, through what causal pathways, and with what evidence. Many companies use social impact strategy to give their CSR program sharper focus and more credible measurement.

How long does a social impact strategy engagement take?

Timelines depend on the size of the portfolio and the depth of community engagement required. A focused strategy for a single business unit or foundation program moves faster than an enterprise-wide redesign with multiple community advisory processes. We agree on scope and milestones at the outset, and we design the engagement so that early findings inform decisions while the full strategy is still being developed.

How do you measure social impact without overburdening programs?

We start with the decisions leaders actually need to make and choose the fewest indicators that inform them. Where possible we use administrative data already collected, sample rather than survey everyone, and pair quantitative indicators with periodic qualitative evidence. Participants and frontline staff help select what is measured, which improves data quality and ensures measurement is useful to the people delivering and receiving services.
