Community Engagement

Our community engagement consulting helps organizations design participation that is honest about the influence it offers, reaches people who are usually left out, and turns what residents say into decisions they can see.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Organizations that affect a place, whether a hospital system, a developer, a utility, a school district or a city agency, are expected to engage the community. Too often that means a public meeting at an inconvenient hour, a survey that reaches the usual voices, and a summary report that nobody acts on. Residents learn that participation changes little, attendance falls, and the organization concludes that the community is disengaged. Meanwhile projects encounter opposition late, when changes are most expensive and relationships are hardest to repair.

The underlying problem is a mismatch between what engagement promises and what it delivers. Leaders rarely decide in advance how much influence residents will actually have, so expectations diverge. Engagement teams lack the time, budget or language access to reach renters, shift workers, young people, immigrants or people with disabilities. And there is no feedback loop telling participants what happened to their input. Community engagement consulting must address the design of the decision itself, not only the format of the meetings.

Our approach

How we deliver community engagement.

  1. 01Listen

    Understanding history, place and trust

    Before designing anything, we learn the history of the relationship between your organization and the community, including past promises and grievances. We conduct interviews with community leaders, trusted messengers and frontline staff, and attend existing community gatherings rather than convening new ones, so we understand how people already organize, communicate and decide.

  2. 02Measure

    Mapping who is affected and who is heard

    We compare the demographics and geography of past participants with the population affected by the decision, identifying gaps in reach by language, age, tenure, disability and income. We also assess current engagement practices against the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation to see where stated intentions and actual influence diverge.

  3. 03Design

    Matching the method to the promise

    With leadership, we decide which decisions are open, what level of participation is appropriate for each, and what constraints are fixed. We then co-design methods with community partners: compensated advisory groups, small-group conversations hosted by local organizations, multilingual and accessible formats, and deliberative sessions where residents weigh real trade-offs with real information.

  4. 04Deliver

    Running engagement and closing the loop

    We facilitate or support delivery of the engagement, train staff to host conversations well, and partner with community-based organizations who are compensated for their role. Every phase ends with a report back that shows participants what was heard, what changed as a result and, where input was not adopted, why.

  5. 05Sustain

    Building lasting engagement capacity

    We help institutionalize engagement through standards, a participation policy, a community feedback tracker and an internal community of practice. Where appropriate we support standing advisory bodies or community benefit agreements, so engagement becomes an ongoing relationship with accountability rather than a series of disconnected project consultations.

Outcomes

What changes.

Residents who are usually absent from decisions take part and see their influence reflected.
Fewer late-stage objections because concerns surface early, when plans can still change.
Clear, shared expectations about which decisions are open to community influence and how.
Staff who can plan and host respectful engagement without relying on outside facilitators.
Who we serve

Who engages us.

We serve community relations and government affairs leaders, public agency directors, hospital community benefit teams, real estate and infrastructure developers, utilities, school districts and foundations working in place-based initiatives. Engagements often begin when a major capital project, rezoning, facility closure, community health needs assessment or rate case requires public input, when prior engagement has damaged trust, or when leaders recognize that their current approach consistently hears from the same small group of voices.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Good community engagement improves decisions and the relationships around them. Residents gain real influence over changes to their neighborhoods and services, and they are compensated for their expertise rather than asked to give it freely. Employees in community-facing roles gain the skills and authority to build trust. Projects designed with community input tend to fit local needs better, face less opposition and serve residents more equitably, which benefits both the organization and the place it serves.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation?

The IAP2 Spectrum, developed by the International Association for Public Participation, describes five levels of public influence: inform, consult, involve, collaborate and empower. Each level carries a different promise to the public. We use it to help leaders decide, before engagement begins, how much influence the community will have over a given decision, and to design methods and communications that match that promise honestly.

How do you reach community members who do not usually participate?

We go where people already are rather than asking them to come to us. That means partnering with trusted community organizations, compensating participants for their time, offering childcare, food, translation and accessible formats, and scheduling around work and caregiving. We track who participates against who is affected so that gaps are visible during the process, not discovered afterward when it is too late to adjust.

Should community members be paid for participating in engagement?

In most cases, yes. Residents who sit on advisory groups, co-design sessions or deliberative panels contribute expertise about their own lives and neighborhoods, often at personal cost. Compensation recognizes that expertise and makes participation possible for people who cannot donate unpaid time. We help clients set fair, consistent compensation policies that account for their procurement, tax and public benefits considerations.

What is the difference between community engagement and stakeholder engagement?

The terms overlap. Community engagement usually focuses on residents and local organizations affected by decisions in a particular place, with attention to equity and power. Stakeholder engagement is broader, covering investors, employees, regulators, suppliers, customers and advocacy groups, often across many places. Many clients need both, and we design them to complement each other rather than run as separate, competing processes.
