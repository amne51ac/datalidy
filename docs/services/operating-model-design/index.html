Operating Model Design

Our operating model design work helps leaders define how value is delivered: the capabilities, structures, decision rights, processes, technology and people practices required to execute strategy without unnecessary complexity.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Operating models usually evolve by accumulation. Functions add teams, regions add local processes, acquisitions bring parallel systems, and shared services are created without clear service levels. Over time the organization becomes harder to navigate: decisions require too many approvals, accountabilities overlap, and customers experience inconsistency depending on which part of the organization they encounter. Leaders sense that cost and speed are suffering but struggle to pinpoint where, because the operating model has never been described as a whole.

Redesign efforts often fail by focusing on structure alone. A new organization chart is announced, yet decision rights, governance forums, processes, metrics and incentives remain unchanged, so work continues much as before under different names. Employees experience uncertainty and lost relationships without seeing improvement. Effective operating model design treats structure as one element among several and pays attention to how people will actually work, decide and collaborate under the new design, including the managers who must make it function.

Our approach

How we deliver operating model design.

  1. 01Listen

    Understanding how work really happens

    We interview leaders and conduct workshops with managers and frontline staff to understand where decisions get stuck, where handoffs fail and where work is duplicated. Customer and employee perspectives reveal how the current model is experienced, which is often different from how it is described in organization charts and policy documents.

  2. 02Measure

    Diagnosing the current model

    We build a current-state view using an operating model canvas covering value chain, capabilities, organization, locations, information systems, suppliers and management system. Activity analysis, spans and layers analysis, decision-rights mapping and cost-to-serve data show where complexity, cost and delay concentrate, establishing a baseline for evaluating design options.

  3. 03Design

    Designing options with clear principles

    With leadership, we set design principles derived from strategy, then develop and evaluate alternative models: centralized, decentralized, hybrid, platform or shared services. The preferred design specifies capabilities, structure, decision rights using tools such as RAPID or RACI, governance forums, key processes, roles and metrics. Managers and employees test the design through realistic scenarios.

  4. 04Deliver

    Transitioning with care

    We develop a transition plan covering sequencing, role mapping, talent selection, consultation obligations, communications and change management. Leaders and managers receive guidance on holding individual conversations with affected employees. Implementation proceeds in waves with readiness checks, so business continuity is protected and issues are resolved before the next phase begins.

  5. 05Sustain

    Embedding the new model

    After transition, we track adoption and performance indicators, run pulse surveys to understand the employee experience of the change and conduct post-implementation reviews. Governance forums are calibrated, decision rights are reinforced and adjustments are made where the design is not working, so the operating model continues to evolve with strategy rather than ossifying again.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Operating models determine the daily working lives of employees and the experience customers receive. A well-designed model gives people clear roles, reasonable spans of control and the authority to make decisions close to the work. Customers benefit from simpler, more reliable service. Transitions handled with transparency and fairness protect trust. And an efficient model frees resources for investment in people, products and the communities the organization serves.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is an operating model?

An operating model describes how an organization delivers value to customers or beneficiaries. It covers the capabilities required, how work is organized into structures and processes, who makes which decisions, the technology and data that support the work, where work is done, the partners involved and the management system of metrics and governance. It translates strategy into the day-to-day mechanics of the organization.

What is an operating model canvas?

The operating model canvas is a one-page tool, developed by Andrew Campbell and colleagues, for describing an operating model across elements such as value delivery chains, organization, locations, information, suppliers and management system. We use it to create a shared picture of the current and target model, making it easier for leaders to compare options and see how changes in one element affect others.

How is operating model design different from a restructuring?

A restructuring typically changes reporting lines and roles, often with a cost objective. Operating model design is broader: it considers capabilities, processes, decision rights, governance, technology and metrics as well as structure. A restructuring may be one outcome, but without changes to how decisions are made and how work flows, structural changes alone rarely deliver the intended improvement.

How do you minimize disruption to employees during operating model change?

We involve managers and employees in testing the design, communicate clearly about what is known and unknown, and follow fair, consistent processes for role mapping and selection. Transitions are phased with readiness checks. Managers receive training for difficult conversations, and pulse surveys help leaders respond to concerns quickly. Change is never painless, but transparency and fairness strongly affect how it is experienced.
