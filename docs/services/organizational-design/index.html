The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Organizations grow by accretion. Teams are added to address new priorities, managers are promoted to retain talent, and reporting lines shift around individuals rather than strategy. Over time, the structure accumulates layers, uneven spans of control, duplicated functions and unclear accountabilities. Decisions slow down because too many people must agree, while important work falls between teams. Employees experience this as frustration, rework and a sense that no one owns the problems they care about.

Restructuring is often treated as the fix, but redrawing boxes on an org chart rarely solves underlying issues. Reorganizations can be disruptive, consume leadership attention and cause talented people to leave, especially when the rationale is unclear or the process feels unfair. Leaders need organizational design grounded in strategy and evidence, attentive to how work actually flows, explicit about decision rights, and implemented in a way that maintains trust and continuity.

Our approach

How we deliver organizational design.

  1. 01Listen

    Start from strategy and pain points

    We clarify the strategic priorities the organization must support and the design criteria leaders will use to judge options. Interviews and workshops with leaders, managers and employees surface where work gets stuck, where accountability is unclear and where collaboration is difficult. These pain points become explicit tests for any design.

  2. 02Measure

    Structural diagnostic

    We analyze spans and layers, role distribution, cost by function, and decision-making patterns. Organizational network analysis shows how information and collaboration actually flow, often revealing informal structures that differ from the formal chart. This evidence grounds design choices in fact rather than assumption. Costs are compared by function and level.

  3. 03Design

    Options, decision rights and roles

    We develop structural options, such as functional, divisional, matrix or hybrid models, and assess them against agreed criteria. We define decision rights using tools like RACI or similar frameworks, clarify accountabilities, and design key roles. Managers and employees test the design to identify issues before implementation.

  4. 04Deliver

    Transition with care

    We plan the transition in detail: sequencing, role mapping, fair selection processes where needed, and communication at every level. We support leaders through difficult conversations and help teams establish new ways of working, including handoffs, forums and governance routines. Employees know where they stand before changes take effect, which reduces anxiety and rumor.

  5. 05Sustain

    Review and refine

    We track indicators such as decision speed, role clarity, spans and retention after the change, and conduct a structured review within months of go-live. Designs are refined based on evidence, and governance ensures the structure does not drift back to old patterns. Leaders own the reviews.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Organizational structure shapes daily work in ways employees feel immediately: who they report to, whether they can make decisions, how often they wait on others and whether their contributions are visible. Good organizational design reduces frustration and burnout and gives people roles they can succeed in. Customers benefit from clearer ownership of their needs and faster responses. Designing changes with employees, and handling transitions fairly, also preserves trust during a period that can otherwise feel threatening.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is organizational design?

Organizational design is the process of shaping an organization's structure, roles, processes, decision rights and governance so that it can carry out its strategy. It goes beyond the org chart to include how work flows between teams, how decisions are made, how people are measured and rewarded, and how the organization adapts. Good design starts from strategy and is tested against how work really happens.

What is the ideal span of control?

There is no single ideal. Appropriate spans depend on the complexity and variety of work, how experienced team members are, how much coordination is required and how much of a manager's time goes to their own individual work. Routine operational teams can support wider spans, while complex or highly varied work needs narrower ones. We analyze current spans against these factors rather than applying a universal target.

How do you clarify decision rights?

We identify the decisions that matter most to strategy and performance, then define who recommends, who decides, who must be consulted and who needs to be informed. Frameworks such as RACI help structure the conversation, but the real value comes from leaders agreeing explicitly on ownership and holding to those agreements. We test decision rights against real scenarios before finalizing them.

How do you reduce disruption during a reorganization?

Disruption is reduced by a clear rationale, fair processes, fast decisions on leadership roles, honest communication and support for managers who must explain changes to their teams. We sequence changes carefully, protect critical customer-facing work and identify key talent at risk. Involving employees in testing the design also surfaces practical problems early and builds understanding.
