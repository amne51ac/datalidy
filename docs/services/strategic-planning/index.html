Strategic Planning

Our strategic planning consulting helps leadership teams and boards make explicit choices about where to compete or serve, how to win, and what to stop, then translate those choices into priorities managers can act on.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Strategic plans often fail in one of two ways. Some are so broad that every existing initiative fits inside them, so nothing changes. Others are thoughtful but disconnected from budgeting, staffing and performance management, so they are revisited once a year and ignored in between. In both cases leaders have not made the hard choices about what the organization will not do, and managers are left to interpret competing priorities on their own, often by defaulting to the most urgent request.

Planning processes also tend to exclude the people who know the most about customers and operations. Strategy is written by a small group, communicated in a town hall and then cascaded as a list of targets. Middle managers, who must translate strategy into team decisions, receive little context about why choices were made. Employees cannot see how their work connects to the plan. Strategic planning consulting should produce a plan with genuine choices and a process that builds understanding and commitment across the organization.

Our approach

How we deliver strategic planning.

  1. 01Listen

    Gathering perspectives across the organization

    We interview the board and leadership team, hold structured conversations with managers and frontline employees, and gather customer, partner and investor perspectives. An organization-wide survey captures views on current direction, priorities and barriers to execution. This research often reveals disagreement among leaders that must be addressed before any plan can work in practice.

  2. 02Measure

    Establishing a strategic fact base

    We build a fact base on market dynamics, customer needs, competitive or peer position, financial performance and organizational capabilities, including people metrics such as engagement, turnover and capability gaps. Scenario analysis tests how different futures would affect the organization, so the plan is robust to uncertainty rather than dependent on a single forecast.

  3. 03Design

    Making explicit choices

    In facilitated sessions, leadership defines its aspiration, where to play, how to win, the capabilities required and the management systems that support them. Options are evaluated against the fact base and tested with managers and employees. We insist on explicit trade-offs: what will receive more investment, what will receive less and what will stop entirely.

  4. 04Deliver

    Translating strategy into action

    We convert choices into a small set of strategic priorities, objectives and key results, initiative charters with owners and resource requirements, and an aligned budget. Managers receive briefing materials and facilitation guides to discuss the strategy with their teams and translate it into team-level goals, so the plan reaches the front line.

  5. 05Sustain

    Governing execution

    We design a strategy management routine: quarterly strategy reviews held separately from operational meetings, a dashboard of leading and lagging indicators, and a process for adjusting priorities when assumptions change. Board reporting focuses on progress against strategic choices. We build internal capability so the organization can run future planning cycles itself.

Who we serve

Who engages us.

We work with chief executives, chief strategy officers, executive teams and boards in companies, investor-backed businesses, nonprofits, health systems, universities and public institutions. Common triggers include a new chief executive or board chair, the end of a planning cycle, a significant market shift, an ownership change or new investment, a merger, underperformance against plan, or a recognition that the current strategy is not shaping decisions because it lacks clear priorities and trade-offs.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

A clear strategy is a gift to the people who carry it out. Employees spend less effort on conflicting priorities and initiatives that will never be funded. Managers can make decisions with confidence. Customers experience a more consistent organization. When planning includes community and stakeholder perspectives, the plan is more likely to account for the organization's broader effects. And focused resource allocation strengthens the financial performance that sustains jobs and investment over time.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does a strategic planning consultant do?

A strategic planning consultant designs and facilitates the planning process, builds the fact base, challenges assumptions and helps leaders make and document clear choices. They also help translate strategy into priorities, budgets and governance routines. An effective consultant brings outside perspective and analytical rigor while ensuring the leadership team owns the strategy and has the capability to manage it after the engagement ends.

How many years should a strategic plan cover?

Many organizations plan over a multiyear horizon with annual operating priorities and quarterly reviews. The right horizon depends on how quickly your industry or environment changes and how long major investments take to mature. More important than the horizon is the review rhythm: a plan revisited quarterly and adjusted as evidence emerges is more useful than a longer plan left unchanged.

How do you involve employees in strategic planning without slowing it down?

We gather employee input early through surveys, focus groups and manager conversations, so it shapes the fact base rather than arriving late. We test draft choices with representative groups and use their feedback to refine implementation. Leadership retains decision rights, but employees understand the reasoning. This adds little time and typically accelerates execution because fewer people need to be persuaded later.

What is the difference between strategic planning and operational planning?

Strategic planning decides direction: where the organization will focus, how it will create value and which capabilities it needs. Operational planning determines how work will be organized, resourced and scheduled to deliver those choices in a given period. Both are necessary. Problems arise when operational plans are built without reference to strategy, or when strategy is set without regard to operational capacity.
