Persuasive Technology Design

Persuasive technology design applies behavioral science to digital products so they help people change behavior they want to change. We design prompts, feedback and habit loops that are effective, transparent and aligned with the user's own goals.

The challenge

The problem behind the problem.

Digital products are among the most powerful tools for changing behavior ever created. They can deliver the right prompt at the right moment, provide immediate feedback, simplify complex tasks and connect people to social support. Yet many products designed to change behavior struggle with engagement that fades after the first weeks. Others achieve engagement through techniques that exploit attention, such as endless feeds, manufactured urgency and variable rewards that serve the product more than the person.

Organizations building health apps, financial tools, learning platforms and workplace software need a better path. Persuasive technology design has a substantial research base, from the original study of computers as persuasive tools to current work on habit formation and digital behavior change. Applied well, it produces products that people keep using because they genuinely help. Applied carelessly, it creates short-lived engagement, user backlash, erosion of trust and growing regulatory and reputational risk for the organization.

Our approach

How we deliver persuasive technology design.

  1. 01Observe

    Understand goals and daily context

    We research what users are trying to achieve, what has helped or hindered them before, and how the target behavior fits into their daily routines. Diary studies and contextual interviews reveal existing habits, natural prompts and moments of motivation that a product could support, along with sources of friction it could remove.

  2. 02Signal

    Find the activation and habit signals

    Product data shows which early actions predict continued use, where engagement decays, and which behavioral types sustain or abandon the behavior. Survival curves of engagement reveal when people drop off. We distinguish engagement that reflects real progress toward the user's goal from engagement that is merely activity.

  3. 03Design

    Design ethical persuasion

    Using the Fogg Behavior Model and related research, we design tiny starter behaviors, well-timed prompts anchored to existing routines, meaningful feedback, progress visualization and appropriate social support. Every persuasive element passes an ethical review: it must be transparent, align with the user's goals, respect autonomy and allow easy opt-out or adjustment.

  4. 04Test

    Test for behavior change, not just clicks

    We run usability tests and A/B tests that measure the target behavior and progress toward the user's goal, not only clicks or time in app. Guardrail metrics track signs of harm, such as notification opt-outs, compulsive use patterns or negative feedback. Results are segmented so designs work for different behavioral types.

  5. 05Scale

    Sustain engagement responsibly

    Effective persuasive patterns become part of a documented design system with ethical guidelines. We help teams monitor long-term retention and behavior outcomes, set policies for notifications and personalization, and train product teams to apply persuasive design responsibly. Periodic ethical audits keep the product aligned as features evolve.

Impact lens

Beyond the balance sheet.

Persuasive technology can do real good: helping people manage chronic conditions, save money, learn new skills and maintain healthier routines. It can also cause harm when designed to capture attention at any cost. We design products that people would endorse on reflection, protect vulnerable users, respect autonomy and avoid dark patterns. That stance benefits users, protects brands and contributes to a healthier relationship between people and technology.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is persuasive technology?

Persuasive technology is any interactive system designed to change people's attitudes or behaviors. Examples include fitness apps that prompt activity, financial tools that encourage saving and learning platforms that support study habits. The field emerged from research on how computers can act as tools, media and social actors to influence behavior, and it now informs much of modern product design.

How do you keep persuasive design ethical?

We apply a clear standard: persuasion must serve goals the user holds, be transparent about its intent, preserve the user's ability to choose and opt out, and avoid exploiting cognitive vulnerabilities. Every persuasive element is reviewed against these criteria before testing. We also monitor guardrail metrics after launch to detect unintended harm, such as compulsive use.

Why do so many behavior change apps lose users quickly?

Common reasons include asking for too much behavior too soon, relying on motivation that naturally fluctuates, sending prompts at poorly timed moments and offering little meaningful feedback. Products that succeed tend to start with very small behaviors, anchor prompts to existing routines, build ability and confidence gradually, and show users real progress toward goals they care about.

Do you work on habit formation?

Yes. Habit formation is central to sustained behavior change. We design for repetition in a stable context, simple starter behaviors, consistent prompts linked to existing routines and positive emotion after the behavior. We also help teams measure habit strength through behavioral consistency rather than relying on self-report, and identify which users need additional support.
