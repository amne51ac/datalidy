Context

The people side of professional & knowledge services.

In professional services firms, the product is people. Client value depends on how professionals listen, advise, collaborate and deliver, and growth depends on whether clients trust the firm enough to bring it more of their work. Yet firms often manage these behaviors indirectly, through utilization, billing and revenue targets. Those measures matter, but they lag. They reveal problems with client relationships or team effectiveness long after the behaviors that caused them have taken hold.

Partnership structures create distinctive people problems. Partners are owners, producers and leaders at once, with significant autonomy and strong personal relationships with clients. Collaboration across practices can feel risky, and leadership decisions often require consensus among peers. Talented professionals are promoted for technical excellence, then asked to lead teams and develop people without preparation. Knowledge sits with individuals, and firms struggle to share it in ways that improve quality across the organization.

Current forces are changing the work itself. AI tools are automating research, drafting and analysis, raising questions about pricing, training of junior staff and the nature of professional judgment. Clients expect integrated advice across disciplines. Talent expectations around flexibility and development continue to shift. Professional services firms that understand the behaviors behind client trust, collaboration and adoption are better prepared to adapt without losing the culture that made them successful.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Client relationships held by individuals

Many client relationships depend on one or two partners. When those partners retire or move, relationships are at risk. Firms want institutional relationships, but professionals may hesitate to introduce colleagues or share client insight. The behaviors that build firm-level trust are rarely defined or developed deliberately.

02

Cross-practice collaboration that stalls

Clients could benefit from expertise across practices, yet referrals between partners remain limited. Concerns about quality, credit and client experience outweigh incentives. Without understanding the behavioral barriers to referral, firms invest in account programs and technology that leave underlying habits unchanged.

03

Leaders promoted for technical excellence

Professionals often reach leadership roles because they are excellent practitioners. Leading teams, giving feedback and managing conflict require different skills that are rarely taught. Without development, new leaders default to doing the work themselves, limiting team growth and contributing to burnout across levels.

04

AI adoption and professional judgment

AI tools promise efficiency in research, drafting and analysis, but adoption is uneven and raises concerns about quality, confidentiality and the development of junior professionals. Firms need to decide where AI supports judgment, where human review is essential and how to bring skeptical professionals along.

05

Feedback measured too slowly

Client satisfaction surveys and annual reviews provide feedback months after the interactions that shaped them. Firms lack timely signals about client sentiment, team health and engagement. Leaders make decisions about staffing, pricing and investment with lagging information and untested assumptions.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Professional services firms help organizations make sound decisions, comply with laws, manage risk and build infrastructure, so the quality of their judgment affects many people beyond their direct clients. Behavioral science supports that public value by strengthening how professionals collaborate, lead and learn, and by helping firms adopt AI responsibly without eroding the expertise future professionals need. Healthier partnership cultures reduce burnout, improve retention and produce better advice. When firms understand the behaviors behind trust, they serve clients more completely and contribute to a more capable professional workforce.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What types of professional services firms do you work with?

We work with consulting, accounting, legal, engineering, architecture, advisory and other knowledge-based firms. The common thread is that value depends on expertise, judgment and relationships. We adapt our approach to each firm's partnership structure, client base and culture, recognizing that professional norms, regulation and client expectations vary across disciplines and markets.

How do you help partners collaborate across practices?

We start by understanding the specific concerns that limit referrals, such as client experience, credit and familiarity with colleagues. We then design processes, routines and leadership practices that address those concerns directly and track leading behaviors, like joint client meetings, that predict collaborative growth. Incentives are part of the picture but rarely the whole answer.

Is executive coaching confidential for partners?

Yes. Coaching conversations are confidential, and goals are agreed with the individual partner. When a firm sponsors coaching, we agree in advance what will be shared, typically progress on development goals rather than conversation content. That clarity builds the trust coaching requires while giving the firm appropriate visibility into whether the investment is helping.
