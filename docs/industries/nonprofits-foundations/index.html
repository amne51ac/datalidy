Context

The people side of nonprofits & foundations.

Nonprofits are asked to solve complex social problems with constrained, often restricted funding. Staff are deeply committed but frequently underpaid, overextended and carrying emotionally demanding work. Burnout and turnover among program staff disrupt relationships with participants that took months to build. Executive directors often hold several jobs at once, and many organizations are approaching leadership transitions with little succession planning. The people problems inside a nonprofit are inseparable from its ability to deliver its mission.

Outside the organization, the people served increasingly expect to shape programs rather than simply receive them. Communities that have experienced extractive research and short-lived initiatives are rightly skeptical. Foundations, for their part, are rethinking their role: moving toward trust-based practices, multiyear general operating support and participatory grantmaking, while still needing to understand whether their capital is contributing to meaningful change. Both grantees and funders need better ways to listen, learn and measure without adding reporting burden.

Several forces are shaping the sector now. Demand for services continues to outpace funding in many fields. Government contracts often underfund indirect costs, stretching infrastructure. Donors and boards ask for clearer evidence of impact, while practitioners caution against metrics that distort the work. Wage pressure makes it harder to retain staff. And new philanthropic vehicles, from donor-advised funds to collaborative funds, change how resources flow. Effective nonprofit consulting must respect these constraints and build capacity that remains after the engagement ends.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Staff burnout and turnover

Program staff in human services, education and advocacy often carry heavy caseloads, secondary trauma and below-market pay. When experienced staff leave, participant relationships and institutional knowledge leave with them. Organizations need to understand which factors they can influence, such as workload, supervision, flexibility and career paths, and act on them within real budget limits.

02

Leadership transitions

Many nonprofits are led by founders or long-tenured executives whose departure creates risk. Boards may lack experience managing a search, and organizations often have not built a second layer of leadership. A poorly managed transition can unsettle staff, funders and partners at once, threatening programs that communities rely on.

03

Measuring impact without distorting the work

Funders want evidence; practitioners worry that narrow metrics push programs toward what is easy to count. Many organizations collect data mainly for reporting, not learning. The challenge is building a measurement approach anchored in a clear theory of change, proportionate to the organization's size, and useful to staff making decisions.

04

Authentic community voice

Organizations increasingly commit to centering the people they serve, but participation is often limited to advisory groups or feedback surveys with little influence on decisions. Building genuine co-design and shared power requires time, compensation for participants, and changes to governance and program design processes that many organizations have not yet made.

05

Funder and grantee alignment

Foundations and grantees often hold different assumptions about strategy, timelines and evidence. Reporting requirements can consume capacity that would be better spent on programs. Funders adopting trust-based or participatory approaches need practical ways to learn alongside grantees, assess their own contribution and adjust their practices in response to feedback.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Nonprofits and foundations exist to advance the public good, from housing and health to education, the arts and civic life. Their impact depends on the people who do the work and the trust of the communities they serve. When staff are supported, fairly paid and well led, they build the lasting relationships that drive real outcomes. When communities shape the programs meant for them, those programs fit better and earn more trust. Stronger people practices and honest measurement help the sector direct scarce resources to what actually works.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What kind of nonprofit consulting do you provide?

Our nonprofit consulting focuses on strategy, leadership, staff experience and impact measurement. We support strategic planning, theory of change development, leadership transitions, board effectiveness, retention diagnostics and community co-design. Every engagement is grounded in research with the people involved, including staff, participants and partners, and is designed to build lasting capacity within the organization rather than dependence on outside advisors.

How do you keep impact measurement proportionate for small organizations?

We start with the decisions leaders and staff actually need to make, then identify the smallest set of measures that inform those decisions. We use existing data wherever possible and combine it with periodic qualitative learning. The result is a practical system that a small team can maintain and that produces credible evidence for funders without diverting resources from programs.

Do you work with foundations on their own practices?

Yes. We help foundations clarify strategy, gather candid grantee feedback, assess the burden of their application and reporting requirements, and adopt practices such as multiyear general operating support or participatory grantmaking. We also help funders design learning and evaluation approaches that recognize their contribution alongside many other actors rather than claiming sole credit for outcomes.
