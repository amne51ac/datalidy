Digital Health

Digital health engagement tends to peak early and fade quietly. We help digital health companies understand why users drift, design products that support lasting self-management and define predictive KPIs that connect daily behavior to clinical and commercial outcomes.

Context

The people side of digital health.

Digital health products promise continuous support for conditions that unfold over years: diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, chronic pain, depression and anxiety. The pattern most teams know well is engagement decay. Users enroll with real motivation, log readings and complete lessons for a few weeks, then open the app less often until they stop. The product has not failed technically. It has asked people to sustain a new behavior without enough attention to ability, timing and meaning in the context of their lives.

Inside the company, product, clinical, data science and commercial teams often hold different definitions of success. Clinicians care about outcomes and safety, product teams track active users and feature use, and sales teams need evidence that employers, health plans and providers will accept. Without shared, behavior-based KPIs, teams optimize for different signals, and the engagement metrics that look healthy on dashboards may have little relationship to whether members actually manage their condition better.

The market has matured. Buyers now expect evidence of sustained use and measurable health improvement, not download counts. Integration with care teams, remote monitoring devices and payer workflows adds complexity. AI coaching and personalization create new possibilities and new ethical questions about persuasion. Companies that understand digital health engagement as a behavioral science problem, and measure it accordingly, are better positioned to retain members, earn clinical trust and demonstrate value to the organizations that pay for them.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Engagement decay after the first weeks

Early enthusiasm carries new users through onboarding, then real life intervenes. Notifications become noise, logging feels like a chore and progress stalls. Teams often respond with more content or more reminders, which can accelerate disengagement. The underlying question, which behaviors predict who will persist, usually goes unanswered because the data has not been examined through a behavioral lens.

02

Self-management that does not fit daily life

Chronic illness self-management asks people to monitor, adjust medication, change diet and stay active, often while managing work, family and fatigue. Products designed around an ideal user overlook the routines and constraints of real patients. When the recommended behavior is too hard or poorly timed, even motivated members fall away and blame themselves.

03

Vanity metrics that buyers do not trust

Daily active users, session counts and content completion are easy to report and hard to connect with health outcomes. Employers, health plans and provider partners increasingly ask for evidence that engagement translates into better control of a condition. Without predictive KPIs that link behavior to outcomes, renewal conversations rely on narrative rather than proof.

04

Onboarding that loses people before value appears

Many digital health products ask for device pairing, health history, goals and permissions before members experience any benefit. Each step is a chance to drop off. Onboarding often reflects internal requirements instead of the user's first meaningful win, which means the people who most need support may never reach it.

05

Persuasion without clear ethical boundaries

Streaks, rewards, social features and AI coaches can motivate healthy behavior, but they can also create pressure, guilt or dependence. Digital health teams need principles for when persuasion supports a person's own goals and when it crosses into manipulation, especially for vulnerable populations living with serious or stigmatized conditions.

How we help

Where we focus.

  1. →

    Engagement signals and behavioral types

    We analyze product event data alongside clinical and enrollment data to find the early behaviors that predict persistence and the points where members drift. Behavioral segmentation then groups users by how they engage, not by demographics, so teams can design different pathways for the people who need structure, those who need flexibility and those who need human contact.

    Behavioral Data Analysis

  2. →

    Self-management product design

    Drawing on clinical health psychology and behavioral medicine, we redesign core routines such as logging, medication tracking and activity goals so they fit real lives. That means smaller starting behaviors, better anchoring to existing habits and prompts that arrive when a person can act. Each change is framed as a testable hypothesis rather than a feature request.

    Digital Health Product Design

  3. →

    Onboarding and first-value design

    We map onboarding step by step, measure where members leave and redesign the sequence around a first meaningful win, such as a useful insight from a first reading. Usability testing with representative patients surfaces confusion early, and A/B tests confirm which changes improve activation without sacrificing data quality or clinical safety.

    Onboarding & Activation Design

  4. →

    Outcome-linked predictive KPIs

    We help product, clinical and commercial teams agree on a small set of behavior-based KPIs that predict health outcomes and renewal. These measures give leaders an earlier, more credible view of program performance and give buyers evidence they can evaluate. Dashboards make the measures usable in weekly decisions rather than quarterly reports.

    Predictive KPI Design

  5. →

    Ethical persuasive technology review

    We review engagement mechanics, messaging and AI coaching behavior against principles from persuasive technology and self-determination theory. The review identifies features that support autonomy and competence and flags those that may create pressure or shame. Teams leave with practical design standards they can apply to future features and experiments.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Digital health can extend support to people who live far from specialists, cannot take time off for frequent visits or feel stigma about seeking care. That promise is realized only if people keep using the product long enough for it to help. Behavioral science advances the mission by designing for real lives, sustaining engagement through meaning and competence rather than pressure, and measuring whether daily behavior actually improves health. It also keeps persuasion honest, ensuring that technology helps people pursue their own goals for managing chronic illness with dignity.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

Why does digital health engagement drop off, and can it be prevented?

Drop-off usually reflects a mismatch between what the product asks and what people can sustain in their daily lives. Some decline is natural as members build routines, so the goal is not constant use but the right behaviors at the right frequency. We identify which early behaviors predict persistence and design pathways that support them. No approach eliminates attrition, but it can be understood, measured and reduced through testing.

Do you work with clinical teams as well as product teams?

Yes. Our roots are in clinical and health psychology and behavioral medicine, so we are comfortable working with medical directors, clinical researchers and care coaches alongside product managers, designers and data scientists. Much of the value comes from aligning these groups on shared, behavior-based measures of success that respect clinical evidence and product realities.

Can behavioral science help us show value to employers and health plans?

It can help you build a more credible case. We define predictive KPIs that link member behavior to health outcomes and design experiments that isolate the effect of specific product features. That evidence supports renewal and sales conversations with buyers who want more than engagement counts. We do not promise particular outcomes, but we help you measure them rigorously.
