Context

The people side of financial services & cdfis.

Financial institutions run on trust, and trust is built or lost by people. Loan officers, branch staff, member service representatives and collections teams shape how customers experience an institution far more than any brand campaign. In community development financial institutions, minority depository institutions and mission-driven credit unions, small teams often carry heavy loads: underwriting complex deals, providing technical assistance, managing compliance and reporting to multiple funders. Recruiting and retaining people who combine financial skill with community credibility is a persistent challenge.

Outside the institution, many borrowers and small business owners have been underserved or mistreated by mainstream finance. They may have thin credit files, irregular income or past experiences that make them wary of lenders. The application process itself, with its document requirements and opaque timelines, can deter the very people a mission lender most wants to reach. Serving these borrowers well means designing products, processes and relationships around their real circumstances, not around assumptions built for prime customers.

Several forces are shaping the sector. Public and private capital directed toward community lending has increased, raising expectations for deployment speed and impact reporting. Larger banks are partnering with CDFIs to meet community reinvestment goals. Digital lending platforms are changing customer expectations, while raising fairness questions about automated underwriting. Regulators continue to focus on fair lending and consumer protection. CDFI consulting must help institutions grow capacity and adopt new tools without losing the relationship-based approach that makes mission lending work.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Reaching underserved borrowers

Mission lenders exist to reach people and businesses that mainstream finance overlooks, yet application processes, document requirements and marketing channels often mirror those of conventional lenders. Borrowers who most need capital may never apply or may abandon applications midway. Institutions need to understand where and why prospective borrowers drop out of the journey.

02

Scaling without losing the relationship

As capital flows increase, CDFIs face pressure to deploy more loans faster. Growth can strain the high-touch relationships, technical assistance and flexible underwriting that distinguish mission lending. Institutions need operating models that scale efficiently while protecting the borrower relationships and community knowledge at the core of their value.

03

Talent for mission lending

Mission lenders need staff who combine credit expertise, cultural fluency and commitment to community. Competition from larger institutions for underwriters, compliance specialists and technology talent is intense, and compensation gaps can be significant. Retaining these people requires clear career paths, strong management and a culture that connects daily work to mission.

04

Credible impact measurement

Funders, investors and regulators want evidence that capital improves borrowers' financial lives and community outcomes. Many institutions report outputs such as loans closed and dollars deployed but struggle to measure outcomes such as business survival, wealth building or housing stability. Building impact measurement that is rigorous yet practical is an ongoing challenge.

05

Fair and responsible technology adoption

Digital applications and automated decisioning can improve speed and access, but they can also replicate historical bias or exclude borrowers who need human guidance. Institutions adopting new platforms and AI tools need governance that tests for disparate impact, keeps humans in the loop for complex cases and preserves borrower trust.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Access to fair, affordable capital shapes whether families can buy homes, entrepreneurs can start and grow businesses, and communities can build wealth over generations. CDFIs and mission-driven institutions work to close gaps that mainstream finance has left open, often in communities shaped by historical exclusion from credit. Their effectiveness depends on staff who understand borrowers and on processes designed around borrowers' real lives. Better people practices and human-centered design help these institutions reach more borrowers, build durable relationships and demonstrate the outcomes that attract further capital.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does CDFI consulting include?

CDFI consulting typically covers strategy, operations, talent and impact. Our work includes borrower journey research, mission-driven growth planning, operating model design for scaling lending, talent strategy for specialized roles, impact measurement frameworks and responsible adoption of digital lending tools. We tailor engagements to the institution's size, lending focus and stage of growth, and we build capability within the team throughout.

Do you only work with CDFIs?

No. We also work with credit unions, community banks, minority depository institutions, larger banks building community lending partnerships, and fintechs serving underserved markets. Across these institutions, our focus is the same: understanding the people the institution serves, strengthening the teams that serve them, and designing products and processes that are fair, accessible and sustainable.

How do you approach fair lending in digital transformation?

We treat fairness as a design requirement from the start. That means testing models and processes for disparate impact, reviewing data inputs for proxies of protected characteristics, designing clear explanations for applicants, and keeping human review for complex or borderline cases. We also involve compliance teams and borrowers in testing so that issues surface before launch rather than after.
