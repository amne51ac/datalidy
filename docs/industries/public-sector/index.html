Context

The people side of government & public sector.

Government agencies face a workforce challenge that is both demographic and structural. Large numbers of experienced public servants are approaching retirement, while recruitment is slowed by lengthy hiring processes, rigid classification systems and pay that often trails the private sector for technical roles. Many agencies have critical knowledge held by a few long-tenured employees. At the same time, public servants describe frustration with outdated tools, layers of approval and limited opportunities to grow, all of which affect morale and retention.

Residents experience the result directly. Applying for benefits, permits or licenses can require repeated visits, duplicate forms and long waits, and the burden falls hardest on people with the least time, money or digital access. Language barriers, disability and distrust of government compound the problem. Constituent experience is no longer a nice-to-have: when services are hard to use, eligible people go without support, compliance suffers and trust in public institutions erodes further.

Several forces are pushing modernization forward. Federal and state policy increasingly emphasizes customer experience and reducing administrative burden. Legacy technology is reaching end of life, creating windows for redesign. Agencies are exploring AI for casework and contact centers under heightened expectations for transparency and fairness. Budget cycles and procurement rules shape what is possible. Effective public sector consulting works within these realities, building capability inside government so that improvements last beyond any single administration or contract.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Civil service modernization

Hiring processes can take many months, job classifications may not reflect modern roles, and pay structures make it difficult to compete for digital, data and engineering talent. Modernizing the civil service requires changes to hiring practices, classification, career paths and performance management, often within statutory constraints and collective bargaining agreements.

02

Retirement waves and knowledge loss

Many agencies have a large share of employees eligible for retirement, including those who hold institutional knowledge about programs, regulations and legacy systems. Without workforce planning and deliberate knowledge transfer, departures create service gaps, slow decisions and increase reliance on contractors for core functions.

03

Hard-to-navigate public services

Residents often face confusing eligibility rules, repeated document requests, inaccessible forms and disconnected channels. These administrative burdens fall hardest on low-income residents, people with disabilities and those with limited English. Agencies need to see services from the resident's perspective and redesign policy, process and technology together.

04

Digital service delivery that works for everyone

Moving services online can improve access for many residents while excluding others who lack devices, connectivity or digital confidence. Digital transformation that replicates paper processes on screens rarely helps. Agencies need user research, accessible design and an omnichannel approach that keeps phone and in-person options strong.

05

Rebuilding public trust

Trust in government is shaped by everyday encounters with public services as much as by policy decisions. Communities that have experienced unfair treatment are understandably wary. Rebuilding trust requires transparent decision-making, meaningful community engagement and services that treat residents with dignity, all of which depend on the people and culture inside agencies.

    Resident-centered service design

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Government touches nearly every part of people's lives: safety, health, education, income support, infrastructure and civic participation. When public services are hard to use, the people most in need are the most likely to be excluded. When public servants are under-supported, service quality and trust both suffer. Better people practices inside agencies, combined with services designed around residents' real experiences, help government deliver on its promises more fairly and efficiently. That strengthens public trust, and it ensures that public resources reach the people they are meant to serve.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does your public sector consulting focus on?

Our public sector consulting focuses on the people side of government: the workforce that delivers services and the residents who use them. We support workforce planning, civil service modernization, change management, service design, community engagement and accessible digital services. We work with federal, state, county and municipal agencies, as well as public authorities and special districts.

How do you work within procurement and budget constraints?

We structure engagements in phases that align with budget cycles and can scale up or down. We prioritize quick, visible improvements that build momentum while laying groundwork for longer-term change. We also focus on building internal capability, through training, playbooks and coaching, so that agency staff can continue the work without ongoing reliance on outside support.

Do you work with labor unions?

Yes. Public sector workforce changes often involve collective bargaining agreements, and union partners bring essential knowledge of frontline work. We engage labor representatives early, share research transparently and design changes that address employee concerns. Involving unions from the start tends to produce better solutions and smoother implementation than consulting them only after decisions are made.
