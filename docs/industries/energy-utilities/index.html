Context

The people side of energy & utilities.

Utilities and energy companies depend on highly skilled people working in demanding, often hazardous conditions. Lineworkers, plant operators, field technicians, engineers and control room staff keep essential services running around the clock. Many organizations face a large wave of retirements among experienced employees, while competition for skilled trades and engineering talent is intense. Building the workforce for new technologies such as grid modernization, renewables, storage and electrification requires new skills that existing training systems were not designed to produce.

Customers experience utilities through bills, outages, service connections and contact centers. Rising rates and extreme weather events have made customer trust more fragile. Low-income households face energy burden and shutoff risk, and many are unaware of assistance programs. Communities affected by new infrastructure, from transmission lines to solar and wind projects, want early and genuine engagement. Communities that have historically borne the pollution burdens of energy production are pressing for a just transition that shares benefits fairly.

Several forces are reshaping the sector at once. Decarbonization goals, federal and state incentives, and rising electricity demand from data centers and electrification are driving major capital programs. Wildfires, storms and heat waves are testing infrastructure and emergency response. Regulators are scrutinizing affordability, equity and performance. Utility workforce consulting must help organizations build the talent, culture and community relationships needed to deliver this transition safely, reliably and fairly, while keeping essential services affordable for every household.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Retirements and skilled trades pipelines

Large numbers of experienced lineworkers, operators and technicians are approaching retirement, taking critical knowledge with them. Apprenticeships take years to produce fully qualified workers, and competition for skilled trades is strong. Utilities need workforce plans that anticipate retirements, accelerate knowledge transfer and broaden recruitment into communities historically underrepresented in the trades.

02

New skills for the energy transition

Grid modernization, distributed energy resources, battery storage and digital operations require skills in data, controls, cybersecurity and new technologies. Many existing employees want to grow into these areas but lack clear pathways. Utilities must define future roles and build reskilling programs alongside traditional apprenticeships.

03

Safety culture under pressure

Utility work is inherently hazardous, and safety is central to culture. Yet storm response, aging infrastructure, contractor reliance and production pressures can erode safe practices. Sustaining a strong safety culture requires leaders who listen, workers who feel safe to stop work and reporting systems that drive learning rather than blame.

04

Customer trust and affordability

Rising bills, outages and complex rate structures strain customer trust. Low-income customers face energy burden and shutoff risk, and many eligible households do not enroll in assistance programs. Improving the customer experience requires redesigning billing, communication, outage response and assistance enrollment around real customer needs.

05

Community engagement on infrastructure

New transmission lines, generation projects and facilities often face local opposition, particularly when communities feel excluded from planning. Historically burdened communities seek a fair share of the transition's benefits. Utilities and developers need early, genuine engagement and benefit-sharing arrangements that build support rather than resistance.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Reliable, affordable, clean energy and water are foundations of health, economic opportunity and dignity. The energy transition is one of the largest infrastructure efforts of our time, and its success depends on a skilled workforce and public trust. Utilities that develop talent from their own communities, keep workers safe and engage customers and neighbors with respect are better able to deliver the transition fairly. Better people practices help ensure that the benefits of cleaner energy, including good jobs and lower burdens, reach the communities that need them most.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does utility workforce consulting include?

Utility workforce consulting addresses how electric, gas, water and clean energy organizations recruit, develop, retain and lead their workforce. Our work includes strategic workforce planning, skilled trades pipeline development, reskilling for new technologies, safety culture assessment, leadership development and change management for large capital programs. We also support customer experience and community engagement work that depends on frontline staff.

How do you help utilities prepare for large retirement waves?

We start by identifying critical roles and the specific knowledge at risk, using retirement eligibility data and interviews with experienced employees. We then design knowledge transfer approaches such as mentoring, job shadowing and documented procedures, accelerate apprenticeship pipelines, and develop recruitment partnerships that broaden the talent pool. Plans are tied to capital programs so workforce needs match investment timelines.

Can you support community engagement for energy projects?

Yes. We design engagement processes for transmission, generation, storage and other infrastructure projects that begin early and reach affected residents, including historically burdened communities. We help project teams understand local priorities, address concerns transparently and develop community benefit approaches. Early, genuine engagement tends to reduce conflict and produces projects better suited to local needs.
