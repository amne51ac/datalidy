Context

The people side of healthcare & life sciences.

Healthcare depends on people more than almost any other sector. Clinical quality, safety and patient experience are produced in thousands of daily interactions between nurses, physicians, technicians, support staff and the patients they serve. Yet many health systems are operating with fatigued teams, persistent vacancies in nursing and allied health, and a growing reliance on travel and agency staff that raises costs and weakens team cohesion. Burnout is no longer an individual wellness issue; it is a systems issue rooted in workload, documentation burden and work design.

Outside the organization, patients navigate fragmented journeys across scheduling, referrals, billing and follow-up care. Communities with the greatest health needs often face the greatest access barriers, from transportation and language to mistrust built over generations. Health equity commitments are now common, but turning them into measurable change requires disaggregated data, community partnership and services designed with the people who use them, not merely for them, with community members treated as partners whose knowledge carries real weight.

Several forces are reshaping the sector at once. Margin pressure is pushing consolidation and new care models, including value-based contracts that reward outcomes rather than volume. Care is moving into homes, retail clinics and virtual settings, changing what roles are needed and where. Life sciences companies face scrutiny on access, pricing and trial diversity. AI tools promise to reduce administrative burden but also raise questions about trust and workflow. Healthcare workforce consulting must connect these forces to the daily reality of the people who deliver care.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Clinician burnout and moral injury

Physicians and nurses report exhaustion driven by heavy documentation, staffing gaps, inefficient workflows and the distress of being unable to provide the care they believe patients deserve. Resilience programs alone do not address these causes. Health systems need to identify which work design, scheduling and leadership factors are driving burnout in specific units.

02

Nurse staffing and retention

Vacancies in nursing and allied health roles strain remaining staff, increase overtime and push organizations toward expensive contract labor. Early-career nurses are especially likely to leave within their first years. Retention depends on onboarding, preceptorship, scheduling flexibility, career pathways and the quality of frontline nurse managers, many of whom have very large spans of control.

03

Fragmented patient experience

Patients encounter disconnected scheduling, unclear billing, long waits and handoffs where information is lost. Experience scores capture symptoms but rarely reveal root causes. Improving the patient journey requires seeing the service end to end, including the staff workflows and systems behind each touchpoint, and designing changes with patients and frontline teams together.

04

Translating health equity into action

Many organizations have adopted health equity goals but lack the data and practices to act on them. Outcome and experience data are often not disaggregated by race, language, disability or geography. Community partnerships may be informal or transactional. Meaningful progress requires measurement, accountable leadership and services co-designed with the communities most affected.

05

Integration after consolidation

Mergers and affiliations bring together hospitals, physician groups and clinics with different cultures, governance structures and clinical practices. Clinicians may feel decisions are being made far from the bedside. Without deliberate integration of leadership, culture and care standards, consolidation can erode trust and engagement rather than producing the coordinated care it promised.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Healthcare exists to protect and restore health, and its capacity to do so rests on the people who deliver care. When clinicians are exhausted and understaffed, patient safety and experience decline, and the communities with the fewest alternatives feel it most. Better people practices, including humane work design, capable frontline leadership and fair access to career growth, strengthen the workforce that patients rely on. Designing services with patients and communities, rather than around them, helps close gaps in access and outcomes and rebuilds the trust that effective care requires.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What does healthcare workforce consulting include?

Healthcare workforce consulting addresses how health organizations attract, develop, deploy and retain clinical and non-clinical staff. It typically includes burnout and engagement diagnostics, retention analytics, workforce planning, leadership development for nurse managers and physician leaders, and work redesign. The goal is a workforce that can deliver safe, high-quality care sustainably, not just a short-term reduction in vacancies.

How do you measure clinician burnout without adding survey fatigue?

We use short, validated instruments sparingly and pair them with existing operational data such as overtime, schedule changes, sick time, after-hours documentation and turnover. Targeted interviews and observation fill in context. This approach gives leaders a clear picture of burnout drivers without asking already stretched clinicians to complete lengthy surveys that rarely lead to visible change.

Do you work with life sciences companies as well as providers?

Yes. For life sciences organizations, we focus on leadership and culture during growth or integration, workforce planning for scientific and commercial roles, and patient-centered design of support programs and trial experiences. We also help teams engage patient communities in research and access strategies, an increasingly important expectation from regulators, payers and the public.
