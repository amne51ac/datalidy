Context

The people side of private equity & portfolio companies.

Private equity investors evaluate businesses under time pressure and with incomplete information. Financial diligence shows what happened. Customer and commercial diligence should explain why, and whether it will continue. Behavioral due diligence examines how customers actually buy, renew, expand and churn, using transaction, product and contract data alongside interviews. It distinguishes durable loyalty from inertia and reveals whether growth depends on a few behaviors that are fragile or on habits that are well established.

The management team is often the largest variable in an investment thesis. Investors want to know how leaders make decisions under pressure, whether the team can scale and how it will respond to the demands of a new ownership model. Traditional assessments rely on interviews and references, which can miss the behavioral patterns that matter. Structured management team assessment, grounded in psychometrics and observation, gives deal teams clearer evidence and gives leaders a fair, development-oriented starting point.

After close, value creation plans set ambitious goals for revenue growth, margin improvement and operational change. Many plans depend on people doing things differently: sales teams adopting new processes, customers moving to new products and leaders aligning across functions. Data across portfolio companies is often inconsistent, making progress hard to see. Investors and operating partners who treat these as behavioral challenges, with predictive KPIs and tested interventions, can track value creation earlier and course-correct before results slip.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Customer diligence built on surveys alone

Diligence timelines often lead to customer surveys and reference calls that describe stated satisfaction. Satisfied customers can still churn, and dissatisfied customers may stay because switching is hard. Without analysis of actual behavior, such as usage trends, renewal patterns and purchase concentration, investors may misread the durability of revenue.

02

Management risk that is hard to see

Deal teams form views of leaders through limited meetings in which executives present their best selves. Behavioral patterns under pressure, team dynamics and gaps in capability are difficult to assess in that setting. Misjudging management fit is costly, and discovering it after close slows value creation and strains relationships.

03

Value creation plans that depend on behavior change

Plans assume price increases will hold, cross-sell will grow and new systems will be adopted. Each assumption depends on how customers, employees and leaders behave. When plans treat these as operational tasks rather than behavioral challenges, adoption lags and expected value arrives late or not at all.

04

Lagging, inconsistent portfolio metrics

Portfolio companies often report different metrics, defined in different ways, from systems of varying quality. Operating partners see financial results after the quarter closes without leading indicators of customer health or team performance. Comparing companies and identifying where to intervene is difficult.

05

Leadership strain during transformation

Ownership transitions bring new expectations, reporting demands and pace. Founders and long-tenured executives may struggle to adapt, and leadership teams may fragment under pressure. Without support, turnover in key roles can disrupt the plan, while the remaining leaders carry heavier loads with limited capacity for strategic work.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Private equity ownership affects employees, customers and communities, not only investors. Behavioral science contributes to better outcomes for all of them by grounding investment decisions in evidence about real customer value, assessing leaders fairly and supporting them through change. Value creation that depends on understanding customers and developing people tends to be more durable than value extracted through short-term cost cutting. When investors measure the behaviors behind performance, they can build companies that serve customers better, support employees through transitions and leave stronger organizations behind at exit.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

What is behavioral due diligence?

Behavioral due diligence examines how a target company's customers and leaders actually behave, using transaction, product and contract data, interviews and structured assessment. It complements financial and traditional commercial diligence by explaining the behaviors behind revenue and leadership performance, helping investors judge whether results are durable and where value creation opportunities lie.

Can this work within a typical deal timeline?

Yes. We scope diligence to the time and data available, focusing on the questions most material to the investment thesis. Where full data access is limited, we prioritize the analyses and interviews that provide the clearest signal. Findings are delivered in a format deal teams can use directly in investment committee discussions.

How is management team assessment handled with leaders?

Assessment is conducted transparently and respectfully, with clear explanation of purpose and process. We use validated instruments and structured methods to reduce bias. After close, findings are typically shared with leaders as a development foundation, so the assessment supports their success rather than serving only as an evaluation for the deal team.
