Banking & Financial Services

Context

The people side of banking & financial services.

Financial decisions are shaped by emotion, habit, attention and trust as much as by interest rates and fees. Customers delay opening savings accounts, avoid looking at balances when worried, accept default options and stay with institutions out of inertia. These patterns matter both to customer wellbeing and to institutional performance. Behavioral science in banking offers a way to understand them and to design products and experiences that help people act on intentions they already hold, such as saving more or borrowing wisely.

Inside banks and credit unions, the people problems are equally significant. Branch staff, relationship managers and contact center teams are asked to shift from transactions to advice, often with incentives and tools built for an earlier era. Digital and branch channels compete instead of complementing each other. Customer data is plentiful, yet it sits in core banking, card, digital banking, CRM and marketing platforms that rarely produce an integrated view of behavior. Leaders make decisions about relationship deepening on assumptions rather than tested evidence.

Current forces intensify the challenge: digital-first competitors, rising expectations for personalized service, growing attention to financial health and fair treatment, and AI tools that can personalize outreach at scale. Each of these creates opportunities and risks. Institutions that apply behavioral science carefully can raise digital adoption, deepen relationships and support financial wellbeing while maintaining the trust that distinguishes a responsible financial partner. Those that apply it carelessly risk eroding that trust quickly.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Digital adoption that plateaus

Many customers open a mobile app once, then return to branches or call centers for tasks they could complete digitally. Adoption stalls when features feel unfamiliar, security steps are confusing or customers lack confidence. Campaigns that simply promote the app rarely change habits formed over years of in-person banking.

02

Relationship deepening that feels like selling

Institutions want customers to hold more products, but customers resist when offers feel irrelevant or pushy. Cross-sell programs built on product targets can erode trust. The behavioral question is how to recognize genuine needs and present options at moments when customers are ready to consider them.

03

Customer financial wellbeing as a measure

Many institutions want to support customer financial health, but measuring it is difficult and connecting it to business outcomes is harder still. Without credible measures, financial wellbeing initiatives remain marketing themes rather than design principles that shape products, communications and frontline conversations.

04

Frontline roles in transition

Branch and contact center staff are being asked to advise rather than process, often without clear guidance, coaching or aligned incentives. Many feel uncertain about how to start needs-based conversations. Leaders need a clear picture of the behaviors that define effective advice and a way to develop them consistently.

05

Fragmented customer data

Core banking, card, lending, digital and CRM systems each describe part of a customer's financial life. Without integration around behavioral signals, institutions miss early indicators of attrition, financial stress or readiness for new products. Analysts default to demographic segments that explain little about how customers actually behave.

How we help

Where we focus.

  1. →

    Behavioral segmentation for banking

    We analyze transaction, channel and product data to define behavioral types, such as digital-ready customers who still visit branches or savers who avoid planning tools. These segments explain behavior better than demographics and guide where to focus design and outreach. Each segment comes with testable hypotheses about what would help those customers.

    Behavioral Segmentation

  2. →

    Digital banking adoption design

    We study why customers do not adopt digital tools and design interventions around confidence, ability and timing. Examples include assisted first use in branches, simplified security steps and prompts linked to tasks customers already perform. Pilots measure sustained digital behavior rather than downloads, so leaders know which approaches change habits.

    Technology Adoption Strategy

  3. →

    Needs-based relationship deepening

    We help institutions redesign cross-sell around customer needs and life moments, aligning frontline conversations, digital prompts and incentives. The approach draws on the psychology of trust and decision-making and is tested against both product outcomes and customer satisfaction, so deeper relationships are earned rather than pushed.

    Cross-Sell & Sales Process Design

  4. →

    Financial wellbeing measurement

    Applying psychometrics and survey design, we help institutions build credible measures of customer financial wellbeing and link them with behavioral data. These measures support product decisions, communications and frontline coaching, and they give leaders a way to track whether initiatives actually improve customers' financial lives.

    Survey & Measurement Design

  5. →

    Frontline leadership and team coaching

    We coach branch and contact center leaders to develop advice-oriented teams, using observation and data to identify the conversation behaviors that matter. Team coaching builds psychological safety and practice routines so staff can try new approaches, learn from them and improve together over time.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Financial institutions influence whether households can save, borrow responsibly, weather emergencies and plan for the future. Behavioral science advances that social good by designing products and conversations that help people follow through on their own financial intentions, such as building savings or avoiding costly fees. It also brings rigor to measuring financial wellbeing and guards against persuasion that benefits the institution at the customer's expense. Applied responsibly, behavioral science in banking aligns long-term institutional performance with the financial resilience of the customers and communities the institution serves.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

How is behavioral science in banking different from traditional marketing analytics?

Marketing analytics often focuses on who to target and which offer to send. Behavioral science in banking begins with the customer behavior that matters, such as saving regularly or adopting digital tools, and asks what motivation, ability and prompts shape it. Interventions may involve product design, frontline conversations or defaults as well as messaging, and they are tested experimentally.

Do you work with credit unions and community banks?

Yes. Credit unions and community banks often have strong relationships and member-focused missions, along with data spread across core and digital systems. We adapt our methods to their scale, starting with existing data and practical pilots. Their emphasis on member wellbeing aligns naturally with an ethical approach to behavioral design.

How do you ensure behavioral design is used ethically in financial services?

We design to help customers act on their own goals, not to exploit biases for short-term gain. That means transparent choices, defaults that serve customer interests and measures that include customer outcomes alongside institutional ones. We review interventions for potential harm, particularly for financially vulnerable customers, before they are tested or scaled.
