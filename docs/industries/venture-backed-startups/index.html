Context

The people side of venture-backed startups.

Founders of venture-backed startups make high-stakes decisions with limited data. Early traction can be noisy: a handful of enthusiastic customers, a spike from a launch or growth driven by the founder's personal selling. The questions that matter most are behavioral. Which users reach real value, which behaviors predict retention and which customers will expand? Answering them early, from small and messy datasets, determines whether a team builds on solid ground or scales an assumption that has not been tested.

Founder-led leadership is both a strength and a constraint. Founders hold the vision, the customer relationships and many of the decisions. As teams grow from a few people to dozens or hundreds, the habits that worked early, such as deciding everything personally or relying on informal communication, begin to slow the company. New leaders join, culture shifts and conflict surfaces. Startups that develop leadership deliberately are better able to maintain speed and clarity through each stage of growth.

Investors want more than lagging revenue figures. Boards ask for evidence that the product is working, that growth is efficient and that the team can execute the next stage. Many startups report metrics that are easy to count rather than predictive. Current forces, including tighter capital, AI-native competitors and buyers who expect proof of value, raise the bar. Founders and investors who share behavior-based KPIs can have more honest conversations about progress, risk and where support will help most.

Challenges

What leaders are up against.

01

Traction signals buried in noise

Early datasets are small, inconsistent and shaped by founder involvement. Distinguishing genuine product pull from enthusiasm, discounts or one-time launches is hard. Teams may scale go-to-market spending before confirming which user behaviors predict retention, burning capital on growth that does not last.

02

Activation that is assumed, not validated

Startups often define activation by intuition or by convenient product events. Without validation against retention, onboarding is optimized for the wrong behaviors. Teams then struggle to explain why promising sign-ups fail to convert into active, paying customers who expand over time.

03

Founder bottlenecks as the team grows

Founders who made every decision in the early days can become bottlenecks as the company grows. Delegation feels risky, new leaders lack authority and communication that once happened naturally breaks down. The behaviors that built the company must evolve, and founders rarely have time or support to make that shift deliberately.

04

Investor reporting built on vanity metrics

Board decks often feature cumulative users, pipeline totals and top-line growth that look encouraging but say little about durability. When reported metrics do not predict outcomes, investors and founders lose time debating numbers instead of discussing decisions. Trust suffers when results eventually diverge from what dashboards implied.

05

Culture that forms by accident

Startup culture forms quickly through the behaviors leaders model and tolerate. Without attention, norms around feedback, accountability and inclusion develop by accident. Rapid hiring can dilute the qualities that made the early team effective, and cultural problems become harder to change once the company has scaled.

Impact lens

The social good at stake.

Venture-backed startups bring new products, services and jobs into the world, and many address problems in health, education, climate and access to services. Behavioral science helps them do so responsibly: building products people genuinely value rather than products engineered for compulsive use, measuring progress honestly and developing leaders who create healthy workplaces as they scale. For investors, behavioral evidence supports better capital allocation toward companies that create real value. Founders who understand behavior are better equipped to build organizations and products that improve people's lives over the long term.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

When should venture-backed startups invest in behavioral science?

Earlier than many expect. The most valuable time is when a startup is defining activation, preparing to scale go-to-market spending or building its first leadership team. At those points, small investments in identifying behavioral signals and developing leaders can prevent costly mistakes. Engagements are scoped to fit startup budgets and timelines.

Can you find meaningful signals in a small dataset?

Often, yes. Early datasets require careful methods: combining product data with qualitative research, focusing on cohorts and treating findings as hypotheses to test rather than settled conclusions. The goal is not statistical certainty at small scale but sharper, testable questions that help founders learn faster with each release and decide where to invest next.

Do you work with investors as well as founders?

Yes. We support venture investors in evaluating customer traction and team dynamics, and we work with portfolio companies after investment. When founders and investors share behavior-based KPIs, board conversations become more productive. We are clear about roles and confidentiality when working with both sides of the relationship, and we agree in writing what is shared with whom.
