What Is Behavioral Signals Design?

Most organizations measure outcomes after they happen. Behavioral Signals Design starts from the outcome, finds the few behaviors that predict it, and builds KPIs that tell leaders where to act before results arrive.

Key takeaways

  • Behavioral Signals Design treats performance as behavior and looks for the observable actions that predict an outcome before it happens.
  • The method focuses on a few key behavioral moments, called conversion points, rather than tracking everything a system can count.
  • Behavioral types group people by what they do, which predicts future action better than who they are on paper.
  • Untested assumptions become explicit hypotheses, and each hypothesis is paired with a KPI and an experiment that can confirm or reject it.

A definition of Behavioral Signals Design

Behavioral Signals Design is a structured method for building more predictive KPIs. It begins with a simple premise: performance is behavior. Customer conversion, technology adoption, patient adherence, employee engagement and leadership effectiveness are all things people do, and the things people do leave traces in data. Those traces are behavioral signals. Most of them sit unused inside large, noisy and unintegrated datasets, spread across a CRM, a product log, a call center platform, a survey tool and a spreadsheet someone maintains by hand. Behavioral Signals Design is the discipline of finding the signals that matter, connecting them to the outcome an organization cares about, and turning them into measures that can be tested and improved.

The word design is deliberate. This is not a reporting exercise or a data warehouse project, although it often improves both. It is closer to how a product designer or a clinical researcher works: define the outcome, understand the people involved, form hypotheses about what drives their behavior, and build instruments that can tell you whether you are right. The result is a small set of behavior-based KPIs, each tied to a specific moment in a journey, each paired with a clear test that can move it. Leaders get measures they can act on, rather than measures they can only watch.

Why conventional KPIs leave leaders guessing

Most dashboards are built from what is easy to count. Revenue, sessions, sign-ups, net promoter scores and headcount are useful, but they are lagging measures. They report what already happened and say little about why, or about what will happen next. When a lagging number turns, leaders are left to infer causes, and inference under pressure tends to fall back on intuition, precedent or the loudest voice in the room. Predicting behavior becomes an expensive guess.

A second problem is fragmentation. The data needed to explain an outcome usually lives in systems that were never designed to talk to each other. Marketing sees the campaign, sales sees the pipeline, service sees the complaint, and no one sees the full sequence of behavior that a single customer, patient or employee actually went through. Gaps between systems get filled with assumptions, and those assumptions harden into strategy without ever being tested. Behavioral Signals Design addresses both problems at once: it reconstructs the journey across systems and replaces silent assumptions with explicit, measurable hypotheses.

  • Lagging metrics describe results but rarely explain the behavior that produced them.
  • Unintegrated systems hide the full sequence of actions behind an outcome.
  • Assumptions fill data gaps quietly and are rarely recognized as assumptions.
  • Vanity metrics rise and fall without changing any decision a leader makes.

The five moves of the method

Behavioral Signals Design follows a consistent sequence, adapted to the question and the data available. It starts from the outcome that matters, not from the data that happens to exist. That reversal sounds minor, yet it changes everything downstream, because it forces a team to ask which behaviors would have to change for the outcome to change. From there the method moves from mapping to diagnosis to measurement to action.

Each move produces something concrete. A journey map shows where people go and where they drop away. A short list of conversion points shows where the outcome is actually decided. A set of behavioral types shows how different groups move through those moments. An assumptions register shows what the organization believes but has not verified. Finally, a KPI and experiment plan shows what to measure, what to change and how to tell whether the change worked.

  • Start from the outcome: conversion, adoption, retention, adherence, satisfaction or performance.
  • Map the full journey and the data around it, across systems that were never integrated.
  • Identify the few conversion points and the behavioral types that actually drive the outcome.
  • Surface untested assumptions and rewrite each one as a testable hypothesis.
  • Define predictive, behavior-based KPIs with A/B tests or pilots designed to improve them.

Conversion points and behavioral types

A conversion point is a behavioral moment where the outcome is effectively decided. In a purchase journey it might be the first time a prospect configures a product rather than browsing it. In a digital health program it might be the second login, not the first. In a leadership team it might be whether a decision is revisited after a meeting ends. Conversion points are rarely the moments an organization already tracks, and they are seldom obvious from aggregate reports. They emerge when behavioral sequences are examined over time and compared between people who reached the outcome and people who did not.

Behavioral types are segments defined by what people do rather than who they are. Two customers with identical demographics can behave in entirely different ways, while two very different customers can follow the same pattern. Grouping people by behavior, such as how quickly they act, which channels they rely on, or where they hesitate, produces segments that predict future action far better than age, title or zip code. Together, conversion points and behavioral types tell an organization where to intervene and for whom, which is the foundation of any useful KPI.

From assumptions to predictive KPIs and tests

Every organization runs on assumptions: that customers drop out because of price, that clinicians resist a tool because of training, that a team underperforms because of skill. Some of these are correct. Many are not, and the cost of a wrong assumption compounds every quarter it goes unexamined. Behavioral Signals Design makes these beliefs visible by asking, at each gap in the data, what the organization is assuming in order to fill it. Each assumption is then rewritten as a hypothesis with a clear prediction that the data or an experiment can confirm or reject.

Predictive KPIs follow from validated hypotheses. A good predictive KPI measures a behavior at a conversion point, moves before the outcome moves, and is something a team can influence directly. Each KPI is paired with a specific intervention and a test, typically an A/B test where volume allows or a structured pilot where it does not. This turns measurement into a learning loop. Instead of asking why last quarter disappointed, leaders can ask which behavior is shifting now, and what the next experiment should be.

  • A predictive KPI measures a behavior, not just a result.
  • It moves before the outcome moves, giving leaders time to act.
  • It sits at a conversion point the team can directly influence.
  • It comes with a defined test that can show whether an intervention worked.

How Datalidy applies Behavioral Signals Design

Behavioral Signals Design is the analytical core of Datalidy's engagement method: Observe, Signal, Design, Test, Scale. In Observe, the team uses interviews, contextual inquiry, user testing and journey mapping to understand behavior in context, because data alone rarely explains motive. In Signal, behavioral data analysis identifies conversion points and behavioral types and defines the predictive KPIs. In Design, those insights shape interventions through behavioral design, experience and service design, messaging and coaching plans. Test validates them with experiments, A/B tests, pilots and usability testing. Scale embeds what works through implementation support, dashboards, capability building and leadership and team coaching.

The method draws on clinical and health psychology, behavioral medicine, human-computer interaction, persuasive technology and psychometrics, and it applies equally to people outside an organization and people inside it. The same approach that clarifies why customers stall before purchase can clarify why a leadership team stalls before a decision. It has been used in settings ranging from an automotive brand's owner experience to a mortgage lender's sales process to digital health programs. The aim is consistent: read the signals, design the behavior and measure what matters, so that predicting performance no longer depends on an expensive guess.

Frequently asked questions

Is Behavioral Signals Design the same as behavioral analytics?

No. Behavioral analytics describes the analysis of user actions in data. Behavioral Signals Design is a broader method that begins with the outcome, combines qualitative research with data analysis, surfaces untested assumptions, and ends with predictive KPIs paired with experiments. Analytics is one input. The output is a set of measures and tests that tell leaders where to intervene, for whom, and how to know whether the intervention worked.

What data do we need before starting?

Usually less than organizations expect, and usually more than they realize they have. Most engagements begin with existing systems such as CRM records, product or web logs, service data and surveys, even if they are incomplete and unintegrated. Part of the method is mapping what exists, identifying the gaps, and naming the assumptions filling those gaps. Qualitative research often supplies the context that the data cannot.

Does this only apply to customer behavior?

No. Any outcome that depends on what people do can be approached this way. Datalidy applies the method to customer conversion and retention, technology adoption, patient engagement and adherence, employee engagement, and leadership and team performance. Inside organizations the signals might come from meeting patterns, workflow systems or pulse surveys rather than purchase data, but the logic of conversion points, behavioral types and testable hypotheses is the same.
