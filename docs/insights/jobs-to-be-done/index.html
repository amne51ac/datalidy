Jobs to Be Done

Jobs to be done is a way of understanding demand by asking what progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. It shifts attention from customer demographics and product features to the underlying need that drives choice.

Key takeaways

  • Jobs to be done holds that people hire products and services to make progress in specific circumstances.
  • Clayton Christensen popularized the theory, with contributions from practitioners such as Bob Moesta and Tony Ulwick.
  • Jobs have functional, emotional and social dimensions, and competition includes any solution that does the same job.
  • The framework applies to services, internal programs and employee offerings as well as consumer products.

What jobs to be done means

Jobs to be done is a theory of customer motivation and a set of methods for uncovering it. Its central claim is that people do not buy products or services for their own sake. They hire them to get a job done: to make progress in a particular circumstance. A commuter who buys a morning milkshake, in the well-known example Clayton Christensen used, is not expressing a preference for dairy. They are hiring something to make a long, dull drive more bearable and to stay full until lunch. Understanding that job explains the purchase better than the commuter's age, income or stated preferences.

Christensen, the Harvard Business School professor best known for the theory of disruptive innovation, developed and popularized jobs to be done as a way to explain why so many innovations fail. Practitioners including Bob Moesta, who worked with Christensen on early applications, and Tony Ulwick, who developed an outcome-driven approach to innovation, built rigorous methods around the idea. Although their versions differ in emphasis, all share the conviction that the job, not the customer or the product, should be the primary unit of analysis for strategy and innovation.

The dimensions of a job

A job is rarely only functional. Most jobs have three dimensions that together explain why people choose one solution over another. The functional dimension describes the practical task to be accomplished. The emotional dimension describes how people want to feel, or avoid feeling, while doing it. The social dimension describes how they want to be perceived by others. A small business owner hiring an accounting service wants accurate books, but also peace of mind and credibility with lenders and investors. A solution that addresses only the functional job may lose to one that addresses all three.

Jobs are also defined by circumstance. The same person may have very different jobs depending on time, place and situation. This is why demographic segmentation often fails to predict behavior: two people with identical profiles may be hiring for different jobs, while two very different people may share the same one. Defining the circumstance precisely, including what triggered the search for a solution, is often the most valuable part of jobs to be done research.

  • Functional: the practical task the person needs to accomplish.
  • Emotional: how the person wants to feel during and after.
  • Social: how the person wants to be seen by others.
  • Circumstance: the context and trigger that make the job urgent.

Rethinking competition and value

Jobs to be done changes how organizations think about competition. If the job is the unit of analysis, then competitors include any solution a person might hire to do that job, including workarounds, doing nothing, or products from entirely different categories. A bank's mobile app may compete not only with other banks but with spreadsheets, a relative's advice or the decision to postpone a financial task. Seeing competition this way often reveals threats and opportunities that category-based analysis misses entirely.

The framework also clarifies what value means. Features matter only insofar as they help people make progress on the job. Many products and services accumulate features that do little for the core job while neglecting the frustrations people actually experience. Jobs to be done research identifies the forces that push people toward a new solution, the forces that pull them, and the anxieties and habits that hold them back. Addressing these forces, especially the anxieties of switching, is frequently more effective than adding capability.

How to uncover jobs to be done

The most common method is the switch interview, an in-depth conversation with someone who has recently started using a solution or stopped using one. Rather than asking what people want, which tends to produce speculation, interviewers reconstruct the actual timeline of the decision: the first thought that something needed to change, the moments of passive and active searching, the options considered, the deciding event and the experience afterward. Concrete stories reveal the real job, the circumstances and the forces at play far better than surveys or focus groups can.

Interview findings are then synthesized into job statements and patterns. Some practitioners go further, quantifying desired outcomes through surveys that measure how important each outcome is and how well current solutions satisfy it. This outcome-driven approach helps prioritize where to innovate. Whatever the method, discipline matters: interviewers must avoid leading questions, focus on past behavior rather than hypothetical preferences, and keep asking what progress the person was trying to make in their particular situation.

Applying jobs to be done beyond products

Although jobs to be done emerged in product innovation, it is equally powerful for services, public programs and internal offerings. A workforce development program can ask what job a participant is hiring it for: a credential, a route to a specific employer, structure during a difficult period or a sense of momentum. A public agency can ask what residents are really trying to accomplish when they contact it. An HR function can ask what job a manager is hiring a performance process to do, which is often quite different from what the process was designed for.

Applied internally, the framework helps organizations redesign employee programs around real needs. Benefits, learning platforms, internal tools and policies all compete for employees' limited time and attention, and they are frequently abandoned in favor of workarounds. Understanding the jobs employees are trying to get done, and the circumstances in which they struggle, leads to services that people actually use and value, rather than programs that look comprehensive on paper but go largely unused in practice.

How Datalidy applies jobs to be done

Datalidy uses jobs to be done as part of our customer strategy, service design and employee experience work. Within our Listen, Measure, Design, Deliver, Sustain methodology, switch interviews and contextual research help us listen for the real progress customers, residents, participants and employees are seeking. We then measure the importance and satisfaction of key outcomes to identify where unmet needs are largest and where current offerings fall short. This pairing of stories and numbers keeps the work grounded.

Those insights inform the design of services, programs and strategies built around real jobs rather than assumed preferences. We help teams translate findings into clear priorities, prototype and test new approaches, and track whether people are making the progress they came for. For organizations whose growth or mission depends on serving people well, jobs to be done provides a disciplined, evidence-based way to understand what those people actually need and why they choose as they do.

Frequently asked questions

Who created the jobs to be done framework?

Jobs to be done is most closely associated with Clayton Christensen of Harvard Business School, who popularized the theory in his writing on innovation. Practitioners including Bob Moesta, who collaborated with Christensen and developed the switch interview approach, and Tony Ulwick, who developed outcome-driven innovation, shaped the methods used today. The underlying idea also echoes earlier marketing thought, such as Theodore Levitt's observation that people want the hole, not the drill.

What is a jobs to be done interview?

A jobs to be done interview, often called a switch interview, is an in-depth conversation with someone who recently adopted or abandoned a product or service. The interviewer reconstructs the timeline of the decision, from the first trigger to the purchase and subsequent experience, focusing on concrete events rather than opinions. The goal is to uncover the circumstances, the progress the person sought, and the forces that encouraged or inhibited switching.

How is jobs to be done different from personas?

Personas describe types of people, often with demographic and attitudinal details. Jobs to be done describes the progress people are trying to make in specific circumstances, independent of who they are. The same person may have different jobs in different situations, and very different people may share a job. Many teams use both, grounding personas in research on jobs so that they reflect real motivations rather than assumed characteristics.
