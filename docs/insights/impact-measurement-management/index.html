Impact Measurement & Management (IMM)

Impact measurement and management is the practice of identifying, measuring and acting on the effects an organization or investment has on people and the planet. The emphasis on management is deliberate: the goal is not to count impact but to improve it.

Key takeaways

  • IMM is the ongoing practice of measuring effects on people and the planet and using that evidence to improve decisions.
  • The Impact Management Project's five dimensions, what, who, how much, contribution and risk, provide a widely used shared language.
  • Frameworks such as IRIS+ and the Operating Principles for Impact Management support consistent metrics and practice.

What impact measurement and management means

Impact measurement and management, commonly abbreviated as IMM, is the process by which organizations and investors identify the effects of their activities on people and the planet, measure those effects, and use the evidence to make better decisions. It is used by impact investors, foundations, nonprofits, social enterprises, development finance institutions and, increasingly, mainstream companies and asset managers. The term emphasizes management because measurement alone has limited value. Data that is collected for reports but never informs strategy, resource allocation or program design does little to improve outcomes for anyone.

IMM covers the full cycle of setting impact goals, defining indicators, collecting and analyzing data, interpreting results with stakeholders, and adjusting strategy and operations in response. It considers positive and negative effects, intended and unintended, and it applies across the life of an investment or program. A mature IMM system treats impact performance with the same discipline as financial performance: it sets targets, monitors progress, reports to governance bodies and holds leaders accountable for results.

The five dimensions of impact

A major step toward a shared language for impact came from the Impact Management Project, a multi-stakeholder forum that convened practitioners to build consensus on how to describe and manage impact. Its work, now stewarded by Impact Frontiers, defined five dimensions of impact that together provide a complete picture of an outcome. Rather than asking simply whether impact occurred, the dimensions ask what outcome occurred, who experienced it, how significant it was, what the organization contributed, and how likely it is that impact differs from expectations.

These dimensions help organizations avoid common errors, such as reporting large numbers of people reached without considering depth of change, or claiming outcomes that would have occurred anyway. They also allow investors to compare and construct portfolios by the type of impact each investment contributes. Because they are descriptive rather than prescriptive, the five dimensions work alongside other frameworks and metrics. Many investors use them during due diligence to set expectations, during the holding period to monitor performance, and at exit to assess what impact was achieved and whether it is likely to endure.

  • What: the outcome and whether it is positive or negative for people or planet.
  • Who: the stakeholders experiencing the outcome and how underserved they are.
  • How much: the scale, depth and duration of the outcome.
  • Contribution: the difference made compared with what would have happened anyway.
  • Risk: the likelihood that impact differs from what was expected.

Frameworks and standards

Several frameworks support IMM practice. IRIS+, managed by the Global Impact Investing Network, provides a catalog of standardized metrics and core metric sets organized by impact theme, helping organizations select credible indicators and compare results. The Operating Principles for Impact Management, launched with the International Finance Corporation, set out a framework for integrating impact considerations across the investment lifecycle, from strategy and origination through monitoring and exit, with independent verification. The Sustainable Development Goals often provide a high-level reference for aligning impact objectives.

Other tools address specific needs. A theory of change articulates how activities are expected to lead to outcomes. Social return on investment values outcomes in monetary terms. Double materiality assessments, required in some jurisdictions, identify the most significant impacts for reporting. These frameworks are complementary rather than competing. The practical challenge is to choose a coherent combination that fits the organization's strategy and capacity, rather than adopting every framework and drowning in metrics.

Building an effective IMM system

An effective IMM system starts with clear impact objectives, grounded in a theory of change that explains how the organization's activities are expected to create outcomes. From there, organizations select a focused set of indicators, prioritizing those that inform real decisions. A small number of well-chosen outcome indicators, complemented by output and process measures, is more useful than a long list of metrics that no one has time to analyze. Data collection should be proportionate, integrated with operational systems where possible, and designed to minimize burden on participants and partners.

Analysis and interpretation are where measurement becomes management. Results should be reviewed regularly by people with authority to act, compared against targets and expectations, and discussed with stakeholders who can explain what the numbers mean. Findings should flow into decisions about strategy, budgets, program design, investment selection and engagement with portfolio companies, and the system itself should be reviewed and improved periodically as needs change. When results fall short, a good system helps teams diagnose whether the problem lies in delivery, in the underlying theory of change or in the data itself.

  • Define impact objectives anchored in a theory of change.
  • Select a focused set of decision-relevant indicators.
  • Collect proportionate data, including direct stakeholder feedback.
  • Review results regularly with decision makers and stakeholders.
  • Link findings to resources, incentives and governance.
  • Report transparently on both progress and shortfalls.

Centering stakeholder voice

The people experiencing outcomes are often the best judges of whether those outcomes are meaningful. Yet many IMM systems rely heavily on administrative data and organizational self-assessment, with little direct input from the people affected. Approaches such as lean data, popularized by the impact investor Acumen, and constituent feedback practices show that it is possible to gather timely, affordable and decision-relevant feedback directly from customers, beneficiaries, employees and communities. Short, well-designed surveys delivered by phone or text can reach people who would never attend a focus group.

Centering stakeholder voice improves IMM in several ways. It surfaces outcomes, including negative ones, that internal metrics miss. It helps organizations understand why results occur, not just whether they occur. It enables disaggregation, revealing which groups benefit most and which are left behind. And it reflects a principle of accountability: those whose lives an organization seeks to improve should have a say in how improvement is defined and judged. For investors, stakeholder feedback from portfolio companies can provide early signals of impact and business risk.

Frequently asked questions

What are the five dimensions of impact?

The five dimensions, developed through the Impact Management Project, are what, who, how much, contribution and risk. What describes the outcome and whether it is positive or negative. Who describes the stakeholders experiencing it. How much covers scale, depth and duration. Contribution considers what difference the organization made compared with what would have happened anyway. Risk assesses the likelihood that impact differs from expectations. Together they provide a structured, comparable description of impact.

What is the difference between impact measurement and impact management?

Impact measurement is the process of collecting and analyzing evidence about the effects of an organization or investment on people and the planet. Impact management is the use of that evidence to make decisions, set targets, allocate resources and improve outcomes. Measurement without management produces reports; management without measurement relies on assumption. The combined practice ensures that evidence about impact actually shapes strategy and operations.

How do impact investors measure impact?

Impact investors typically set impact objectives aligned with their strategy, assess expected impact during due diligence, often using the five dimensions of impact, and agree indicators with investees, frequently drawn from IRIS+. They monitor performance throughout the holding period, collect stakeholder feedback where possible, and assess impact at exit. Many align their practices with the Operating Principles for Impact Management and obtain independent verification of their impact management systems.
