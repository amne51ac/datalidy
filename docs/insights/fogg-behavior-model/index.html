The Fogg Behavior Model, Explained

Behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt come together at the same moment. The Fogg Behavior Model turns that idea into a practical diagnostic for products, services, health programs and organizations.

Key takeaways

  • The Fogg Behavior Model holds that behavior occurs when motivation, ability and a prompt converge at the same moment.
  • When a behavior fails to happen, at least one of the three elements is missing, which makes the model a diagnostic tool.
  • Increasing ability by making a behavior easier is often more reliable than trying to raise motivation.
  • The model is most useful when paired with behavioral data that shows where in a journey each element breaks down.

What the Fogg Behavior Model says

The Fogg Behavior Model, developed by B.J. Fogg at Stanford, is usually summarized as B=MAP: behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt converge at the same moment. Motivation is how much a person wants to do the behavior. Ability is how easy the behavior is for that person in that context. A prompt is the cue that says do it now. If any of the three is absent at the moment it matters, the behavior does not occur, however strong the other two may be.

The model is often drawn as a graph with motivation on the vertical axis and ability on the horizontal axis, separated by a curved action line. When the combination of motivation and ability places a person above the line, a prompt will lead to action. When it places them below the line, the same prompt will fail or feel annoying. The shape of that curve captures a practical insight: very easy behaviors need little motivation, and very hard behaviors require a great deal.

Motivation: powerful but unreliable

Fogg describes motivation in terms of core motivators such as seeking pleasure and avoiding pain, hope and fear, and social acceptance and rejection. These forces are real, and good design respects them. The difficulty is that motivation fluctuates. It peaks after a health scare, a new year, an exciting launch or an inspiring offsite, and it declines when routine returns. Strategies that depend on sustaining high motivation tend to work briefly and then fade.

This is why many programs that rely on persuasion alone disappoint. Campaigns, incentives and motivational messages can raise interest for a while, yet behavior often returns to its prior pattern once the push ends. The Fogg Behavior Model does not dismiss motivation. It reframes it as one variable among three, and usually the hardest one to control. Designers and leaders get better results when they ask how to make the behavior possible at ordinary levels of motivation, rather than how to keep people permanently inspired.

Ability: the most reliable lever

Ability in the Fogg Behavior Model is not a fixed trait. It describes how simple a behavior is for a specific person at a specific moment. Fogg identifies several factors that make a behavior harder: it takes too much time, costs too much money, requires physical effort, demands mental effort, conflicts with social norms or breaks an existing routine. Whichever factor is scarcest for that person becomes the limiting one, which Fogg sometimes describes as the weakest link in a simplicity chain.

Making a behavior easier is often the most dependable way to increase it. Removing a form field, pre-filling information, shortening an approval chain, scheduling a follow-up appointment before a patient leaves, or reducing the number of decisions in a process can all raise ability without asking anyone to care more. In practice, many organizations discover that the behavior they want is blocked not by indifference but by friction they had stopped noticing.

  • Time: does the behavior take longer than people have available?
  • Money: does it require spending that feels risky or unaffordable?
  • Physical effort: does it demand travel, waiting or exertion?
  • Mental effort: does it require new learning or complex decisions?
  • Social deviance: does it run against what peers expect?
  • Routine: does it conflict with established habits and schedules?

Prompts: the overlooked trigger

A prompt is the cue to act. Without one, even a motivated and able person may never take the action, simply because nothing reminded them at the right time. Fogg distinguishes between prompts that work in different situations: a facilitator, which makes a behavior easier for someone who is motivated but lacks ability; a spark, which adds motivation for someone who is able but not yet motivated; and a signal, a simple reminder for someone who already has both.

Timing is everything. A prompt that arrives when a person is unable to act, or when motivation is low, tends to be ignored and can erode trust. A prompt delivered at the moment of highest ability and relevance is far more effective. This is where behavioral data becomes essential. Knowing when people are most likely to act, through which channel and in what context, turns prompting from guesswork into design. Too many prompts, poorly timed, create noise that people learn to ignore.

Using the model as a diagnostic

The most practical use of the Fogg Behavior Model is diagnostic. When a desired behavior is not happening, ask three questions in order. Is there a prompt at the right moment? Is the behavior easy enough for this person in this context? Is there enough motivation to act at that level of difficulty? The order matters, because prompts and ability are usually cheaper and more reliable to fix than motivation, and they are often where the real problem lies.

The model also helps teams avoid a common bias: attributing failure to people rather than to design. When customers abandon a process, clinicians skip a tool or employees ignore a new policy, the instinctive explanation is that they do not care enough. The Fogg Behavior Model encourages a more useful question about what in the environment is making the behavior hard or easy to forget. That shift from blame to design is often where improvement begins.

  • Name one specific behavior, not a vague goal.
  • Check for a well-timed prompt before anything else.
  • Find the scarcest ability factor and reduce it.
  • Address motivation last, and match it to the difficulty that remains.

How Datalidy applies the Fogg Behavior Model

Datalidy uses the Fogg Behavior Model as one of several lenses within its Observe, Signal, Design, Test, Scale method. In Observe, interviews and contextual inquiry reveal where motivation, ability or prompts break down in real settings. In Signal, behavioral data analysis locates the conversion points where people stall, so the model can be applied to the moments that actually drive an outcome. In Design, interventions are shaped to add a prompt, remove friction or match a message to genuine motivation, and in Test, A/B tests and pilots show which change made the difference.

The model applies well beyond consumer products. Datalidy uses it in patient engagement and adherence, technology adoption, sales processes and leadership development, where the same three elements govern whether a new behavior takes hold. Fogg's later work on group-level change and on tiny habits also informs how the firm approaches culture and team behavior. Throughout, the aim is ethical: help people do what they already want to do, more easily, rather than pressure them into actions they would not choose.

Frequently asked questions

What does B=MAP stand for?

B=MAP is shorthand for the Fogg Behavior Model: behavior equals motivation, ability and a prompt occurring together. It is not a mathematical equation in the strict sense. It expresses the idea that all three elements must be present at the same moment for a behavior to happen. If one is missing, the behavior does not occur, regardless of how strong the other two are.

How is the Fogg Behavior Model different from nudge theory?

The two are compatible but have different emphases. Nudge theory, associated with Thaler and Sunstein, focuses on how choice architecture influences decisions without restricting options. The Fogg Behavior Model is a broader framework for understanding any behavior through motivation, ability and prompts. Many nudges can be read through the Fogg lens as ways of increasing ability or providing a well-timed prompt.

Can the model be used inside organizations?

Yes. Adopting a new system, running effective meetings, giving feedback or following a revised process are all behaviors governed by motivation, ability and prompts. Leaders often focus on motivation through communication and incentives while neglecting ability and prompts. Applying the model to internal change often reveals simple design fixes, such as clearer workflows or better-timed reminders, that make new behaviors easier to sustain.
