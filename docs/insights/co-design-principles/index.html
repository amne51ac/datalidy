Principles of Co-Design

Co-design is more than asking people for feedback. It is a way of working in which the people affected by a decision help shape it from the start. These co-design principles explain what that requires in practice.

Key takeaways

  • Co-design means sharing decision-making power with the people affected, not only gathering their opinions after options are set.
  • Core co-design principles include shared power, lived experience as expertise, accessibility, reciprocity, and transparency about influence.
  • Good co-design starts before the brief is written, so participants help define the problem as well as the solution.
  • Co-design takes more time up front but reduces the risk of services and policies that fail the people they are for.

What co-design is and is not

Co-design is a participatory approach in which designers, decision makers, and the people affected by a product, service, or policy work together as partners throughout the design process. It grew from participatory design traditions in Scandinavian workplaces, where workers helped shape the technologies that would change their jobs, and it is now widely used in public services, health care, community development, and employee experience. Its central idea is simple: the people who live with a problem hold expertise that professionals lack, and solutions improve when that expertise shapes decisions rather than merely informing them.

Co-design is often confused with consultation. In consultation, an organization develops options and asks people to react. In co-design, people help decide which problem to solve, what good looks like, which ideas to pursue, and how to test them. The difference is about power. A useful test is to ask what participants could change. If the honest answer is very little, the process is consultation, which has its place, but should not be described as co-design. Being candid about that distinction protects trust.

Core principles of co-design

Practitioners describe co-design principles in different words, but the same commitments appear across health, public sector, and community design practice. Together they define the conditions under which collaboration is genuine rather than symbolic. Each principle carries practical implications for budgets, timelines, facilitation, and governance, so they should be agreed before the work begins and revisited as it progresses. They also give participants a basis for holding the organization to account if the process drifts.

  • Shared power: participants have real influence over decisions, and the limits are stated honestly.
  • Lived experience as expertise: personal experience is valued alongside professional and technical knowledge.
  • Early involvement: people help frame the problem, not only react to finished options.
  • Accessibility: methods, venues, language, and timing suit participants rather than the organization.
  • Reciprocity: people are compensated fairly and receive tangible value from taking part.
  • Transparency: participants see how their input shaped decisions, including where it did not.
  • Building capability: the process leaves participants and staff more able to collaborate in future.

Who should be at the table

Deciding who participates is one of the most consequential choices in any co-design effort. The aim is to include people whose experience is most relevant to the problem, with particular attention to those who are usually least heard: people facing the greatest barriers, those who rely most heavily on a service, and those most likely to be harmed if it fails. Frontline staff who deliver the service belong in the room as well, because they understand operational realities and will be responsible for making any new design work.

Recruitment should go beyond the usual channels. Partnering with trusted community organizations, offering multiple ways to participate, and removing practical barriers such as transport, childcare, scheduling, and digital access all widen who can take part. A small group of participants with directly relevant experience is often more valuable than a large group drawn from the people who already engage. Representation should be reviewed during the process, not only at the start, so gaps can be addressed.

The co-design process in practice

Co-design typically moves through recognizable phases: building relationships and agreeing how the group will work, exploring the current experience, framing the problem together, generating ideas, prototyping, and testing. Methods include journey mapping, storytelling, collaborative sketching, role play, and low-fidelity prototypes that anyone can critique. Facilitation matters enormously. Skilled facilitators manage power differences in the room, make sure quieter voices are heard, and translate between lived and professional expertise without privileging either.

Two practical disciplines distinguish strong co-design. First, decision rights are explicit: participants know which choices they are making, which they are advising on, and which sit elsewhere. Second, the loop is closed: after each phase, participants see what was decided and why. Without these disciplines, even well-intentioned processes can slide back into consultation, and participants come away feeling that their time was used to legitimize decisions already made. Documenting decisions and sharing them back in accessible formats is a small effort that protects the integrity of the whole process.

Common pitfalls

The most frequent failures in co-design come from organizations underestimating what it requires. Timelines are set without allowing for relationship building. Budgets omit participant compensation and accessibility costs. Leaders who will make final decisions stay out of the process and then overturn its outcomes. Participants are asked to share painful experiences without proper support. Each of these erodes trust, not only in the project but in future efforts to engage the same community or workforce. Leaders should treat these costs and commitments as part of the design budget from the outset, not as optional extras.

Another pitfall is treating co-design as a single workshop. Real collaboration happens over a series of sessions, with time between them for reflection, prototyping, and testing. Organizations should also plan for what happens after launch. Involving participants in evaluating whether the new service works keeps accountability with the people it was designed for and creates a foundation for continuous improvement rather than a one-time project. Ongoing involvement also helps the organization notice when conditions change and the design needs to adapt.

How Datalidity practices co-design

At Datalidity, co-design sits at the center of our Design phase and shapes the phases around it. We Listen first, building relationships and understanding lived experience through interviews and community listening. We Measure to establish a baseline and to identify whose experience is most different from the average. We Design with participants, using clear decision rights, fair compensation, and accessible methods. We Deliver by supporting the organization through implementation and capability building, and we Sustain by involving participants in measuring outcomes after launch. Whether the people affected are employees, patients, residents, or customers, our view is the same: solutions are better and more durable when the people who live with them help make them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between co-design and user research?

User research gathers information about people's needs and behaviors, which designers then interpret and act on. Co-design goes further by involving people as active partners in defining problems, generating ideas, and making design decisions. Research is often an input to co-design, but co-design shares decision-making power, while research alone typically leaves decisions with the organization and its designers.

Should co-design participants be paid?

Yes, in most cases. Participants contribute expertise, time, and often emotional labor, and fair compensation recognizes that contribution as valuable. Payment also widens participation by making it possible for people who cannot afford to volunteer their time. Organizations should budget for compensation alongside costs such as transport, childcare, interpretation, and accessibility support, and agree payment terms clearly before sessions begin.

When is co-design not the right approach?

Co-design may not fit when a decision is already fixed, when there is no genuine room for participants to influence outcomes, or when timelines are too short to build relationships responsibly. In those situations, honest consultation or transparent communication is better than a process labeled co-design that participants experience as tokenistic. Organizations can still plan to use co-design for later phases where real influence is possible.
