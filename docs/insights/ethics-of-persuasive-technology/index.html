The Ethics of Persuasive Technology

Every interface influences behavior. The question is not whether technology persuades, but whether it does so transparently and in people's interest. Clear ethical principles help organizations design persuasive technology they can stand behind.

Key takeaways

  • Persuasive technology is any interactive system designed to change attitudes or behaviors, a field B.J. Fogg helped define.
  • Persuasion becomes manipulation when it relies on deception, exploits vulnerabilities or works against the interests of the person influenced.
  • Dark patterns and sludge erode trust and invite regulatory scrutiny, even when they lift short-term metrics.
  • Ethical design starts with aligned goals, transparency, respect for autonomy and measures that reflect user wellbeing, not only engagement.

What persuasive technology is

Persuasive technology refers to interactive computing systems designed to change people's attitudes or behaviors. B.J. Fogg helped establish the field at Stanford, describing how computers can act as tools that make behaviors easier, as media that provide experiences, and as social actors that people respond to as they would to other people. Today persuasive technology is everywhere: fitness apps that encourage activity, financial tools that prompt saving, onboarding flows that guide new users and notifications that bring people back.

Fogg emphasized from the beginning that persuasive technology raises distinct ethical questions. Interactive systems can be persistent, personalized and available at any moment, and they can operate at a scale no human persuader could match. They can also obscure who is doing the persuading and why. These features make persuasive technology powerful for good, such as supporting health or learning, and equally powerful for harm when it is used to serve narrow interests at people's expense.

Persuasion versus manipulation

The central ethical question is where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins. Persuasion appeals to reasons, emotions or social cues in ways that a person could recognize and endorse. Manipulation bypasses or exploits a person's decision-making in ways they would reject if they understood what was happening. The distinction is not always sharp, but several markers help: whether the intent is disclosed, whether the methods involve deception, whether the influence exploits a particular vulnerability and whether the outcome serves the person influenced.

Thaler and Sunstein drew a similar line for nudges, arguing that legitimate nudges preserve freedom of choice and aim to help people by their own standards. Influence that works only because people do not notice it, or that relies on confusion, false urgency or hidden costs, fails that test. A practical check is publicity: would the organization be comfortable explaining exactly how and why the technology influences behavior to the people affected, and to a regulator?

  • Intent: is the goal of influence disclosed or concealed?
  • Methods: does it rely on accurate information or deception?
  • Vulnerability: does it exploit fatigue, distress or limited understanding?
  • Outcome: does it serve the person, or only the organization?

Dark patterns and sludge

Dark patterns are interface designs that steer people toward choices they might not make with full understanding. Common examples include subscriptions that are easy to start and hard to cancel, pre-checked boxes that add unwanted services, confusing wording that makes declining feel like a mistake, fake scarcity or countdown timers, and hidden fees revealed only at the last step. Sludge is a related term for unnecessary friction that makes beneficial actions harder, such as complicated refund processes or burdensome paperwork.

These practices may improve short-term metrics, but they carry real costs. They damage trust, generate complaints, increase churn once customers realize what happened, and attract regulatory attention, as consumer protection authorities in several jurisdictions have increasingly focused on deceptive design. Most importantly, they harm people. In health, finance and education, manipulative design can have serious consequences for wellbeing. Organizations that rely on these patterns are building on a fragile foundation.

  • Hard-to-cancel subscriptions and hidden renewal terms.
  • Pre-selected add-ons that people did not request.
  • False urgency, scarcity claims or countdown timers.
  • Confusing language that makes declining feel risky.
  • Unnecessary friction that blocks beneficial actions.

Principles for ethical persuasive design

Ethical persuasive technology starts with aligned goals. The behavior being encouraged should be one the person already values or would endorse on reflection, such as maintaining their health, saving money, learning a skill or completing a task they started. Transparency follows: people should be able to understand what the system is trying to do and why. Autonomy matters too. People should be able to decline, opt out, change settings and leave without penalty or excessive effort.

Attention to vulnerability is also essential. Persuasive techniques that are acceptable for a typical adult may be inappropriate for children, people in distress, people with limited literacy or people facing financial hardship. Measurement shapes ethics as well. When teams are rewarded only for engagement, time on site or conversion, they will find ways to increase those numbers, sometimes at users' expense. Including measures of user benefit and satisfaction alongside business metrics keeps design incentives aligned with people's interests.

Making ethics operational

Ethical principles only matter if they shape decisions. Organizations can make them operational in several ways. Design reviews can include explicit questions about intent, methods, vulnerability and outcome. Experiments can be reviewed for ethical acceptability before launch, ensuring every version tested is one the organization would be comfortable deploying. Teams can maintain a list of prohibited patterns and examine new features against it. Assigning clear ownership for these reviews, rather than leaving ethics to individual conscience, makes it far more likely that concerns are raised before launch rather than after complaints arrive.

Research is a valuable safeguard. Usability testing and interviews reveal whether people understand what a system is doing and whether they feel respected or tricked. Monitoring complaints, cancellations and support requests can surface problems that metrics miss. Leaders play a decisive role by making clear that short-term gains achieved through manipulation are not acceptable, and by rewarding teams for outcomes that benefit both the organization and the people it serves.

  • Add ethical questions to design and experiment reviews.
  • Maintain a list of prohibited dark patterns.
  • Test comprehension and perceived fairness with real users.
  • Balance engagement metrics with measures of user benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Is all persuasive technology manipulative?

No. Persuasive technology includes many systems that help people achieve their own goals, such as apps that support exercise, saving or medication routines. What makes persuasion ethical is alignment with the person's interests, transparency about intent, accurate information and respect for autonomy. Manipulation occurs when influence relies on deception, exploits vulnerabilities or serves the organization at the person's expense.

What are dark patterns?

Dark patterns are design choices that steer people toward decisions they might not make with full understanding, often benefiting the organization at the user's expense. Examples include hidden fees, pre-checked add-ons, difficult cancellation processes, false urgency and confusing wording. They can raise short-term metrics but tend to damage trust, increase complaints and attract regulatory scrutiny.

How can teams balance business goals with ethical design?

Start by choosing goals that serve both the organization and its users, then measure both. Include indicators of user benefit, comprehension and satisfaction alongside conversion or engagement. Review designs and experiments against clear ethical principles before launch. Over time, trustworthy design tends to support retention, reputation and lifetime value, which makes ethics and performance more aligned than they first appear.
