Equity-Centered Design

Equity-centered design asks not only whether a solution works for its users, but who has power in the design process, whose needs have historically been ignored, and how a solution might reduce or reproduce inequity. It is a discipline of design and of accountability.

Key takeaways

  • Equity-centered design extends human-centered design by explicitly addressing power, history and the systemic causes of inequity.
  • It centers people most affected by a problem, especially those historically excluded, as partners and decision makers in design.
  • Designers examine their own positions and assumptions, recognizing that design choices are never neutral.
  • Practiced well, it produces solutions that work better for everyone, because designing for the margins strengthens the whole system.

What equity-centered design is

Equity-centered design is an approach to designing products, services, policies and systems that explicitly aims to reduce inequity and to avoid reproducing it. It builds on human-centered design, which places the needs of users at the center of the design process, but it adds a critical lens. It asks who defines the problem, whose voices shape the solution, who benefits and who bears the risks. It recognizes that systems often produce unequal outcomes not by accident but because of how they were designed, and that new designs can either interrupt or reinforce those patterns.

The approach has been developed by practitioners and organizations across education, public health, civic technology and community development. Groups such as Creative Reaction Lab, which articulated Equity-Centered Community Design, and the Stanford d.school's work on liberatory and equity-centered practices have shaped the field. While frameworks differ, they share commitments to examining power, honoring lived experience and building the capacity of affected communities to lead change. The approach is increasingly adopted by public agencies, foundations, healthcare systems and employers seeking to design more fairly and effectively.

How it differs from human-centered design

Human-centered design has been a powerful force for making solutions more usable and relevant. Its critics, however, note that it can treat users as research subjects rather than partners, that it often focuses on individual needs while overlooking structural causes, and that it rarely questions the power held by the designers and the institutions commissioning the work. A team can conduct excellent user research and still produce a solution that serves the majority well while failing, or harming, people at the margins.

Equity-centered design responds to these concerns in several ways. It asks designers to understand the history and context of the problem, including past harms that may shape trust. It shifts decision-making power toward the people most affected rather than simply consulting them. It examines how a solution might affect different groups differently and builds in safeguards. And it treats the design process itself as an opportunity to build capacity and leadership within communities, so that the benefits of the work extend beyond any single product or service.

Core principles

Several principles recur across equity-centered design frameworks. They begin with self-awareness: designers and sponsoring organizations examine their own identities, assumptions and positions of power, because these shape what they notice and what they ignore. They extend to the people most affected, who are engaged as co-designers and, where possible, decision makers. They require attention to history and systems, so that solutions address root causes rather than symptoms, and they emphasize accountability for outcomes over time.

These principles also have practical implications for how projects are scoped, funded and governed. They take time and resources, including compensation for community members who contribute expertise. They also require humility about what an external team can and should decide on behalf of others, and willingness to change course when communities identify concerns that the original brief did not anticipate. Leaders who commission equity-centered work should expect it to challenge some of their assumptions, and should plan for that from the outset.

  • Acknowledge and examine the designer's own power and assumptions.
  • Center people most affected, especially those historically excluded.
  • Understand history and context before defining the problem.
  • Share decision-making power, not just opportunities for feedback.
  • Assess differential impacts and design safeguards against harm.
  • Build lasting community capacity and remain accountable for outcomes.

Methods and practices

Equity-centered design uses many familiar design methods, adapted to its commitments. Research is conducted with attention to who is included and excluded, with recruitment that deliberately reaches people often missed, such as those with disabilities, limited English proficiency, unstable housing or irregular work schedules. Community members may be trained and paid as co-researchers. Journey maps and service blueprints are disaggregated to show how experiences differ across groups. Equity impact assessments examine how proposed changes would affect different populations before decisions are made.

Co-design sessions are structured to balance power: meeting in community settings, providing childcare and interpretation, compensating participants, and giving them real influence over choices. Prototypes are tested with those most likely to be harmed or excluded, not only with typical users. Measurement disaggregates outcomes by relevant characteristics so that improvements on average do not hide widening gaps. Throughout, teams document how community input shaped decisions, which is essential for maintaining trust and for demonstrating that participation was meaningful rather than symbolic.

Why it matters for organizations

Equity-centered design is sometimes seen as relevant only to public agencies and nonprofits, but its value is broader. Any organization whose customers, employees or communities are diverse risks designing systems that work well for some and poorly for others. A benefits enrollment process, a hiring system, a patient pathway or a financial product can all produce unequal outcomes if designed around an assumed typical user. These failures carry real costs, including lost customers, legal and reputational risk, and wasted talent.

Designing for those at the margins often improves outcomes for everyone, a pattern familiar from accessibility work, where features such as captions and curb cuts benefit far more people than those they were designed for. Equity-centered design also builds trust with communities and employees, which in turn improves participation, adoption and the quality of the information organizations receive. For leaders committed to fairness and to serving their full range of stakeholders, it offers a disciplined way to turn those commitments into concrete design decisions.

How Datalidity practices equity-centered design

Equity is central to how Datalidity works. Within our Listen, Measure, Design, Deliver, Sustain methodology, we listen first to the people most affected, recruiting deliberately to include those often left out and compensating community expertise. We measure outcomes disaggregated by relevant characteristics, so that gaps are visible rather than averaged away, and we use equity audits and analytics to identify where systems produce unequal results. That evidence helps leaders see which parts of a service or employee system are working for some groups and failing others, and where redesign will matter most.

We design with communities and employees as partners, sharing decision-making where possible, and we support delivery and governance that keep organizations accountable for equitable outcomes over time. Whether we are redesigning a public service, an employee experience or a community program, our aim is solutions that work better for the people who have been least well served, and therefore better for everyone who depends on them, including the organization itself.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between equity-centered design and inclusive design?

Inclusive design focuses on making products and services usable by people with the widest possible range of abilities, circumstances and characteristics, often with a strong emphasis on accessibility. Equity-centered design shares that goal but adds explicit attention to power, history and systemic inequity. It asks who has decision-making authority in the design process, how past harms shape the problem, and whether a solution reduces or reinforces existing disparities in outcomes.

How do you involve communities in equity-centered design?

Meaningful involvement starts early, at problem definition rather than at the testing stage. It includes recruiting people most affected, meeting in accessible community settings, compensating participants for their time and expertise, providing interpretation and childcare where needed, and giving community members real influence over decisions. Organizations should explain how input shaped outcomes and maintain relationships beyond a single project, so participation builds trust and lasting capacity.

Can equity-centered design be used inside companies?

Yes. Companies use equity-centered design to improve hiring processes, performance and promotion systems, benefits, workplace policies, customer services and products. Internally, it means involving employees from underrepresented groups in designing systems that affect them, disaggregating data to reveal disparities, and assessing how changes will affect different groups. It helps organizations build fairer systems, reduce legal and reputational risk, and make better use of talent.
