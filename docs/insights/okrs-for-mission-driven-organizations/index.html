OKRs for Mission-Driven Organizations

OKRs were popularized by technology companies, but their core discipline of choosing a few ambitious outcomes and measuring progress honestly suits mission-driven organizations well. Using OKRs for nonprofits and impact-led businesses requires some important adaptations.

Key takeaways

  • OKRs pair a qualitative objective with a few measurable key results that show whether the objective is being achieved.
  • Mission-driven organizations should anchor OKRs in their theory of change, linking quarterly goals to long-term impact.
  • Key results should describe outcomes for people served, not activities completed, wherever outcomes can be credibly measured.
  • OKRs work best as a learning and focus tool, separated from individual performance ratings and compensation decisions.

What OKRs are

Objectives and key results, usually shortened to OKRs, are a goal-setting framework that originated in management practice at Intel and was later popularized by Google and other technology companies. An objective is a short, qualitative statement of what an organization or team wants to achieve in a given period. Key results are a small number of measurable statements that show whether the objective has been reached. A typical set includes one to three objectives per team, each with two to five key results, reviewed regularly and reset each cycle.

The framework's value lies less in its format than in the disciplines it encourages: choosing a few priorities instead of many, making goals visible across the organization, distinguishing ambitious aspirations from firm commitments, and reviewing progress frequently enough to adjust. These disciplines address problems that mission-driven organizations know well, including too many simultaneous initiatives, strategic plans that are rarely consulted after approval, and reporting that counts activity rather than change in people's lives. OKRs offer a lightweight structure for addressing all three.

Why mission-driven organizations need adaptations

OKRs for nonprofits, foundations, social enterprises, and public agencies face challenges that commercial teams may not. The most important outcomes often unfold over years, not quarters. They depend on many actors beyond the organization's control, and they can be difficult or costly to measure. Funders frequently require reporting against their own indicators, which can pull attention in several directions. Staff are often motivated by mission and may experience metrics as reductive or bureaucratic if they are imposed without explanation.

None of these challenges makes OKRs unsuitable, but each requires thoughtful design. The answer is not to abandon measurement, nor to pretend that a quarterly key result can capture long-term social change. It is to build a clear chain of logic from the organization's mission to near-term, measurable signals that indicate the work is on track, and to be honest about which links in that chain are evidence and which are assumptions. Done well, this approach respects both the complexity of social change and the need for practical accountability.

Anchoring OKRs in a theory of change

A theory of change describes how an organization's activities are expected to lead to outputs, short-term outcomes, and long-term impact, along with the assumptions connecting each step. It is the natural foundation for OKRs in a mission-driven setting. Annual objectives can be drawn from the most important outcomes in the theory of change, and quarterly key results can track the leading indicators that suggest those outcomes are coming within reach. This connection keeps OKRs tied to mission rather than to whatever is easiest to count.

Using a theory of change this way also helps teams choose between output and outcome key results. Outcomes for people served, such as improved skills, stable housing, or better health, are preferable where they can be credibly measured within the period. Where they cannot, well-chosen outputs or leading indicators are acceptable, provided the team states the assumption linking them to the outcome and tests it over time. Over several cycles, this practice builds an evidence base that strengthens both strategy and fundraising.

Writing good mission-driven OKRs

Strong OKRs in mission-driven organizations share several characteristics. They are few in number, written in plain language, and meaningful to the people doing the work. Objectives inspire without being vague. Key results are specific enough that, at the end of the cycle, a reasonable person could agree whether they were achieved. The following guidelines help teams avoid the most common mistakes. Applying them consistently matters more than perfecting the wording of any single key result.

  • Start from the outcome you want for people served, then work back to measures.
  • Limit each team to a small number of objectives so focus is real.
  • Include at least one key result that reflects quality or equity, not only volume.
  • Involve the people closest to the work and, where possible, community members in setting them.
  • Separate aspirational goals from committed ones and label them clearly.
  • Write key results that a team can influence meaningfully within the cycle.

Running OKRs as a learning system

The cadence of OKRs matters as much as their content. Brief, regular check-ins allow teams to discuss progress, surface obstacles, and adjust tactics before a cycle ends. End-of-cycle reviews should ask not only whether key results were met but what was learned about the theory of change. A missed key result can be valuable if it reveals that an assumption was wrong. Treating OKRs as a learning system rather than a scorecard encourages honesty and keeps ambition high.

For the same reason, OKRs should be kept separate from individual performance ratings and compensation. When goals determine pay or job security, people set safer targets and report progress less candidly, which defeats the purpose. Leaders can also use OKRs to manage funder relationships, showing how restricted grants contribute to organization-wide outcomes and opening conversations about which indicators genuinely reflect impact. Shared visibility of OKRs across teams also reduces duplication and helps staff see how their work connects to colleagues elsewhere in the organization.

The goal is focus in service of mission, never metrics for their own sake.

Frequently asked questions

Do OKRs work for nonprofits?

Yes, when adapted thoughtfully. Nonprofits benefit from the focus, transparency, and regular review that OKRs encourage. The key adaptations are anchoring objectives in a theory of change, measuring outcomes for people served where possible, accepting leading indicators where long-term outcomes cannot be measured within a cycle, and treating OKRs as a learning tool rather than a performance scorecard tied to individual pay.

How are OKRs different from KPIs?

KPIs are ongoing measures of health or performance that an organization monitors continuously, such as client retention or program enrollment. OKRs are time-bound goals focused on change: what the organization wants to achieve or improve in a specific period. KPIs often inform which OKRs to set, and a key result may aim to move a KPI, but the two serve different purposes and work best together.

How many OKRs should a team have?

Most teams should have one to three objectives per cycle, each with two to five key results. Fewer is usually better, especially in mission-driven organizations where staff already balance program delivery, fundraising, and reporting. If everything becomes an objective, OKRs lose their power to focus attention. Ongoing responsibilities that are not priorities for change can be tracked separately as operating measures.
