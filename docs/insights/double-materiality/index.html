Double Materiality

Double materiality asks two questions at once: how sustainability issues affect a company's finances, and how the company affects people and the environment. Now a regulatory requirement in Europe, it is reshaping how boards view risk and impact.

Key takeaways

  • Double materiality combines financial materiality, outside-in, with impact materiality, inside-out, to determine what a company must report.
  • The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive makes double materiality the basis for sustainability reporting under European standards.
  • A credible assessment relies on evidence and engagement with affected stakeholders, not only internal judgment.
  • Used well, the assessment informs strategy, risk management and governance, not only compliance and disclosure.

What double materiality means

Materiality is the principle that determines which information is important enough to report. In financial reporting, information is material if it could reasonably influence the decisions of investors. Double materiality extends this idea to sustainability by adding a second perspective. A sustainability matter is material if it is financially material, meaning it affects or could affect the company's cash flows, performance, position, cost of capital or access to finance. It is also material if it is impact material, meaning the company's operations or value chain have actual or potential significant effects on people or the environment.

These two perspectives are often described as outside-in and inside-out. The outside-in view asks how the world affects the company, for example how climate change, labor shortages or regulation create risks and opportunities. The inside-out view asks how the company affects the world, for example through emissions, working conditions in its supply chain or the effects of its products on customers and communities. Under double materiality, a matter is reportable if it is material from either perspective, or from both.

Double materiality and the EU CSRD

The concept was articulated in European Commission guidance on non-financial reporting and has since been embedded in law through the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, known as the CSRD. The directive requires companies within its scope to report according to the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, developed by EFRAG, and makes a double materiality assessment the foundation of that reporting. Companies must determine which sustainability topics are material through both lenses, disclose their assessment process, and report on the impacts, risks and opportunities identified.

Scope, timing and some requirements of the CSRD have been subject to revision through European simplification initiatives, and companies should take current legal advice on their obligations. The underlying principle of double materiality, however, remains central to the European approach. This contrasts with frameworks focused primarily on investors, such as the standards issued by the International Sustainability Standards Board, which take a financial materiality perspective. The GRI Standards, by contrast, focus on impact materiality. Many multinational companies must navigate several of these frameworks at the same time.

How to conduct a double materiality assessment

A double materiality assessment begins by understanding the company's business model, operations and full value chain, upstream and downstream. Teams then compile a long list of potential sustainability matters, drawing on reporting standards, sector guidance, peer practice, regulatory developments and, crucially, engagement with affected stakeholders. For each matter, they assess impacts on people and the environment by considering severity, which includes scale, scope and irremediability, along with likelihood for potential impacts. Separately, they assess financial risks and opportunities by considering magnitude and likelihood over short, medium and long time horizons.

The results are combined to determine which matters are material and therefore in scope for reporting. Thresholds and methods must be documented, and the process should be reviewed and approved through appropriate governance, often including the audit committee or board. Because the CSRD requires assurance of sustainability information, the assessment must be robust, evidence-based and auditable, not a one-off workshop exercise. Companies should expect auditors to examine how stakeholders were engaged, how thresholds were set and how judgments were reached, so clear records matter as much as the conclusions themselves.

  • Map the business model and full value chain.
  • Identify potential impacts, risks and opportunities across sustainability topics.
  • Engage affected stakeholders and users of sustainability information.
  • Assess impact severity and likelihood on people and the environment.
  • Assess financial magnitude and likelihood across relevant time horizons.
  • Set thresholds, document judgments and obtain governance approval.

The role of stakeholder engagement

Impact materiality cannot be credibly assessed from inside the company alone. The people affected by a company's activities, including employees, workers in the value chain, local communities, customers and end users, often understand impacts that internal teams overlook. European standards expect companies to consider the views of affected stakeholders in their assessment, and well-designed engagement makes the result more accurate and defensible. Engagement also tends to strengthen relationships with the groups whose cooperation the company depends on, and it gives leaders an earlier view of issues that could later become legal, reputational or operational risks.

Effective engagement goes beyond surveys of senior internal stakeholders. It uses interviews, focus groups, community dialogue and representative organizations, such as trade unions or civil society groups, to understand experiences directly. It pays particular attention to vulnerable or marginalized groups, whose experiences of severe impacts may not surface through routine channels. It also draws on existing evidence, such as grievance mechanisms, employee listening programs and human rights due diligence, so the company builds on what it already knows rather than starting over each reporting cycle.

From compliance to strategy

Many companies approach double materiality as a reporting requirement, and for those in scope it is one. But the assessment also produces strategically valuable information. It maps where the company's most significant impacts lie and how sustainability issues could affect its financial resilience. That information can inform risk management, capital allocation, product strategy, supply chain decisions and workforce planning. It can reveal opportunities as well as risks, such as markets for more sustainable products or advantages from better workforce practices.

The connection between impact and financial materiality is often dynamic. An impact on workers or communities that is not financially material today can become so as regulation, litigation, consumer expectations or labor markets change. Companies that understand their impacts early can act before they become financial risks. Integrating the assessment into strategy and governance, rather than treating it as a separate reporting process, ensures that what the company discloses reflects what it actually manages.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between single and double materiality?

Single materiality, often called financial materiality, considers only how sustainability matters affect a company's financial performance and value, which is the main concern of investors. Double materiality adds impact materiality, which considers how the company's activities affect people and the environment, regardless of whether those effects are currently financially significant. Under double materiality, a topic is reportable if it is material from either perspective.

Who needs to conduct a double materiality assessment?

Companies within the scope of the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive must conduct a double materiality assessment as the basis for reporting under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards. Scope and timing have been subject to revision, so companies should confirm their obligations with legal advisors. Other organizations, including companies outside the EU with European operations and those preparing for investor or customer expectations, increasingly adopt the approach voluntarily.

How often should a double materiality assessment be updated?

Companies should review their assessment each reporting period to confirm it remains valid, and update it more fully when there are significant changes in the business, value chain, operating context or stakeholder expectations, such as acquisitions, new markets or regulatory developments. Many conduct a comprehensive assessment periodically and lighter annual refreshes, using ongoing stakeholder engagement and monitoring data to identify emerging issues between full cycles.
