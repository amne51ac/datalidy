Leading and Lagging People Indicators

Turnover, absence, and engagement scores tell leaders what has already happened. Leading and lagging indicators used together give an earlier, more actionable view of the workforce, connecting today's signals to tomorrow's outcomes.

Key takeaways

  • Lagging indicators measure outcomes after the fact; leading indicators signal conditions that predict those outcomes.
  • Most people dashboards are dominated by lagging metrics such as turnover, so leaders learn about problems too late.
  • Good leading indicators are causally plausible, measurable frequently, and within the organization's power to influence.
  • A balanced scorecard links leading people indicators to lagging people and business outcomes, and tests those links over time.

The difference between leading and lagging indicators

A lagging indicator measures an outcome that has already occurred. Revenue, profit, and customer churn are lagging indicators in business performance. In the people domain, common lagging indicators include voluntary turnover, absence, workplace injuries, time to fill vacancies, and annual engagement scores. They are essential for accountability and are usually easy to measure, but by the time they move, the conditions that caused the change have often been in place for months. Relying on them alone is like steering by the wake.

A leading indicator measures a condition or behavior that tends to precede an outcome. In safety management, near-miss reporting and completion of safety observations are classic leading indicators for injuries. In the people domain, examples include manager one-to-one frequency, workload, internal mobility, and early-tenure sentiment. Leading indicators give leaders time to act. The value of leading and lagging indicators comes from using them together, so that early signals can be checked against the outcomes they are supposed to predict.

Why people dashboards look backward

Most people dashboards are built from data that HR systems capture automatically: hires, exits, headcount, absence, and compensation. These are overwhelmingly lagging measures. They show what happened last quarter but say little about what will happen next. Leaders reviewing such dashboards can see that turnover rose in a particular team, but not that workload had been climbing and manager check-ins had been declining for months beforehand. The data that would have warned them was either not collected or not reviewed.

This backward-looking bias has consequences. It encourages reactive responses, such as counteroffers and exit surveys, rather than prevention. It makes it hard to judge whether interventions are working until long after they are launched. And it reinforces the perception that people metrics are a reporting function rather than a management tool. Adding well-chosen leading indicators shifts the conversation from explaining results to shaping them. It also helps people functions earn a stronger voice in strategic decisions, because they can speak to what is likely to happen rather than only to what has already occurred.

Examples of leading and lagging people indicators

The right indicators depend on strategy and context, but pairing them by outcome is a useful discipline. For each lagging outcome the organization cares about, ask which conditions plausibly drive it and how those conditions could be measured more frequently. The pairings below are common starting points rather than a universal set. Each organization should test which leading indicators actually predict its outcomes. Start with the outcomes that matter most to strategy, and resist adding indicators simply because the data is available.

  • Turnover (lagging) paired with manager quality, workload, and internal mobility (leading).
  • Engagement (lagging) paired with recognition frequency and pulse sentiment (leading).
  • Injuries (lagging) paired with near-miss reporting and safety observations (leading).
  • Time to productivity (lagging) paired with onboarding completion and early check-ins (leading).
  • Promotion equity (lagging) paired with access to stretch assignments and sponsorship (leading).
  • Customer satisfaction (lagging) paired with frontline staffing levels and training (leading).

Choosing good leading indicators

Not every early metric is a useful leading indicator. Strong leading indicators share several properties. They have a plausible causal relationship with the outcome, grounded in research or in the organization's own experience. They can be measured frequently enough to give timely warning. They are within the organization's power to influence, so that a change in the indicator can prompt action. And they are hard to game, since any measure that becomes a target can be manipulated if it is too easy to move without changing the underlying condition.

Validation matters. An indicator chosen because it seems logical should be tested against outcomes over time, using the organization's own data. Some will prove predictive; others will not. Analysts should also watch for differences across groups, since a leading indicator that predicts turnover well for one population may not work for another. Treat the scorecard as a hypothesis that improves with evidence, not a fixed set of measures. Over time, this testing builds confidence that the scorecard reflects reality.

Building a balanced people scorecard

A balanced scorecard connects leading people indicators to lagging people outcomes, and then to the business and mission outcomes leaders care about most. For example, investments in manager capability might be tracked through manager effectiveness scores (leading), which influence retention and engagement (lagging people outcomes), which in turn affect customer experience, quality, and financial performance. Making this chain explicit helps leaders see how people decisions contribute to results and where to intervene. Each link in the chain is an assumption worth testing.

Keep the scorecard short. A small number of well-chosen indicators reviewed regularly by leaders is more useful than a large dashboard that no one reads. Present leading indicators at the team level, where managers can act on them, and with appropriate privacy safeguards. Review the full set periodically to retire measures that no longer predict anything and to add ones that reflect new strategic priorities. Pair each indicator with an owner and an agreed response, so that a warning signal reliably leads to a conversation and, where needed, an action.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between leading and lagging indicators?

Lagging indicators measure outcomes that have already happened, such as turnover, revenue, or injuries. Leading indicators measure conditions or behaviors that tend to come before those outcomes, such as workload, manager check-ins, or near-miss reporting. Lagging indicators confirm results and support accountability. Leading indicators provide early warning and give leaders time to act. Effective measurement uses both together.

Is employee engagement a leading or lagging indicator?

It can be either, depending on the outcome. An annual engagement score is a lagging indicator of the employee experience over the past year. At the same time, engagement often acts as a leading indicator for outcomes such as turnover, customer satisfaction, and productivity. Frequent pulse measures of sentiment function more clearly as leading indicators because they provide timely signals.

How many people indicators should leaders track?

Fewer than most organizations do. A focused scorecard might include a handful of lagging outcomes and two or three leading indicators for each, reviewed regularly. More detailed data can sit beneath the scorecard for analysis. The goal is a set that leaders actually discuss and act on, rather than a comprehensive dashboard that reports everything but changes nothing.
